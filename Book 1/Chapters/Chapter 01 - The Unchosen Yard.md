---

They say the river at Greyhold was here before the kingdoms and will be here after them, and that it has never once taken a side. It runs brown between two countries that don't trust each other and carries the silt of both down to the same sea. In spring the fishermen on both banks curse it in two accents. In winter it freezes from the edges in, and for a week or two a person could walk across into Saltreach, and every year someone tries, and every year the river takes one of them back.

The school sits on the Hearthmere bank where the water bends. It was a fort before it was a school and a quarry before it was a fort, and the yard is still quarry floor under the sand, so anyone who falls in it hard enough learns the difference. Over the gate, in letters cut deep enough to hold snow, the charter of the knightly orders says that the bonded and the unbonded serve as equals. The letters are very old. Most of the students have stopped seeing them, the way you stop seeing a crack in your own ceiling.

Maren Ashdown saw them every morning. She made a point of it.

---

She fell in the yard twice before the second bell.

The first time was her own fault. The second time was Corwin Brandt's bird.

"Again," said Instructor Pell.

She got up. Sand in her collar, sand in her teeth, a hot line along one forearm where the quarry stone had come through. Brandt was back on his mark already, practice blade low, smiling the way he smiled at the tavern girls in Millbrook and at the chaplain. Behind him, on the fence post nearest the well, Kesh was settling her feathers back into place as if nothing had happened.

Nothing had, really. The falcon hadn't come near her. She had only opened her wings, all at once, at the moment Maren committed her weight to the lunge, and something under Maren's training, something older than it, had turned her head toward the movement. Half a heartbeat. Brandt had needed less.

"Eyes on the man," Pell said.

"The man brought a bird."

A few laughs from the fence. Pell didn't laugh. He was a narrow grey heron of a man who had lost two fingers on the Saltreach road before most of this year's students were born, and he did not laugh in the yard on principle.

"The man will bring a bird to every fight he has for the rest of his life," he said. "So will the man who kills you. Again."

The third time, she watched the man.

He was good. That was the trouble with Brandt; it would have been easier if he weren't. He had quick feet and a wrist like a coachman's whip, and he'd been taught by a master in Ashford before he ever came to Greyhold, and it showed in everything but his patience. He was twenty. He had never had to wait for anything in his life, and so he didn't.

She gave him the opening he wanted, a little low on her left, a little slow on the recovery. He took it. She let his blade go past her ribs close enough to feel the air move, and then she was inside his guard before he'd finished being pleased with himself. Pommel to the breastbone. Heel behind his heel. Down.

Sand in his collar now.

The fence went quiet. On her post, Kesh turned her head all the way round and looked at Maren out of one flat gold eye. Then somebody at the back snorted, and somebody else said *oh, well done* in the voice you use for a good throw at skittles, and it was over.

Pell wrote on his slate. He took his time about it. Brandt got up on his own and brushed himself down and offered his hand across the mark, which was the worst part, because he meant it.

"Ashdown takes the bout," Pell said. He tucked the chalk behind his ear. "Brandt takes first lance."

---

She had known he would say it. She had known since spring, since the morning the lists went up for the summer bouts with her name at the bottom, where the late-oath candidates always were, and Brandt's at the top, where the bonded always were. Nobody had to explain it. The gate said there was nothing to explain.

She saluted anyway. Blade to brow, as you were taught. Then she walked back to her mark and stood on it.

First lance meant riding at the front of the knights' patrol when the school went out on the river road in autumn. It meant your name read first at the muster, and the first posting when the year was done, and a good horse instead of whatever the stables had left. In a war it would mean dying first. There wasn't a war. There hadn't been one on this stretch of the river in sixty years, which was why first lance was worth having, and why it always went to someone with a beast at his shoulder.

Tobin was waiting for her at the end of the line. He was nineteen, all elbows, unbonded like her and much louder about it.

"First lance," he said, low. "For lying down."

"For the bird."

"You put him in the sand."

"And the bird would've put me in the ground." She wiped her mouth on her wrist. Blood, a little. She'd bitten the inside of her cheek on the second fall. "Pell's right."

"Pell's a coward."

"Pell's alive. Most of his year isn't."

Tobin opened his mouth and shut it again. It was the most sense she'd had out of him all summer.

Down the line the bonded students were drifting back to their beasts the way people drift back to a fire. There were eleven of them this year. Last year there had been nine. Maren counted them without meaning to, because she counted most things without meaning to: the brindled hound that belonged to the Darrow girl, the long low creature with a badger's stripe that Pell insisted was a marten and nobody believed, Brandt's falcon, a grey cat the size of a calf asleep in the only shade. Each of them with a person beside it who stood a little differently from everyone else. You could see it if you knew how to look. As if they were never quite alone in their own bodies.

Her father had stood like that.

She remembered his dog better than she remembered his face, which she was not proud of. It had been a big rough-coated thing with a jaw like a mantrap and a habit of leaning its whole weight on your legs, and it had followed him from the stable to the river and back every day of her childhood. When a poacher's bolt took it in the throat on the far bank one autumn, her father carried it home across the ferry himself. He sat with it in the barn all night.

He lasted four days after that. The healers called it grief, because they had to call it something. Maren had been fourteen. She had watched a strong man go out like a lamp with nothing wrong with him, and she had understood, sitting on the end of his bed on the fourth morning, exactly what a bond was. A door you couldn't close again. One life with two people standing in it.

She had never wanted one. She'd told herself so often enough that she mostly believed it.

---

The shadows came over the yard just before noon.

Nobody shouted. You didn't, at Greyhold. But every head along the fence went up together, and the cat in the shade was suddenly not asleep, and Kesh dropped flat on her post with her wings half open. Maren was counting before she'd decided to.

Five. Six. Seven.

Clan dragons, high up and in no kind of order, following the river south the way geese follow it, as if the water were a road someone had drawn on the land for them. The sun came through their wings the colour of a cut. They made no sound that reached the ground. That was the thing nobody told you about dragons until you'd seen them: how quiet they were, how the size of them filled the sky without disturbing it, like weather.

One banked low over the water, low enough that she saw its rider, a dark knot strapped in at the shoulders, a pale smear of a face turned toward the school. Then it levelled and followed the others over the far bank, and they were gone into the brown haze that had hung over Saltreach since dawn.

Someone let out a long whistle.

"Seven," said Tobin. "That's a war party."

"That's a funeral," said a boy behind him, one of the Weir cousins. "My uncle runs barges to the Saltreach side. Their old chief's dead. Three days ago."

"Dead how?"

"Old, I think. Just old." The boy shrugged. "My uncle says they'll be fighting over his bones by harvest. Brother against sons. They're like that over there."

"Better their bones than ours," said someone, and that got a laugh that ran all the way down the fence.

Maren didn't join in. She was looking at the haze. Her mother had crossed this morning, the way she crossed most mornings, with a load of Hearthmere wool for the Saltreach weavers. Liss Ashdown had run that ferry for thirty years and said the far bank was the same people as this one, only poorer and with worse bread. She would be on the water now, coming back. Or she would be sitting in some Saltreach kitchen, waiting to see what the smoke meant.

Burning stubble, probably. It was the end of summer. Farmers burned stubble.

Maren watched it anyway, until Pell called the yard to the noon line and she had to turn her back on it.

---

They said the oath at noon. They said it every day, bonded and unbonded together, standing in their lines facing east toward the river and the old grey stones of the inner wall, and they said it the way you say anything you learned before you understood it. Which is to say, without listening.

Maren listened. She always had. It had been her father's oath first.

*I will hold the river.*
*I will keep the road.*
*I will stand between the weak and what would harm them,*
*and I will keep the watch below,*
*and I will not ask what I keep.*

Halfway through the fourth line, she felt the beasts move.

She didn't see it. She was facing the wall, like everyone else. She felt it the way you feel a change in the wind on the back of your neck: a ripple running down the line of the bonded behind her, a stir of claws on sand. Then it was gone, and the oath was ending, and Tobin beside her was already scratching his ear.

She looked back. The beasts were where they had been. The Darrow girl's hound was lying down. Kesh was preening. The cat was asleep again, or pretending.

Only every one of them had turned its head the same way. Not toward the river. Down. Toward the sand under the yard, and whatever was under the sand.

Then the Darrow girl clicked her tongue, and the hound looked up at her, and the moment broke.

*The watch below.* Nobody knew what it meant. Maren had asked once, in her first month, and Pell had looked at her the way you look at a horse someone has just told you is lame. "Below the river," he'd said, and walked off. As if the river had anything below it but mud.

She'd asked the chaplain, who said it meant the dead. She'd asked an old knight at the muster, who said it meant the poor. Brandt, when she was new and still asked people things, had told her cheerfully that it was a joke from the old days, and that the founders had kept their wine in the cellars.

The cellars of Greyhold were locked, and had been for as long as anyone could remember. Students didn't go into them. That was simply how it was, like the oath, and the gate, and first lance.

*I will not ask what I keep.*

The bell stopped. The lines broke up and went in to eat.

---

She went up to the river wall after supper, as she did most evenings when the light was going. It was the old fort wall, broad enough to walk two abreast, and from the north corner you could see a long way up the water to the Old Ruin, a dark lump on its island where the river split. She had poled past that island a thousand times in her years on the river watch and never once set foot on it. Nobody did. There were stories about it. There were stories about everything on this river, and her mother knew all of them.

Brandt was already there.

He was sitting on the parapet with his boots hanging over the drop and Kesh on his knee, feeding her strips of something from a cloth. He looked up when Maren came round the corner and didn't look embarrassed, which she had to give him.

"I wondered if you'd come up," he said.

"It's a wall. I'm allowed on it."

"I know." He fed the falcon another strip. "You should have had first lance."

"Tell Pell."

"I did."

That stopped her. She hadn't expected it.

"He said I'd earn it by autumn." Brandt frowned down at the bird. "I will, too. I'm not saying I won't. But you should have had it today, and I wanted you to know I know that."

He had a frank, open face that had never had to hide anything. He would make a good knight, she thought. He would make a good knight and never once wonder why.

Maren leaned on the parapet beside him, not too close. Below them the water ran brown and fast and talked to itself against the stones.

"The gate says it's fair," she said.

"The gate's old."

"So's the river." She watched the far bank. The haze had thickened as the day went, and now, in the dusk, there was a dull red under it in two places, low down, like coals under ash. Not stubble. You didn't burn stubble in the dark. "Doesn't stop it drowning people."

Brandt followed her look. He was quiet for a moment.

"That's Clan land, isn't it," he said. "Over there."

"Further north. That's farms."

"Why would anyone burn farms?"

She didn't answer. She was counting the fires. Two. Then, while she watched, a third, small and far back from the water, like someone striking a light in a dark room.

Out on the river a lantern was coming across from the Saltreach bank, low and steady, the way a lantern moves on a ferry when the woman poling it has done it ten thousand times. Maren watched it until it was halfway, and then until it was close enough that she could hear the pole knock the hull, and only then did she find that she'd been gripping the stone hard enough to hurt.

She let go of the wall. Kesh watched her do it.

"Goodnight, Brandt," she said.

"Goodnight, Ashdown." Then, as she reached the stairs: "You'll be first lance next year."

"Next year," Maren said, "I'll be twenty-eight."

She went down to meet the ferry.
