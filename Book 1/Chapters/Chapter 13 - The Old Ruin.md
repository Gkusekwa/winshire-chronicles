---

There's a thing the river watch tells new recruits on their first night out, when the boat's in the middle of the stream and the lantern's shuttered and there's nothing to see in any direction but black. They tell you that the river is a road. Not like a road; a road. That everything on it is going somewhere, the water and the boats and the fish and the drowned, and that if you sit still long enough and listen, you can hear it all going by: a long, low, endless sound like breath. And they tell you that the only places on a river where nothing is going anywhere are the islands. That's why you don't land on them. An island is a place where the road stops. And whatever's stopped there, has stopped for a reason.

Maren had been told that on her first night. She'd thought it was a story to frighten recruits. She'd thought so for eight years.

She grounded the skiff on the gravel spit in the last grey of the dusk, and climbed out into water to her knees, and stood there holding the bow line, and listened, and heard the river going by on both sides of her. And behind her, under the trees, nothing at all.

---

She didn't go in that night.

She pulled the skiff up the spit as far as it would go, into the lee of a drift-log as thick as a horse, and turned it over, and made her camp under it as they'd taught her on the watch: blanket, pack, lamp shuttered, Pell's sword unbuckled and laid beside her with the hilt by her hand. She ate bread and cold bacon in the dark. She didn't light a fire.

Across the water, the fair at Harrow's End went on for hours. She could see the big fire in the top field above the village, a red smear on the black, and hear the fiddle when the wind came round. Once, very late, somebody sang. A thin old voice, by itself, so faint it was more like remembering a song than hearing one. She lay under the skiff with her cheek on her pack and listened to it, and thought she knew the tune, and couldn't think from where.

The fire went down, and the music stopped, and there was only the river.

She didn't sleep much. She lay on her back on the gravel with the overturned boat a hand's breadth above her face and counted the sounds. The water on the stones. The wind in the trees above the spit. An owl, twice, far off on the Hearthmere bank. The drift-log creaking as the river shifted it. She counted them all and put them away, as you do on watch, so that when a new one came she'd know it.

No new one came. That was what kept her awake. On the river at night there was always something: an otter, a heron, a fox on the bank, a fish jumping. Here there was nothing. The island lay behind her in the dark.

*If you hear it breathing, you lie still, and you count.*

She didn't hear anything breathing. Toward dawn she slept.

---

In the morning the island was just an island.

She woke stiff and cold under the skiff with grey light coming in under the gunwale and a wren scolding her from the drift-log, and she lay there for a moment and felt almost foolish. Birds. There were birds. She crawled out and stood up and stretched, and the sun was coming up behind the Saltreach bank, and the mist was lying on the water in long flat sheets, and the trees on the island were just trees: oak and ash and alder, old and close, with dew on them, and a robin singing somewhere in among them as if nothing in the world was wrong.

She ate. She checked the skiff. She buckled on Pell's sword, and went up the spit and in under the trees.

---

The bridge-fort took up the whole top of the island.

She'd known it was there. You could see the stump of the tower from Greyhold's wall. But she hadn't known how big it was, because from the water you only saw the tower, and from under the trees you saw everything.

It had been a fort once. A real one, square, with walls as thick as Greyhold's and corners rounded by time. The walls were mostly gone now, broken down to shoulder height and lower, grown over with ivy and moss and young trees that had rooted in the cracks and lifted the stones apart. But you could still walk the line of them, all the way round, and see where the gates had been: one on the west side, facing Hearthmere, and one on the east, facing Saltreach, each a great round arch of grey stone with its keystone still in place.

Between the two gates, straight through the middle of the fort, ran the road.

It was under the moss, but it was there. She knelt and scraped at it with her knife and found paving: big flat stones, square-cut, fitted so close together you couldn't have put a blade between them, worn in two long shallow grooves by wheels that had stopped turning before anybody's grandmother was born. It came in through the west gate and went out through the east gate, straight as a spear, and at both ends, beyond the gates, it ran down to the water and stopped, where the bridges had been.

The Oneroad. The road of the old realm. She'd seen bits of it before, on the Hearthmere side: a stretch of paving under a field near Ashford, a milestone in a hedge with letters on it nobody could read. Her father had shown her the milestone once, when she was small. He'd traced the letters with his finger and told her what some of them said. He'd had them, he said, from a Reader out of the Isvaren colleges, a little man with an iron ring on his hand, who had come down the river one summer copying stones for a book. She'd forgotten nearly all of it.

Standing in the middle of the fort with her boots on the old road, she remembered it all at once, like a smell. His finger on the cold stone. His voice.

*This one's an old A. This one's a W. That's how they wrote it, before the six.*

---

The tower stood at the north-west corner of the fort, where the walls met, and it was the only thing on the island that was still taller than a man.

It was round, and grey, and broken off at the height of three men, with ivy all over it and a young ash growing out of the top like a feather in a cap. There was a doorway at the bottom, an arch, low and deep, with darkness behind it. Under the arch, on the inside, on the stones of the curve above her head, there were letters.

She found them because the light came in sideways through the arch from the east, low and gold, and caught the edges of the cuts. She'd been about to go through into the dark. She stopped with one hand on the stone and looked up and saw them.

Six lines. Cut deep, a long time ago, in letters like the ones on her father's milestone: square and spare, with odd little hooks at the ends of the strokes. Some of the lines were almost whole. Some were half gone, eaten by water and frost and the lichen that had grown into the cuts. She stood on her toes and traced them with her fingers the way he'd traced the milestone, one letter at a time, and said them under her breath, and got some of them, and lost others, and went back and got them on the second try.

It took her the whole morning. She sat on a fallen stone under the arch with her notebook on her knee and copied what she could, a letter at a time, leaving gaps where the stone was gone.

When she'd finished, she read it through.

*Here the road of the one realm crossed the water,*
*WINSHIRE, where the old ones walked unbound,*
*and when the old ones went into the light …*
*… the last … sleeps … below the stone …*
*… let no hand wake it …*
*Keep the watch below, and do not ask.*

She read it three times.

She sat very still on the fallen stone with the notebook on her knee and the gold light coming sideways through the arch, and said the last line out loud, quietly, to nobody.

"*Keep the watch below, and do not ask.*"

*I will keep the watch below, and I will not ask what I keep.*

She'd said it every day for two years. She'd said it at the noon bell in Greyhold's yard, facing east toward the river and the old grey stones of the inner wall. Her father had said it every day of his life. Every knight of the orders had said it, for three hundred years, and none of them knew what it meant. Here it was, carved under an arch on an island in the middle of the river, in letters older than Greyhold, older than the orders, older than Hearthmere, in the old script of the one realm.

The oath was older than the knights.

She sat on the stone until the sideways light had gone off the letters.

---

She found the first fire that afternoon, at the north edge of the fort, just inside the broken wall.

It was cold. A ring of blackened stones, a heap of grey ash, a few half-burned sticks. She crouched and put her hand in the ash and felt the bottom of it, the way Ottery had taught her on the watch, and it was cold right through. Days old. A week, maybe. Around it, the moss had been trampled flat over a wide circle, and there was a broken bottle in the bracken, and a scrap of oiled canvas, and a heel-print in a patch of mud by the wall: a good boot, a heavy man.

There were two more fires further along the wall. All cold. All the same.

At the third fire, half under the bracken where someone had dropped it and not bothered to pick it up, there was a chain.

It wasn't much of one: four links, and the end of a fifth that had been cut through. But it was the cut that made her kneel and pick it up and turn it over in her hands. It wasn't broken. Chains broke at the weakest link, and the break was always ragged. This had been cut, clean, with a good chisel, by somebody who knew what they were doing and had been in a hurry.

The metal was dark and heavy, much heavier than it should have been for its size. Beast chain. She'd seen it on the jetty at Millbrook, on the duskcat's cage. She'd seen it on the watch, on the Saltreach barges that came up the river with things in the hold you didn't ask about.

On every link, neat as a smith's mark, there was a stamp. A small ring of six dots.

She didn't know it. She turned the links in her fingers and still didn't know it, and she put it in the notebook in pencil anyway, a little ring of six, because Pell would want to know and Rowan would want proof. Then she worked her knife into the last link and twisted it, and it was tougher than she'd expected, but she was patient, and in the end the cut link came away in her hand. She put it in her pocket.

*Something I can hold in my hand,* Rowan had said. *Proof.*

It was a start.

---

She found the traps on the second day.

They were in the trees on the slope below the fort's north wall, where the island ran down toward its northern tip. She found the first one by nearly stepping in it: a great iron jaw-trap, wide as a cartwheel, half buried in the leaf litter with the jaws open and the plate covered over with moss. A trap for something big. Bigger than a deer. Bigger than a boar. The chain on it was beast chain, staked into the ground with a spike as long as her arm, and on every link was the little ring of six.

There were five more. She found them all, prodding ahead with a long stick, sweating in the cold. They'd been laid in a careful line across the slope, a line you'd only lay if you knew where something walked, and wanted to be across its path.

None of them had been sprung.

She sat on a root at the top of the slope with the stick across her knees and looked down the line of open traps toward the north end of the island, where the trees grew thickest.

She hadn't been down there yet. She'd been all round the fort, and down both shores to the spit, and along the east side as far as a fallen oak that lay across the slope into the water. But she hadn't gone north of the traps. She'd been telling herself it was because she was being careful, which was true. It was also because, every time she got near the north end, something in her didn't want to.

It was the quiet.

On the rest of the island there were birds. Not many, but some: the wren on the spit, the robin in the fort, rooks going over, a woodpecker somewhere. Every time she came up the slope toward the north end, toward the thick trees past the traps, the birds stopped. Gradually, like the noise of a village fading as you walk away from it. Until there was nothing at all. No birdsong, no rustle, no wind even, somehow, though she could see the tops of the trees moving.

Just a deep, green, waiting quiet, like the inside of a held breath.

She thought about her mother's hands on her face. *If you hear it breathing, you lie still, and you count, and you don't go looking.*

She hadn't heard it breathing. She hadn't heard anything. That was the point.

She sat on the root until the light began to go, and then she went back to the fort, and the birds came back one by one as she climbed, and she didn't look behind her.

---

On the third day it rained, and she spent most of it in the tower doorway, out of the wet, with the notebook, going over the verse and eating her mother's bread, which was as soft on the third day as on the first; Hanna's keeping-words had held. She got two more words out of the fourth line, by tracing them with her finger in the grey light: *the last … great …* and then the stone was gone again. *The last great something sleeps below the stone.*

She thought about her mother's song. She thought about the drunk boy on the river wall who'd sung it, once, in her first year, badly, until somebody threw a boot at him.

*And the last great beast lay down below.*

She thought: it's a song. Everyone knows that song. It's a lullaby. That's all.

She wrote *great* in the gap in pencil, and looked at it, and didn't rub it out.

---

On the fourth day, at noon, the smoke went up.

She was on the spit, washing her shirt in the river and keeping one eye on the Saltreach bank the way she always did, because it was the habit of eight years, when she saw it. North-east, away inland, beyond the fields above Harrow's End where the hills rose toward the Scarfell. A single grey column, thin at first, going straight up in the windless noon.

She stood up with the wet shirt in her hands and watched it.

No farm fire: too far up, too far inland; there were no farms up there, only moor and the beck coming down from the hills. Nor stubble, nor a charcoal burner. It went up thin and straight, and then it thickened, and then, as she watched, a second column went up beside it, and then a third, darker, spreading at the top in the way smoke spreads when what's burning is more than wood.

Somewhere up there, on the Saltreach side, in the hills where the Drover's Way climbed to the Clan's hold, something had gone wrong.

She thought of Rowan in her bare room at the top of the north tower, writing in her neat slanting hand. *Something will break across the river before the moon is new. When it does, I'd like to know what it was.*

She thought of Weir's boy on the river wall. *The young one walked out between them and made them do it together.*

She stood on the spit with the river going by on both sides of her and the wet shirt dripping in her hands and watched the smoke go up over the hills, three columns, then four, until it was one great grey smear on the noon sky. Faint, very faint, so faint she thought at first she'd imagined it, a sound came down across the fields and the water from that direction. A horn. A long, low, rising note, held, and then cut off. Another answered it, from somewhere else.

Behind her, on the island, every bird went quiet at once.

She didn't turn round. She stood very still, with her back to the trees, and the wet shirt in her hands, and counted. She got to forty before the robin in the fort started singing again.

Whatever had happened across the river, and whatever was at the north end of the island, she knew, standing on the spit at noon on the fourth day with the smoke going up over the Scarfell, that both of them had just started.

She wrung out the shirt, and went up to the fort, and wrote it down.
