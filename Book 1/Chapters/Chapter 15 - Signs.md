---

The horns went on all night.

She'd heard the first ones at noon on the spit, when the smoke went up. She heard the rest from the fort, after dark, sitting in the lee of the west gate with her back against the stone and her blanket round her and Pell's sword across her knees. Long, low, rising notes from away across the river, inland, up in the hills. One, and then an answer. A long silence. Another, nearer, from somewhere above Harrow's End. Then two at once, from different directions, overlapping, so that the sound went into a kind of wail and broke off.

Once there was a light on the hills, low, flickering. A fire. It burned for an hour and then went out.

She didn't know what had happened. She knew what it sounded like. On the river watch she'd heard horns like that once before, eight years ago, the winter the Clan had fought a sellsword company in the hills above Cotter's Cross over grazing. That had lasted a week. It had sounded like this.

She sat in the gate and listened and counted the horns. Fourteen by midnight. Twenty-two by the time the sky began to go grey.

Nobody on the Hearthmere bank did anything. She'd watched for it. She'd watched Greyhold's wall, far downstream, a low line of lights, and seen nothing change. No boats put out. No riders on the River Road. Greyhold slept, or didn't sleep, and kept to its bank, the way it always had.

*They never cross,* they said on the Saltreach bank. She'd heard it from Greta in the smokehouse, and from her mother, and in other words from a child at a gate in Ferriby. *They never do.*

---

In the morning she went north.

She hadn't meant to. She'd meant to go round the island again, the way she had every day: down the east shore, round the spit, up the west shore, checking. Looking for hunters. Looking for anything. She went down the east shore, and it was all as it had been, and she came round the spit, and the skiff was there under its log where she'd left it, and she started up the west shore.

Halfway up the west shore, in a patch of soft black mud where a little stream came down out of the trees into the river, she found the track.

She stopped so suddenly that her boot slid in the mud and she nearly sat down.

It was a print. One print, pressed deep into the mud at the edge of the stream, so deep the water had come up into it and stood there, dark and still, like a little pond. It was as broad as a round shield. Four toes, splayed, each one as big as her two fists together. At the front of each toe, a long, deep, curved gash, where something had come out of the foot and gone into the mud: a claw. Four claws. Each of the gashes was as long as her hand.

She knelt beside it. She didn't touch it. She looked at it the way Ottery had taught her: the depth, the edges, the way the mud had squeezed up between the toes, the water in the bottom.

It was fresh. Last night. Maybe this morning.

A track, Ottery used to say, is a letter from someone who's gone. It tells you everything about them except where they are now, and it's the only kind of letter where the one who wrote it might still be standing behind you, reading over your shoulder.

Something had come down to drink at this stream in the dark, while she sat in the west gate and listened to horns. Something that weighed more than a horse. More than a dray-beast. More than two dray-beasts. Something that walked on four great soft feet with claws that came out like a cat's, and went down to drink, and went back.

She looked up at the trees.

There were more prints. Going up the stream, into the wood, north. She could see them, now she knew how to look: a line of deep, round, splayed dents in the leaf mould along the stream bank, a long stride apart. A very long stride. Longer than she was tall.

---

She followed them.

She told herself she was being careful, and she was. She went with Pell's sword drawn and held low. She stopped every ten paces and listened. She marked the way back, notching the trees with her knife. And she counted: her steps, her breaths, the birds.

The birds went quiet after two hundred paces.

She kept going.

There were older tracks, now, mixed with the new. Some in mud that had dried hard in the summer and never softened again, so that they stood there like casts, with leaves blown into them. Some so old they'd half filled with moss. There was a path, worn rather than made, like a deer path but wide, wide enough for a cart, beaten into the earth by something that had walked it, back and forth, for longer than the moss had been growing in its prints. It went north. Toward the end of the island where the trees grew thickest and the quiet was deepest and she had never been.

At the edge of the thick trees she stopped.

She could see, a little way ahead, where the path went in under the oaks: a darkness, a kind of green cave where the branches came down and met overhead and the light didn't go. Nothing moved in it. No sound came out of it.

She stood at the edge of it with the sword in her hand, and every part of her that had been trained on the river watch and in Greyhold's yard said, very quietly and very clearly: *no.*

The quiet in there was the quiet of something listening.

*If you hear it breathing, you lie still, and you count, and you don't go looking.*

She hadn't heard it breathing. Not yet.

She went back the way she'd come, notch by notch, walking backwards for the first fifty paces with the sword up, and she didn't stop shaking until she was back at the stream and the river, and the sun was on the water, and a heron was standing in the shallows on one leg looking at her with deep contempt.

---

She found the hunters' camp in the afternoon.

It was on the north-west shore, below a little bluff where the island's edge rose steep out of the water. From the river you'd never see it. From the island you'd only find it if you were looking: a notch in the bank, a strip of gravel, and above it, tucked under the bluff where the overhang kept the rain off, a camp.

Empty. But not cold, the way the fires by the fort had been cold. This was a camp somebody meant to come back to.

There was a canvas lean-to, oiled, pegged down tight. There was a stack of firewood, cut and dry, under a tarp. There were sacks of salt meat, and a barrel of water, and a crate of something she didn't open. There was gear: coils of beast chain, heavy, dark, with the little ring of six on every link, the same as the piece in her pocket. A heap of iron stakes. Three more jaw-traps, unset, folded up like great iron flowers. Laid out in pieces along the foot of the bluff, under another tarp, the bars and frame of a cage. A big one. Bigger than any beast cage she'd ever seen. Each bar as thick as her wrist, and the frame bolted, and the bolts greased.

At the water's edge, the gravel was scored in long grooves where something heavy had been dragged up out of the river and dragged back down again. A raft. A big raft, by the width of the grooves. They'd come across from the Saltreach side on a raft, and brought all this, and gone again.

She stood in the hunters' camp and looked at the cage.

She thought about the track by the stream, as broad as a shield. She thought about the jaw-traps on the slope below the fort, laid in a careful line across a path. She thought about the path itself, worn as wide as a cart.

She thought: they know what's here. They've known for a while. They've been watching it, and learning where it walks, and laying traps across its path, and building a cage big enough to hold it. And they've gone away to get something. More men. More chain. A bigger raft.

And they're coming back.

She did the only thing she could think of. She took the cut link out of her pocket, the one from the cold fire, and compared it to the coils in the camp, link by link, and they matched: the same dark metal, the same weight, the same little ring of six. She drew the camp in her notebook, everything, the lean-to and the stores and the cage and the grooves on the gravel. She counted the chains: eleven coils. She counted the stakes: forty. She didn't touch anything else.

She went back to the fort, and moved her camp.

---

She moved it to the tower.

The spit was too open. From the spit, anyone coming across from the Saltreach bank in the dark would see the overturned skiff and know someone was here. So she dragged the skiff off the spit and round into the reeds of the west channel, where the water was shallow and silted and nobody would bring a raft, and tied it down among the reeds where you'd have to step on it to find it. She carried her pack and her blanket and her food up through the fort to the tower, and in under the arch with the verse cut into it, and into the dark.

It was dry inside. Round, small, with the stump of a stair going up the wall on one side and stopping at nothing, and the young ash tree growing out of the top far above, so that a little grey light came down through the leaves. The floor was earth and old stones and the bones of some small animal. It smelled of moss and cold stone and, faintly, of something else, something warm, like an animal's fur in the sun. She couldn't place it. She put her blanket against the far wall, facing the arch, and laid Pell's sword beside it, and sat down.

Across the river, the horns had started again. Two, then three. Then, very far off, a sound she'd never heard before and didn't know: a long, high, tearing cry, from somewhere in the hills, that went up and up and broke into a kind of scream and stopped. Another answered it.

Dragons.

She'd heard dragons before. Everyone on the river had. Calling to each other over the Scarfell on summer evenings, a deep, slow, grinding sound like rocks in a river. This wasn't that. This was something else. This was what a dragon sounded like when it was grieving, or fighting. She couldn't tell which.

She sat in the tower in the dark with her blanket round her and listened to it, and the light went out of the gap at the top of the tower, and the stars came, and she didn't light the lamp.

---

She heard it breathing a little after midnight.

She'd been dozing. Not sleeping, exactly; dozing as you do on watch, with half of you under and half of you sitting up behind your eyes, listening. The half that was listening heard it, and woke the other half, gently, as you wake someone in a boat when there's something on the water.

It came from the north. From the north end of the island, from the green dark under the oaks where the path went in.

It was slow. That was the first thing. So slow that at first she thought it was the wind, or the river, or the blood in her own ears: a long, low, deep sound, going on and on and on, and then stopping, and then, after a long time, coming back. In. And out. Like a bellows in a forge. Like her father had said. Like something huge asleep, very far down, at the bottom of a sleep so deep you couldn't see the top of it from where it lay.

She lay very still.

*You lie still, and you count, and you don't go looking.*

She counted.

She counted the breaths against her own heartbeat, and then, because her heart was going too fast to be any use, against the slow count she'd learned on the watch for the sand-glass: one-river, two-river, three-river. She counted one breath. She counted the long silence after it, and lost track, and started again. She counted another.

Eleven breaths, she made it, in a quarter of an hour.

Eleven breaths in a quarter of an hour. A horse breathed that many times in a minute. A man, in less than a minute. Nothing she knew breathed that slowly. Nothing she knew *could*. Unless it was so big that every breath took as long to draw as a man takes to walk across a room.

She lay in the tower with her back against the wall and her hand on Pell's sword and listened to it, and counted, all night.

Some time after the horns started again across the river, very late, toward dawn, the count changed. Eleven became thirteen. Thirteen became fifteen. As if, somewhere at the bottom of that enormous sleep, something had heard the dragons screaming in the hills, and the horns, and was turning over, slowly, as a sleeper does when there's a noise in the house that might be nothing.

Then the horns stopped. After a while the breathing slowed again, and settled, and went back to eleven.

She didn't move until the grey came in through the gap in the top of the tower. She sat up, very carefully, and looked at her hands, and found they were steady.

She took out her notebook and wrote, in pencil, by the first light, under the drawing of the hunters' camp:

*Breathing. North end. Eleven to the quarter hour. Faster when the dragons called.*

Under that, after a long moment:

*Da heard this.*

She looked at it for a while, and put the notebook away and got up and went out under the arch with the verse over it, and stood in the old road in the middle of the fort with the birds starting up in the trees around her, and looked north, to where the quiet was, and didn't go.

Not yet.
