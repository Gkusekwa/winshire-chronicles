---

Pell told her once, in the first month at Greyhold, that there are only two ways to stand in front of something that can kill you. You can stand as if you're going to kill it first. Or you can stand as if you're not there at all.

---

She was still sitting on the wet grass of the west shore when the dragons screamed.

The glow over the trees had grown. It stood up past the island's far shore now, east, the whole underside of the sky orange with it, and above the oaks she could see the fire on the high slope above Harrow's End, in long running lines where the stubble had caught, laced with orange like the cracks in a coal. Three shapes had been going round in the air above it. Two big, one small. She'd watched them meet, and tear, and come apart, and come together again, with fire going out of them in streams that lit the clouds from end to end. She'd seen one fall, and another. Then the small one, coming in low, and something happening to it, and it going down too.

Then the sound came across the water.

She'd heard dragons call all her life. She'd heard them fight, three nights before, in the hills. This was neither. It came from three throats at once, from the dark of the far bank where they'd fallen, and it was high and long and broken, and it went on longer than any breath could go on, and it came across the river and into her and through her, and she found she had her hands over her ears and her face on her knees and was rocking, like a child, like her mother the winter her father died.

It went on. And on. Three voices, tangled, going up and up, until the sky over the far bank seemed to glow with it, the fire and the sound together, a single long orange scream.

Then it stopped.

All three at once, as if a door had shut on them.

She lifted her head.

The silence after it was so complete that she could hear the river going past the reeds below her, and her own heart.

And she couldn't hear the breathing.

---

She'd been hearing it all night, and four nights before that. Under everything, slow, so slow she'd stopped noticing it as you stop noticing your own heart, the long deep bellows-breath from the north end, in, and out, eleven to the quarter hour. She'd counted it so long it had gone into her. It had been there behind the horns and behind the screaming and behind the men dying in the hollow.

It wasn't there now.

She sat very still on the grass and listened, and there was nothing. Only the river.

Then, from the north, from the dark under the oaks, a sound like a tree coming down.

A crack. A long splintering tearing crack, and a crash, and the shaking in the ground she'd felt in the hollow, when the hill turned over. But not once. Again. And again. Coming nearer. Heavy, and slow, and uneven, like a lame horse. Under it, clinking, dragging, a sound she knew. Chain. Chain dragging over roots, over stones, catching and pulling free.

It was coming down the path. The worn path, the cart-wide path, the path its own feet had made coming down to drink at the stream on the west shore, for longer than the moss had been growing in its prints.

It was coming down to the water.

Toward her.

---

She stood up.

She didn't decide to. She found she was on her feet, in the starlight, on the open grass of the shore between the trees and the reeds, with Pell's sword in her hand. Her cloak was over her arm, scorched, stinking. Her legs felt like water. Every part of her that the river watch and Greyhold had ever trained was shouting at her to run, to go into the reeds, to lie down in the mud with her face in it and be not there at all.

She didn't run. She couldn't have said why. Maybe because there was nowhere to run to. No boat. No island but this one. Maybe because she'd come looking, eight days ago, and you don't get to stop looking just because you've found it.

She stood there, and the trees at the top of the shore moved, and it came out.

---

It was a lion.

She'd known. She'd known since the hollow, since the hair like a horse's mane and the foot like a shield and the warm smell of fur in the sun. Some part of her had known since the first print by the stream. She'd known, and she hadn't let herself put the word to it, because there were no lions, not in Hearthmere, not anywhere in the six kingdoms, not for a thousand years. They were a thing in songs and carvings and the banners of old houses that had died out. A thing on a coin.

It came out of the trees onto the top of the shore and stood there, against the glow over the trees, and it was a lion.

It was bigger than anything she had words for. Longer than a hay wain. Taller at the shoulder than the biggest dray-beast in Greyhold's stables, nearly twice a man's height, so that its head, held low, was still above hers, and she'd have had to stand on a cart to touch its shoulder, and broad, and heavy, and hung with a mane so great that it seemed to have a second, darker animal crouched round its head and neck and chest. The mane was grey. Not the grey-gold of its flanks: grey, iron grey, gone almost white at the roots, like an old man's beard. Old leaves were caught in it. Moss. A long tangle of something like ivy, trailing. It had been lying a long time.

Its face was long and heavy and broad between the eyes, and scarred, and it held its head low, like a bull. Its eyes were pale. Almost colourless. Like river ice in the morning, with the light coming through it.

From its near forefoot, chain trailed. Two loops of it, still round the great foot just behind the toes, where the men in the hollow had laid it. One end hung loose. The other ran back along the ground behind it into the trees, taut, and at the end of it, where the men had driven the stake, the stake was still on it, iron, as long as her arm, dragging, digging a long furrow in the earth.

The lion didn't look at her.

It looked back, east, over the trees of its own island, at the glow above the far bank, where the three dragons had come down and screamed and stopped. It stood at the top of the shore with its head low and its pale eyes on the glow, and she saw its sides go in, in a breath so deep and long that she could hear the air going into it.

Then it lifted its head, and opened its mouth, and roared.

---

She'd thought the dragons screaming was the loudest sound in the world.

It wasn't. This was. It came out of the lion and went into her like a wall falling on her. She felt it in her teeth and her eyes and her stomach. She felt it in the ground. It shook the trees behind her so that leaves came down. It went out across the river in a long rolling wave and she saw the water shiver under it, the reeds bend, the starlight on the surface break up into pieces. It went on and on, rising, deep and then deeper and then, impossibly, louder, until it wasn't a sound any more, just a kind of weight, the whole weight of the night pressing down on her.

She didn't run.

She stood in it. She stood on the grass in the starlight with her boots in the earth and her sword in her hand and the roar going through her, and she didn't put her hands over her ears and she didn't go down on her knees, though her knees wanted to go so badly they shook. She stood. Something in her said: *if you go down now, you'll never get up.*

Under her boots, the shore moved, a little. A shiver, a settling, as a riverbank settles in spring when the water goes down, but she saw it. Pebbles at the water's edge hopped and rattled like dried peas in a pan. Dust lifted off the tumbled stones of the old bridge in a fine grey breath. And a crack ran across the flat top of the nearest block, from one side to the other, slow and clean, as if someone had drawn it with a nail.

The roar went out over the island and the river, over the fire, into the hills. From the hills, long after, faint, it came back.

Then it stopped. The lion lowered its head.

And turned it, slowly, and looked at her.

---

It knew she was there. She saw that. It had known all along; it had smelled her the moment it came out of the trees, the way it had smelled the men in the hollow. It just hadn't cared. Not then.

It cared now.

She saw the nostrils move. Wide, dark, wet, each one big enough to put her fist into. Breathing her in. She saw the lips go back from the teeth, wrinkling, like a dog's, and the teeth were yellow and long and there were so many of them. She saw the pale eyes change. They didn't narrow. They went flat. Like ice going hard over water.

And she knew what it smelled.

The cloak. The cloak over her arm, scorched and black from the fire in the hollow. Stinking of honey smoke. Of the pots. Of the stuff the men had burned to keep it under, night after night, while they crept up on it with chains.

It smelled the men who'd chained it. On her.

She thought: *oh.*

The lion came down the shore toward her.

---

It came slowly. That was the worst of it. Everything about it was slow. It came the way the foot had closed on the young man in the hollow, lazily, almost gently, with its head low and its shoulders rolling and its great paws coming down one after another on the grass without a sound. Only the chain made a sound. The chain dragged and clinked, and the stake at the end of it furrowed the earth behind.

She had the sword up. She didn't remember lifting it. Pell's sword, his own, the one he'd given her at the armoury door with his ruined hand and said *bring it back.* Up, in both hands, the point toward the lion's face, the way he'd taught her, the way she'd stood a thousand times in Greyhold's yard against Haverly and the others and the straw men.

*You can stand as if you're going to kill it first.*

She couldn't kill it. She knew that, quite calmly. Nothing could kill this with a sword. Not with ten swords. It would be like trying to kill the river with a stick.

It came on. Twenty paces. Fifteen. Ten.

At ten paces the chain caught.

It had been dragging behind, through the grass and down the slope, and at the bottom of the shore, where the grass gave out and the old stones of the bridge came down to the water, the stones of the landward end of the broken arch, tumbled and heaped, the stake at the end of the chain went in between two of them and jammed.

The chain went taut. The lion's forefoot was pulled back. It stopped, with a jerk, its weight thrown forward onto the other foot, its great head swinging down and toward her. And it was close. So close. Its head was a sword's length from her. Less. She could feel its breath. Hot, and enormous, and smelling of old meat and leaves and something like cold stone.

Its head was low. Its throat was open, right there, under the great jaw, under the grey mane, where the fur was short and pale and the big vein would be. Right there. A sword's length.

She could see it. She could see exactly where to put the point. Pell had shown her, on the straw men, on the pig at the midwinter kill. *There.* Under the jaw. Up. In. With all her weight behind it.

She stood with Pell's sword in her hands and the point a hand's breadth from the lion's throat, and its pale eyes looking at her over it, flat as ice.

*You can stand as if you're going to kill it first. Or you can stand as if you're not there at all.*

There was a third way. Pell hadn't said it. Maybe he didn't know it. Maybe nobody did.

She lowered the sword.

---

She didn't drop it. She brought the point down, slowly, slowly, so it was pointing at the ground between them. She held it there. She didn't step back. She didn't step forward. She stood with her boots in the earth and her sword pointed down and looked at the lion, and the lion looked at her.

She didn't know why she did it. Maybe it was the chain. The chain on its foot, with the ring of six on every link, that she'd seen men lay over it while it slept, and drive stakes through, and drag a cage up a bluff for. Or that she'd stamped out the fire in the hollow, and having done that, she couldn't now do this. Or the breathing, four nights of it, in, and out, that she'd lain and counted in the dark until it was part of her. Or the verse over the arch. *Let no hand wake it.*

Maybe it was just that it was the last of something. She could see it. The grey in the mane, the scars, the age of it. Whatever it was, there weren't any more. And she was not going to be the one who put a sword in the last of something, on a riverbank, in the dark, because it had frightened her.

She lowered the sword, and stood there, and waited.

The lion looked at her for a long moment. She saw something move in the pale eyes, and couldn't tell what it was.

Then it struck her.

---

It was so fast. After all that slowness, it was so fast she never saw it. The free forefoot, the one without the chain, came up off the grass and round, sideways, in a single sweep, and hit her.

She felt the claws. Not all the way out. She'd think about that later, much later, lying on the cold stone. Half out. Three of them. They went across her left shoulder and down her arm, and she felt them go in, and drag, and come out, and it was like being hit by a cart, like being hit by a door slammed by a giant, and the world went sideways, and she was in the air.

She hit the water.

It was black and so cold it stopped her heart. It went over her head and into her mouth and her nose and the cuts on her arm, and the current took her at once, the hard cold current of the west channel where it ran out from among the reeds past the old bridge, and rolled her over, and pulled her down. She didn't know which way was up, or where the sword was. Nothing but cold and black and the arm on fire and the river.

*The river doesn't take sides.*

Something hit her in the back. Hard. Stone. She grabbed at it without thinking and her fingers went into a crack and held, and the current pulled at her and dragged her round, and she held, and her head came up out of the water and she breathed in, half air and half river, and choked, and held.

It was the pier. The stump of the first pier of the old bridge, standing out of the water in the west channel a few lengths from the shore, where the broken arch came down. Old stone. Square-cut, slimy, with weed on it. She'd looked at it every day from the shore.

She held on to it. She couldn't have said how. She got her good arm over the top of a block of stone and her knee onto a ledge below the water and pulled, and pulled, with the river dragging at her legs and the left arm hanging, and she came up out of the water onto the top of the pier, onto the flat broken top of it, and lay there on her face on the cold wet stone.

She lay there.

---

When she could lift her head, the lion was still on the shore.

It hadn't followed her. It stood at the bottom of the bank, at the water's edge, among the tumbled stones of the landward end of the bridge, with its great head down, looking at the water. Looking for her. Or not. She couldn't tell. Its pale eyes went over the river and came to the pier, and stopped there, and she lay very still on the top of the pier in the dark and didn't breathe.

It looked at her.

It turned its head away, and looked down at its foot.

The stake was still jammed between the stones. The chain ran from it, taut, to the great forefoot. The lion looked at it, as a dog looks at a thorn in its paw: puzzled, almost, and patient. It put its head down and smelled the chain. It took a link in its teeth and pulled, gently, and the chain didn't give.

Then it set its weight back, all of it, and pulled the foot away.

She heard the iron go. A shriek, metal on stone, and the stake came out from between the blocks in a spray of grit, and the chain, which had been looped round the foot just behind the toes, slid, and caught, and tore. She heard that too. She heard the lion make a sound, a short, deep, coughing grunt, like a man who has been hit in the stomach. There was dark on the stones where it had been standing. Dark, and spreading, shining wet in the glow over the trees.

The chain was still round the foot. One loop. It had torn the foot open going over, but it hadn't come off. The stake hung at the end of it, free now, swinging.

The lion stood there on three legs, with the hurt foot held off the ground, and breathed, deep and slow, the way she'd heard it breathe for four nights. Eleven. Twelve.

It turned, and went away along the shore, south, toward the spit. Slowly. On three legs, and then four, limping, the chain dragging and the stake clanking on the stones, leaving a dark trail on the grass. She watched it go. She watched it all the way along the shore until it went into the trees below the fort and she couldn't see it any more, and could only hear the chain, fainter and fainter, and then not that.

---

She lay on the pier and the night went on.

She didn't sleep. She was too cold to sleep, and her arm was too bad. She got her belt off, after a while, one-handed, with her teeth, and got it round the top of her arm above the cuts and pulled it tight, as they taught on the river watch, and the bleeding slowed. She thought of the staunching-word the surgeon said over cut knees, and didn't bother. She had no licence for it, and out here, with the river going by on both sides, it would only have gone downriver. She lay with her cheek on the wet stone and looked at the glow over the trees, which was going down now, red and then dull and then only a smudge on the sky, and thought about nothing.

Some time toward dawn, she thought: *the sword.*

Pell's sword was in the river. She'd had it in her hand when the lion struck her and she didn't have it now. It was on the bottom of the west channel somewhere, in the black and the cold, in the silt, among the old stones of the bridge, where nobody would ever find it again.

*Bring it back,* he'd said.

She lay on the stone and cried, then, for the first time. Not for her arm, or the boat, or the trial, or the men in the hollow. For the sword. She knew it was stupid. She did it anyway, quietly, with her face on the cold stone and the river going past below.

When she'd finished, she lay still and thought about the claws.

Half out. Three of them, not four. Across the shoulder and down the arm, a sweep, sideways, as you'd brush a fly off a table. She'd seen what one of those feet did to a man in the hollow. She'd seen the young one opened from the shoulder to the hip, and the other one crushed like a sack of grain, and that was the lion asleep. That was the lion not even trying.

It had been awake on the shore. It had been angry. It had smelled the men who chained it on her cloak. And it had hit her. Once. Sideways. With its claws half out.

She was alive.

She lay on the stone and worked at it in her mind, like a knot in wet rope, and couldn't make it come out any way that made sense. Either it had meant to kill her and missed, which she didn't believe. Or it hadn't meant to.

The light came up grey over the Saltreach bank. The birds started in the trees on the island, one, then another. Across the river, the smoke from Harrow's End went up in a long, thin, crooked column into the morning, and somewhere in the hills, very far off, a horn blew. Just one. Nobody answered it.

She lay on the top of the first pier of the old bridge, in the middle of the river, between the two banks, belonging to neither, with her arm bound tight and her teeth chattering, and watched the sun come up.

Somewhere on the island, along the shore, under the trees below the fort, the lion was lying wounded in its own blood with a chain on its foot, and she was the only person in the world who knew it was there.
