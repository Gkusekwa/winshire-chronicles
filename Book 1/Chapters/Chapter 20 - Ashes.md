---

The Clan says a bonded pair has one life and two voices.

Nobody had ever said what you'd call it if there were four.

---

*Are you dead?*

He was lying on his back. There was something cold and wet under him, and something warm and heavy against his legs, and the sky above him was grey and enormous and had a single star left in it, low over the hill.

*You're not dead. Are you? You don't feel dead. I've felt dead. Dead feels like nothing. You feel like a lot of things. You feel like a rib. Did you know you've got a broken rib? Kharrak. Kharrak, is he dead?*

*Not dead.*

*How do you know?*

*Breathing. Shut up.*

*I'll shut up when he's awake. He's not awake. Is he awake? I think he's awake. His eyes are open. Look, they're open. Hello. Hello? Can you hear me? You can, can't you. I can feel you hearing me. That's strange. That's very strange. Is it strange for you? It's strange for me.*

He shut his eyes again.

*Oh, don't do that. That's rude.*

*Lisska.* A third voice. Deep, slow, so deep and slow it was like the ground speaking, like the bottom of the river. *Let him be. He has been where we were, and further, and he has come back up through all of it, which none of us could have done, and it was not done gently. Give him a moment to find which parts of himself are his own.*

*I'm only asking.*

*I know, child. You are always only asking.*

He lay there with his eyes shut and the voices going on, and something in his chest, low down, on the left, was warm. Not the stake. The stake was there, in his side, every time he breathed. This was different. This was over his heart. Like a hand laid there, flat, warm, and then another hand over it, and another.

He opened his eyes.

"Who," he said. It came out a croak. He tried again. "Who's that."

There was a silence. In his head. He'd never known there could be a silence in his head that was so full of something.

*He talks,* said Lisska. *Out loud. With his mouth. Do you always do that? You don't have to. We can hear you anyway. Or can we? Can we hear him if he doesn't talk? Try not talking. Think something at me. Go on.*

*Lisska.*

*I'm only asking!*

---

He sat up.

It was slow work. The stake in his side went in and out and in, and his head swam, and he had to stop halfway, propped on one elbow, and breathe. But he did it. He sat up in the stubble, and looked round.

The field was black. The top half of it, where the stooks had been, was burned, the stubble gone to ash and the stooks to grey heaps that still smoked a little, thin and white, in the cold dawn air. Down the middle of it ran a furrow as wide as a lane, ploughed through the earth to the subsoil, red and raw, and at the bottom of the furrow, beside him, lay Kharrak.

The rust-red dragon was lying on his belly now, not his side, with his torn wing folded badly against him and his long neck stretched out along the ground, and his head was resting beside Calder's knee. The gold eye was open. Looking at him. The grey that had been spreading across his scales last night in the firelight had gone. He was rust-red again, dull, scorched, scarred, but red.

Above him, a little way up the slope, lay Aurveth. She was lying like a cat, with her forelegs tucked in under her chest and her great head up on her neck, high, higher than the roof of the house, looking down at him. One eye. The other side of her face was a dark crusted ruin, and he couldn't look at it. Her back was torn open in long black lines from the shoulders down. She was so still she might have been carved out of the hill.

Against his legs, the warm heavy weight: Lisska. Curled round his feet like a dog round a hearth, the smallest of them, green, with her long thin head lying on his shins and her pale green eyes open, looking up at him.

*There,* she said. *See. Awake. I told you. Hello.*

He looked at her.

"Hello," he said.

*He's so polite. Is he always this polite? Kharrak, you're never this polite. You could learn something.*

*Shut up.*

---

They were all looking at him. He could feel it. He could feel all three of them, as you feel the sun on your back without looking at it. He could feel Kharrak's wing, the torn root of it, as a deep sick ache in his own shoulder, so that he put his hand up to it without meaning to. He could feel the long lines of fire down Aurveth's back, and the dark hole where her eye had been, cold and wrong, so that he wanted to cover his own eye with his hand. He could feel Lisska's bruises, small and many and sharp, and under them something else, something that wasn't a bruise.

He could feel what they were feeling.

Under the voices, under Lisska's chatter and Kharrak's growl and Aurveth's slow deep calm, there was something else. A great dark tide of it, going in and out like the river, in all three of them at once. He knew what it was. He'd felt it on the cliff path at Varrhold, and at the ford, and at Teo's pyre, and in the yard of his own house the winter Senna died.

Grief. Three griefs. Each one the size of a life.

Under Kharrak's, something else: red, hot, swelling, a rage so big he could feel it pressing at the back of his own eyes like a headache. *Who. Who did it. Who. Find them. Burn them. Who.*

He put his hand over the warm place on his chest. Three hands.

"You're in my head," he said.

*Yes,* said Aurveth.

"All of you."

*Yes.*

"How?"

The silence again. Full.

*That,* said Aurveth at last, very slowly, *is a question I have been asking myself since the moment it happened, and I am more than three hundred years old, and I have no answer to it. I have been bonded three times before. I have felt a bond take, and I have felt two of them break, and now a third, and I have lain on the shore of Coldtarn for a year waiting to wither and not withered, which no one could explain either. But I have never heard of this. Not in the Clan. Not in any song. No one has ever held more than one. And you are holding three.*

*Is that bad?* said Lisska. *That sounds bad. Is it bad?*

*I don't know, child.*

*You always know.*

*Not this time.*

---

He looked down at himself.

His shirt was torn and black and stiff with blood, his own and other things. He pulled it open at the neck with his fingers, which were shaking, and looked.

There were marks on his chest.

Three of them, over his heart, a little to the left, overlapping at the edges like coins dropped on a table. They looked like burns at first, and then they didn't. They looked like scales. Like a patch of a dragon's hide that had grown into his skin in the night. One was broad and smooth and the colour of old bronze, as big as his palm. One was smaller, jagged at the edges, like a broken plate, rust-red. One was small and narrow and green, bright green, beetle green, tucked in under the other two.

He touched them. They were warm. Warmer than the rest of him. Hard, and smooth, and he could feel his own heart beating under them.

*Oh,* said Lisska. *Look. That's me. The green one's me. Isn't it? Is it? It's small. Why is mine small? Kharrak's isn't small. Aurveth's is huge.*

*It's the size of you, child.*

*I'm not small.*

*Small,* said Kharrak.

*I'm the fastest.*

*Small.*

He sat in the stubble with his shirt open and his fingers on the three marks and listened to two dragons argue in his head about which of them was smaller, and found that he was laughing. Or something like laughing. It hurt the stake in his side so badly that he had to stop, and bend over, and hold himself, and when he straightened up there were tears on his face again, and he didn't know if they were from laughing or from the other thing.

*He's crying,* said Lisska, in a different voice. Very small. *Why is he crying? Did I do that? I didn't mean to.*

"No," said Calder. Out loud. To nobody. To the field. "No. You didn't. It's all right, little one. It's all right."

*It isn't,* said Lisska. *Is it?*

He didn't answer. He didn't need to. She felt it.

---

That was when he heard the voices at the hedge.

He looked down the field. The hedge at the bottom, where the crossbow had been in the dark, was black and broken where Kharrak's slide had gone through it, and beyond it was the lane down to the village, and the lane was full of people.

The whole of Harrow's End. Or it looked like it. They stood in the lane and at the gaps in the hedge in the grey light, in their nightclothes and coats, with their faces smudged with smoke, and some of them had pitchforks, and some had axes, and old Garrow had a scythe. Nobody had come into the field. They stood at the hedge, crowded together, as sheep crowd at a gate when there's a dog in the field, and looked at him.

At him sitting in the stubble with three dragons round him, talking to the air.

He saw Hob. Hob was at the front, in his apron, black with soot from head to foot, his face streaked where he'd wiped it. Behind him, at the bottom of the valley, the mill was a heap of black timbers with smoke still going up from it. Hob was looking at the dragons. Now he was looking at Calder. His face was very strange.

Calder lifted his hand.

Nobody waved back. A woman at the back of the crowd made a sign against evil, the old one, two fingers across the eyes. Somebody else did it too.

*Why are they standing there?* said Lisska. *Why don't they come up? Are they frightened? They look frightened. Is it us? It's us, isn't it.*

*It's us,* said Kharrak.

*I don't like it.*

*Get used to it.*

---

Wren came through the hedge.

He saw her come. A small figure in a coat over a nightgown, wriggling through a gap at the bottom of the hedge where Kharrak had broken it, and standing up on the field side, in the stubble, and looking up the slope. Behind her, in the lane, he saw Ada push through the crowd and grab for her and miss. He heard Ada shout her name.

Wren didn't stop.

She came up the field toward him, walking, carefully, in her boots, through the black ash and the burned stubble, past the grey smoking stooks, up the long raw furrow Kharrak had made. She didn't look at the dragons. She looked at him.

The crowd at the hedge had gone silent. Ada had stopped shouting. Everybody was watching her come.

*It's her,* said Lisska. He felt her lift her head off his shins. Felt her go still, and bright, all at once, like a cat that's heard a mouse. *It's her. Isn't it? The small one. From the burned farm, with the cups of water, who looked up at me and wasn't frightened. The one who said hello. You told me. At the hold. Wren says hello.* Her voice went very quick. *I remember. That's Wren. Isn't it? That's your Wren.*

"Yes," he said. "That's Wren."

*At the hold, on the ledge, when you said it, Imra told you I said hello back. I did. I did say it. Nobody had ever sent me a hello before.* A pause, very small. *Nobody has. Except her.*

Wren came up the last of the slope and stopped. She was a few paces off. Kharrak's head was between her and Calder, lying on the ground, with the great gold eye open, looking at her. She looked at it. It looked at her.

Calder felt Kharrak's rage, the great red swelling tide of it, go still, like a dog that's been growling and stops to listen.

*Small,* said Kharrak.

"Da," said Wren. "You're bleeding."

"I know, chick."

"Your face is all blood."

"It's mostly not mine."

She looked at Kharrak's head again. It was longer than she was tall. The jaw was scarred and scorched, and the teeth showed at the edge of it, long and yellow, and the nostrils were wider than her two hands, and a long slow breath came out of them, smoke and iron and hot stones, and stirred her hair.

"Is that Kharrak?" she said.

"Yes."

"He's hurt."

"Yes."

She looked at the dragon for a moment longer. She walked round its head, carefully, and came to Calder and sat down in the stubble beside him and leaned against him, on the side that didn't have the stake in it, and put her arms round him, and put her face in his torn shirt.

She was shaking. But she wasn't afraid. He could feel the difference. He'd always been able to feel the difference, with beasts.

*Oh,* said Lisska. Very softly. *Oh. I like her.*

---

Ada came up the field after a while, with her shawl held tight round her, and stopped at the edge of the furrow, and looked at the three dragons, and then at him. She didn't come any closer. Her face was grey.

"Ma," he said.

"You're alive," she said.

"Yes."

"I thought you were dead. We came out at first light, and you were lying there, and they were all round you, and nobody would go near." Her voice was very steady. Too steady. "I thought they were eating you."

*What is she saying?* said Lisska. *She smells frightened. What's she saying?*

*She thought you were eating me,* he told her.

*Eating you!* said Lisska. *We wouldn't eat you. Would we? Kharrak, would we?*

*No.*

*Why not?*

*Bonded.*

*Oh. Yes. Well, also you're very thin.*

"They're not going to hurt anybody," Calder said. He didn't know if it was true. He said it anyway. "They're hurt. They're… They lost their riders, Ma. Last night. In the fighting. All three. Sigrun, and Dag, and Imra."

Ada looked at him.

"Then they should be dead," she said. "That's what happens. Everybody knows that. The rider dies and the beast goes after. They should be dead, all three of them, lying in the field."

"I know."

"Then why aren't they?"

He didn't know how to answer. He just pulled his torn shirt open again, with his fingers, and showed her.

His mother looked at the three marks over his heart and didn't say anything. He watched her face, and saw something go across it, and couldn't read it. It was nearer the look she got when she sang the old songs, the ones about the one realm, *before the six, there was one,* and came to the last verse, and sang it too fast.

Then she said, "Your ribs are broken," in quite an ordinary voice, and came across the furrow, past Kharrak's head, without looking at it, and knelt down in the stubble and began to feel along his side with her hands.

---

They didn't take him in. They couldn't. The dragons wouldn't let him go.

That was how it felt. Kharrak lay with his head by Calder's knee, and Lisska curled round his feet, and Aurveth lay above them like a hill, and when Ada and Wat Garrow, who'd come up the field at last with his face white, tried to lift him and carry him to the house, all three heads came up at once. The feeling that went through him from all three, through the marks, was so strong and so sudden that he cried out with it: *no. No, don't. Don't take him. Don't go. Stay. Stay. Stay.*

Like children. Like children when you take the candle out of the room.

"Leave me," he said. "Leave me here. Ma, leave me. I'll be all right."

So they left him. Ada bound his ribs where he lay, with strips of her own petticoat, tight, while he swore at her through his teeth, the mild farm swearing his father had used, and Lisska asked what every word meant. Ada cleaned the cut on his head and the gash on his leg and bound those too, and said her mending-words over the leg, the ones she used for the ewes at lambing. She said they did about as much for a man as for a ewe, which was something, some years. Wat Garrow brought a blanket, and a stool for Ada, and a jug of water, and set them down at the edge of the furrow and went away again fast, with his eyes on Kharrak the whole time.

In the middle of the morning, Ada went back to the house, and came out again with a coat.

His best coat. The one he'd hung on the byre door three days ago and not been able to touch since. The byre's far end was a heap of stone and timber where Kharrak's tail had gone through it, but the door end had stood, and the door, and the coat on its peg. Bramble was down in the beck pasture with the ewes, lame and furious; Ada had found her there at first light.

"It's the only one that isn't torn," said Ada, and put it round his shoulders.

He felt the weight of the chain in the right-hand pocket, against his hip. He didn't touch it.

*What's that?* said Lisska, at once. *In there. That heavy thing. It's cold. I don't like it. What is it?*

He went very still.

*He's gone very still,* said Lisska. *Why have you gone very still? What is it? Is it a secret?*

He found, sitting there in the stubble with the coat round him and her voice in his head, that she didn't know. That she could feel he'd gone still, and feel there was something, a coldness, a weight, a thing he didn't want to look at. But not what it was. Not the man on the bucket, or the ring of six, or the seventeen words he hadn't said. Not unless he gave her them.

He let his breath out, slowly, round the stake.

"It's a chain," he said. "It's nothing. I'll tell you later."

*Promise?*

"Promise."

*All right.* She put her head back down on his shins. *I'll remember. I remember everything. Imra says…*

She stopped.

The grief came up through all three of them, then, through the marks and into him, like the river coming up over the stones of the ford in spring. He sat in the field with his mother's hand on his arm and felt it rise, and rise, and go over his head, and he held on to the coat with both hands and let it.

*Imra said,* said Lisska, after a while, very small. *Imra said I remember everything. She said it was the most annoying thing about me.*

---

At midday a rider came.

He came down the Drover's Way from the moor, a single horseman in grey, at a canter, and came over the shoulder of the hill above the farm, and saw them, and pulled up so hard his horse sat back on its haunches. He sat there on the skyline, a small dark shape against the clouds, and looked down at the black field and the three dragons lying in it and the man sitting among them.

Calder couldn't see his armband from that far. Red thread or plain grey. It didn't matter.

*Clan,* said Kharrak. The red tide came up in him so fast that Calder's vision went dark at the edges. *Clan. Theirs or ours. Doesn't matter. One of them did it. Fire him. Burn. Let me…*

*No,* said Aurveth.

*He's one of them…*

*He is a boy on a horse who will carry what he has seen back over the hill, and nothing we do will stop that now, and if you burn him there will only be one more pyre on the Scarfell and one more family to hate us. Lie still. You can't fly. And if you could, I would not let you.*

Kharrak lay still. Calder felt it cost him.

The rider sat on the skyline a moment longer. He wheeled his horse and went back over the hill at a gallop, the way he'd come, toward the moor and the hold and whoever he belonged to, and was gone.

*Now everyone will know,* said Lisska.

*Yes,* said Aurveth.

*Is that bad?*

Nobody answered her.

---

At dusk they burned their riders.

He hadn't known they would. He hadn't known it was a thing they could do. He'd been lying in the stubble under the blanket half asleep, with Wren asleep against him and Ada on her stool, when he felt Aurveth stir, in the marks, not in his body. A slow deep turning, like a great wheel beginning to move.

*It is time,* she said. *They have lain in the open long enough, and the crows have been at the hedge since noon, and I will not have that. Not for her. Not for any of them. The Clan burns its dead high, where everyone can see, so that no one can pretend afterwards that it did not happen. Well. Let everyone see.*

She got up.

It was slow. Her back was bad. He felt it, every movement, a long tearing ache down his own spine so that he gasped and Wren woke up. But she got up, onto her feet, and turned on the hillside, and went up the field, dragging her torn back, to the place at the top under the burned stooks where Sigrun Holm was lying on her face in the ash with her white hair spread out round her.

She stood over the old woman with her head down.

Then she opened her mouth.

The fire came out of her slowly, nothing like the roaring torrent of the night before: a long, slow, steady pour, pale gold, almost white, that went down over the small grey shape on the ground and wrapped it and held it. It didn't flicker. It burned as a forge burns, so hot and so still that the air above it shook, and the light of it went up the hill and down the field and out across the valley.

At the bottom of the field, by the broken hedge, Lisska was doing the same. He hadn't felt her go. She was standing over the dark shape by the hedge where Imra had fallen, with her thin green body curled round it, and the fire coming out of her was thinner and brighter and quicker, a white-blue flame, and she was making a sound as she did it, a high thin sound, going up and down, like a child singing to itself in the dark.

And Kharrak got up.

He got up off the ground beside Calder, with his torn wing dragging, and went away down the hill. On foot, slow, heavy, lurching, like a gored bull. Down through the black field and the broken hedge and across the lane, while the people in the lane scattered in front of him, screaming, and down the lane to the bottom of the valley, to the burned roofs below the mill where a man had fallen out of the sky the night before.

Calder felt him find Dag. He felt it go through Kharrak like a blade, and through him. Then he saw it: a third fire, red and roaring and furious, going up from the bottom of the valley among the black timbers of the houses, so high and so fierce that it lit the underside of the smoke.

Three fires.

He sat in the stubble with Wren in his arms and Ada beside him, and watched them burn. The size of the fire is the size of the life. He thought of Teo. *One night. I'd better make it a good one.*

They burned all night.

---

The village watched from the lanes. He could see them by the light of the fires: all of Harrow's End, standing in the lanes and at their doors and on the green, not going in, not going to bed. Watching the dragons burn their dead. Watching him.

He heard them talking, some time in the night, when the wind came up the hill. He couldn't hear the words, only the shape of the voices, low and frightened and going on and on. Later, Wat Garrow came up to the hedge with a lantern, and stood there a while, and then came up the field as far as the edge of the furrow, and crouched down by Ada's stool.

"They're saying things," he said, low, to Ada, not looking at Calder. "Down there. In the lanes. About him."

"Let them," said Ada.

"They're saying it was him called them down. The dragons. Out of the sky. That he did something. A spell, or a bargain." Wat Garrow swallowed. "And Fisk's boys were out at the Eelweir last night, when the fighting was on. And they're saying they heard it, across the water, right in the middle of it all. From the island. From the Old Ruin." He looked over his shoulder, out across the dark valley toward the river, where the island lay low and black under the stars. "They're saying something woke up out there. The same night. The same hour. Something that roared like the end of the world. And they're saying that's not a chance, Ada. They're saying that's not a chance at all."

Ada didn't answer.

Calder sat in the stubble with his hand over the three warm marks on his chest, and looked across the valley at the island, and remembered lying in the field with the roar going over him. And the three heads coming up, all at once. The voice in the dark, that might have been any of them, or none.

*Old. Something old.*

*What was it?* said Lisska, from the bottom of the field, where her fire was burning down. Her voice was very tired. *That noise. Last night. You remember it. I can feel you remembering it. What was it?*

"I don't know," he said.

*Will you find out?*

He looked at the island.

"I don't know," he said again.

*That's not an answer,* said Lisska. *Imra would say that's not an answer.*

"No," said Calder. "It isn't."

He held his daughter, and watched the three fires burn down, and the dark go on over the river.
