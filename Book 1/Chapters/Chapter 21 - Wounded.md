---

There's a word on the river watch for a beast that's been hurt and gone to ground. *Holed.* Everything that keeps a wild thing careful is for tomorrow, Ottery said, and a holed beast has run out of tomorrows. So you leave it be, and you never, ever, go and look.

---

She came off the pier at first light.

It wasn't far. Three lengths of water, maybe four, between the pier and the shore, and the west channel ran shallow there over the old fallen stones of the bridge. She'd swum further than that on the river watch with a coil of rope on her shoulder. But she couldn't use the left arm. She went in off the stone with her teeth set and the cold took her breath, and the current took her legs, and she went under once and came up choking, and kicked, and got her good hand on a stone, and another, and pulled, and stood up waist-deep in the shallows with the water pouring off her.

She stood there a while, and walked out.

On the grass she sat down and undid her belt from her arm, slowly, and looked.

Three cuts. They ran from the top of her shoulder, at the back, down over the point of it and down the outside of the upper arm almost to the elbow. Long and straight and evenly spaced, like the furrows of a little plough. Not deep at the top, where the claws had first touched. Deeper lower down, where they'd dragged. The lowest was deep enough that she could see into it, and she looked away, and looked back, because you have to look. The bleeding had mostly stopped. The edges were white and puckered from the river.

She could move her fingers. All of them. She could close her hand. She tried lifting the arm, and stopped, and sat for a while with her head down between her knees until the world came back.

She got up and went to the hunters' camp.

---

It was as they'd left it. They'd gone in a hurry, in the dark, with three men dead behind them, and they'd taken nothing off the gravel but themselves. The lean-to. The woodpile under its tarp. The sacks of salt meat. The barrel of water. The coils of chain, fewer now. The cage in pieces at the foot of the bluff.

And the crate she hadn't opened.

She opened it now. It was tools. Files, in a roll of leather. Shackle pins, a bag of them. A pot of grease. A pair of heavy pincers. A smith's hammer with a short ash handle, and beside it a cold chisel as long as her forearm, its edge bright and new-ground. Rolls of linen, clean, wrapped in oilcloth. For binding, she thought; for binding men, when the beasts they were binding bit them.

She took the linen. She washed the arm with water from the barrel, which hurt so much she had to stop more than once, and sat on the gravel and bound it, one-handed and with her teeth, round and round, tight, from the shoulder to the elbow. It took a long time. She pressed the heel of her good hand over the worst of it and counted to three hundred, the way Ottery taught, and the arm stopped seeping through the linen, or she wanted it to. When it was done she ate some of their salt meat, slowly, and drank, and sat with her back against their woodpile and her eyes shut.

When she opened them she was looking at the cold chisel.

She took it, and the hammer, and put them in her pack without thinking about it. She just found that she had them, and went on.

---

The blood trail was easy to follow.

It went south along the west shore, where she'd watched the lion go in the night, and it was dark on the grass, and broad, and every few paces there was the print of a great forefoot set down lightly and lifted quickly, with a smear in the middle of it. The chain had dragged beside it. She could see the long scrape in the turf, and sometimes, where the stake had swung and bitten in, a gouge.

It went in under the old west gate and across the paved road in the middle of the fort, and out through the south side where the wall was down, and on down the slope toward the spit.

She stopped in the broken gap of the south wall, where she could see down the slope of the island to the south tip, and stood very still.

The lion was lying on the spit.

It lay on the gravel at the very end of the island, where she'd pulled her skiff up nine days ago, at the foot of the old landing stair that came down out of the fort in a long slope of tumbled paving to the water. It was lying on its side, half on the paving and half on the gravel, with its back to the fort and its head toward the river. The hurt foot was stretched out in front of it. It had its head down over the foot, and as she watched, it licked it. A long broad stroke of the tongue, and another. It stopped and lay with its great head on the gravel, and its side went up and down.

She could see the chain. One loop, still round the foot, bitten deep into the fur. The stake lay on the gravel at the end of a short length of it.

She could see the blood. A great dark stain on the gravel all round the foot, gone brown at the edges.

Holed.

She sat down in the gap of the wall with her back against the stone, and watched it, and didn't go any closer. She sat there all morning. It didn't move, except to lick the foot, and breathe. Its breathing wasn't eleven to the quarter hour any more. It was faster. Shallow. Like a horse that's been ridden too far.

It knew she was there. Once, near midday, it lifted its head off the gravel and turned it and looked back up the slope at the gap in the wall, at her, with its pale eyes. For a long moment. It laid its head down again.

---

The raft came at midday.

She heard it before she saw it. Poles knocking. A man's voice calling a stroke. It came round the north-west point of the island, out in the current, the big square raft of lashed logs, low in the water, heavy. There were men on it. She counted them as they came closer: eight, and the Captain at the front, tall and broad in her long oiled coat.

In the middle of the raft, upright and whole, stood the cage.

They'd put it together. Bars as thick as her wrist, bolted. A door at one end, on hinges as big as a man's hand. It was a box of iron as big as a cottage, and they'd brought it round the island on the raft in broad daylight, as if they didn't care any more who saw.

*They don't,* she thought. *There's no one to see. The patrol boats aren't coming. There's a war on.*

The raft came down the west side of the island, past the reeds where her skiff had been, past the pier she'd lain on all night, and round, and in, toward the spit. Toward the lion lying on the gravel.

The Captain lifted her hand. The men stopped poling. The raft drifted, a few lengths out, and held there against a pole jammed into the bottom, and the men stood on it and looked at the lion, and the lion lifted its head off the gravel and looked back.

It didn't get up. Maren watched it try. She saw the great shoulders bunch, and the forelegs gather under it, and the hurt foot come down on the gravel, and then the lion made the short deep coughing grunt she'd heard in the night, and the foot came up again, and it lay down. It lay with its head up, watching the raft, with its lips going back from its teeth. But it lay down.

"Well," said the Captain, and her flat Saltreach voice came clear across the water. "Look at that. It's done half our work for us."

---

They did the rest carefully. She had to give them that, again.

They brought the raft in to the far side of the spit, away from the lion, downwind, and came ashore there, and set the pots first. Six of them, in a half-circle, upwind of it on the slope below the fort, so the river air carried the smoke down over the gravel. The sweet heavy smell came up the slope to her in the gap of the wall and she put her sleeve over her face and breathed through it. Below her the lion turned its head toward the smoke, and its lips went back, and it made a low sound, and then its head went down onto the gravel. Its eyes stayed open. Pale, and flat, and watching. But its head went down.

Then the chain.

They put a collar on it first. A great hinged collar of iron, in two halves, that two men carried up from the raft between them on a pole, and that they had to go right up to its head to fit. She watched them go. They went as you'd walk out onto ice. Slow. Not breathing. The Captain stood a few paces off with something in her hands, a long pole with a hook on the end, and talked to them low the whole time. They got the collar round its neck, under the mane. They closed it. One of them put the pin through it and hammered the pin over, and at every blow the lion's eyes moved, and the man's hands shook so badly he missed twice.

From the collar ran a chain. Short, heavy, thicker than any of the others. They took the end of it a little way up the slope to the foot of the landing stair, and drove a great iron stake down through the end link into the gap between two paving stones, a stake as long as a man's leg, with a sledge, blow after blow, until it was in to the head.

Then chains over its body. Three of them. Over the shoulders, over the ribs, over the hindquarters, and staked on both sides into the gravel. They didn't go near the hurt foot. Nobody did.

When it was done, they stood back, all of them, and looked at it lying there on the spit under its chains, with the smoke going over it and its pale eyes open, and somebody laughed. High, shaky.

"Shut up," said the Captain. But she was smiling.

---

They sat on the gravel by the raft afterwards, out of the smoke, and ate, and talked. They thought they were alone. They'd seen nothing of her in the night but a cut rope and an empty boat, and they'd decided, she supposed, that whoever had been on the island had drowned, or bled, or run off into the trees to die, and that it didn't matter which. The river carried their voices up the slope to the gap in the wall.

"We load at dark," said the Captain, before anyone asked. "Not before." She jerked her head across the west channel, toward Redwatch. "They've had boats all round this island two days, in daylight, looking for something. I don't want them looking at a cage."

"What were they looking for?"

"Whoever came over in that boat we cut loose. One of theirs, I'd guess. Who cares. They're not here now."

"The man said…"

"The man," said the Captain, "isn't here either. The man's gone after the farmer. You heard him." She drank from a leather bottle and passed it on. "He's done with the cat. He said so. He says it's ours to finish, and the money's at Wendmouth when we bring it in. He's after something else now."

"The dragons," said one of them.

There was a silence.

"The Clan's three," said another. Older. A slow, careful voice. "The loose ones. Is it true, then? What the boy at the Eelweir said?"

"Who knows what's true over there," said the Captain. "Their riders are dead. That's true. All three, in the fight at the mill, the Clan boy said, and the beasts should've gone after them, the way they do. And they didn't. They're lying up in some farmer's field over by that burned village, alive, all three." She shrugged. "Bonded, the boy says. To the farmer. All three of them. Overnight."

Somebody laughed, disbelieving.

"I'm telling you what he said. I don't know. The man believes it. He had it from the boy at the Eelweir this morning, when we put in for water, and was off up the hill toward that farm inside the hour, and I've never seen him move that fast for anything." The Captain looked across the river at the far bank, where a long thin smoke was still going up from Harrow's End into the grey sky. "He says the people he works for would pay twenty times what they're paying for the cat. For the three of them. Twenty times." She spat on the gravel. "And we get the cat."

"Twenty times," somebody said, wonderingly.

"Twenty times," said the Captain, "for three full-grown war dragons, bonded to a man you'd have to get past them to get at. You want that job? Go after him. Swim." She stood up. "I'll take the cat, that's lying here bleeding with a collar on. Get some sleep. Two on watch. We move at dark."

---

Maren sat in the gap of the wall with her sleeve over her face and her arm burning under the linen.

*Bonded. To a farmer. All three of them. Overnight.*

It wasn't possible. Everyone knew it wasn't possible. One beast, one rider. One life between two. She'd grown up knowing it. Her father had died of it. And three dragons, three, to one man, overnight? It was the kind of thing the Clan would say. It was the kind of thing a boy on a jetty would say to a stranger for a drink.

But the man had believed it. *I've never seen him move that fast for anything.*

She thought of the boots with the brass eyelets. The hands in the pockets. A face like water. *No. We won't need to.*

She thought of the dragons screaming across the river in the night. Three voices, going up and up, and then stopping, all at once. As if a door had shut on them.

She looked across at the long thin smoke going up from the far bank and thought that whatever had happened over there, the man with the forgettable face had gone across the river to find it, with whatever he carried in those pockets, and she didn't like that at all.

She looked down at the spit.

The lion lay on the gravel in its chains, in the smoke, and its eyes were open, and it was looking at her.

---

She waited for the dusk.

She didn't decide anything. She'd tell herself afterwards that she'd sat there all afternoon deciding, weighing it up, the trial and the law and her arm and the eight men by the raft and the Captain with her hooked pole. She hadn't. Somewhere in her, in the gap of the wall, under the smoke, it had been decided already, and she just sat there and waited for it to be dark enough.

*You leave it be,* Ottery said, in her head. *You never, ever, go and look.*

She'd gone and looked.

The light went. The river went from grey to black. Across the water, a few lights came on in Harrow's End, fewer than there should have been. Down on the spit the hunters were moving. She watched six of them get onto the raft and push off, with poles and a lantern, out into the current and away across toward the Saltreach bank, for a windlass and the rest of the gear, and come back for it.

Two stayed. They sat by a small fire on the gravel, upwind of the pots, between the fire and the lion, with crossbows across their knees. Crossbows. She looked at them. Every hunter she'd ever stopped on the river carried a bow, or a spear, or nets. These had crossbows. She didn't know why that bothered her. It did.

She took the cold chisel and the hammer out of her pack.

She soaked her neckcloth in the water from the little stream that came down out of the fort, and wrung it out, and tied it over her nose and mouth. She put the chisel through her belt, and the hammer through it on the other side. She looked at her arm. She flexed the hand. It hurt. It worked.

She went down the slope in the dark.

---

The landing stair came down out of the fort's south side in a long slant of broken paving to the water. The collar-chain was staked at the bottom of it, at the foot of the paving, where it met the gravel. A stone's throw from the guards' fire. Three paces from the lion's head.

She went down the edge of the paving on her belly, along the inside of the old parapet where the shadow was deepest, the way she'd learned to come up on smugglers in the reeds. Slowly. One hand. Then the knees. Then the hand again. The smoke came over her in long sweet waves and she breathed through the wet cloth and her head swam anyway, and she stopped, and waited, and went on.

She came to the stake.

It stood up out of the paving, an iron head as big as her fist, with the last link of the collar-chain round it. The chain ran from it, taut, three paces, across the gravel, to the collar. To the lion.

It was so close. She could feel the heat of it. She could hear it breathing, short and shallow, through the smoke. She could see one great forefoot on the gravel in the light of the guards' fire, and the curve of its jaw, and the collar under the grey mane, and one eye.

The eye was open. It was looking at her.

She lay still for a long time, looking back.

*Don't,* she thought at it. Not words, really. Just the feeling. *Don't move. Not yet. I know. I know. Just wait.*

The eye looked at her. It didn't blink.

She got up onto her knees. She put the edge of the cold chisel on the link round the stake, where it was thinnest, on the side away from the fire. She lifted the hammer.

---

The first blow rang out across the river like a bell.

She'd known it would. There was no way to cut iron quietly. She'd thought about it all afternoon, in the gap of the wall, and known that the first blow would wake the guards and the second would bring them and that she'd have, perhaps, three. Four if she was lucky. And she'd thought: the chisel's new. The link's old. Their own chain, from their own camp, that's been lying out in the rain on that bluff since summer. She'd seen the rust on it. Four might be enough.

The first blow rang, and she lifted the hammer and brought it down again.

"*What…*" One of the guards. On his feet. She heard the crossbow come up.

Third blow. The link bent. She felt it go, under the edge of the chisel, a little, as ice goes before it breaks.

"*Somebody at the chain!* Somebody at the… *Gods,* it's a girl, it's…"

She heard the crossbow go *thock*, the sound she'd heard on a hundred autumn shoots on the river. Something went past her ear, close enough that she felt the wind of it, and rang off the paving behind her. She didn't look up. She lifted the hammer.

Fourth blow.

The link parted.

---

She was on her back before she knew it, thrown or fallen. She was lying on the broken paving with the hammer still in her hand and the chisel gone, and the stars over her, and the ground moving.

Moving. Under her. Not the way it moves when something heavy walks on it. The way it moves when it has decided to. The whole slope of the stair heaved under her back like the flank of a horse, and the old paving stones lifted, and dropped, and lifted again, and she heard them grind against each other along their seams. A stake came up out of the gravel beside her with a sound like a cork from a bottle. Then another. She heard stone split. A long, clean, cracking sound, going away from her down the slope, as if somebody were tearing a sheet of linen as big as a field.

The lion stood up.

It came up off the gravel in the firelight, in the smoke, all of it at once, with the broken collar-chain swinging from its neck and the body-chains sliding off its shoulders and its ribs and its flanks like water off a dog, clanking down onto the gravel. The stakes that held them came up out of the ground after them, pulled loose, one after another, and fell. It stood there with its head up and its mane up round its head like a storm cloud, nearly twice a man's height at the shoulder, in the light of the fire, on three legs. The ground under it went still.

The guard with the crossbow was on his knees. He was trying to reload. His hands couldn't do it.

The lion turned its head and looked at him.

It didn't roar. It didn't need to. It went toward him, two steps, three, on three legs, limping, slow, and he dropped the crossbow and got up and ran, and the other one was already running, and it swung its head at him as he went and caught him, and he went through the air and into the river with a cry and a great splash, and came up, and went away downstream in the dark, shouting.

It stood alone on the gravel by the fire, with the chain hanging from its collar, and breathed.

And turned, and looked at her.

---

She lay on the paving and didn't move.

It came up the slope toward her, slow, limping badly, holding the hurt foot off the ground, setting it down only when it had to. The broken collar-chain dragged and rang on the paving beside it. It came up the slope until it was standing over her, so close that the grey mane hung down over her, and she could smell it, fur and blood and honey-smoke and cold stone, and its breath went over her face, hot.

Its head came down. Lower. Lower. Until the great broad scarred face was an arm's length above hers, and the pale eyes were looking straight into her own, one and then the other, as a horse looks at you, first with one eye and then the other, to be sure.

She lay there with the hammer in her hand and didn't lift it.

The lion looked at her. Whatever it saw, whatever thinking was to a thing like this, that was older than the fort, older than the road, older than anything, she couldn't guess at it. She just lay there, and let it look.

At last it lifted its head and turned away.

It went up the slope past her, limping, and through the gap in the south wall where she'd sat all day, and across the paved road in the middle of the fort, and out the other side, north. Into the trees. She lay on the paving and listened to it go. The chain, dragging, fading. The slow uneven tread. When she couldn't hear the chain any more, very faint, from the north end, under the oaks, a long breath let out, like a door closing.

She lay there till she could feel her hands.

---

They came back for her, of course.

The raft came back across the water an hour later with its lantern swinging and the men poling hard, and she heard the shouting on the gravel when they found the guards gone and the chains lying empty on the spit and the collar-chain cut through at the stake. She heard the Captain. She'd never heard a voice go so flat and so quiet.

She was up the tower by then. Up the broken stair, as far as it went, with her back against the wall and her knees drawn up, in the dark, under the ash tree. She heard them come up into the fort with their lanterns. She heard them go through it, every corner, every heap of fallen stone, calling to each other. She heard one of them come in under the arch, under the verse, into the tower, and stand there at the foot of the stair, breathing hard, and lift his lantern, and the light went up the inside of the wall toward her, and stopped three steps below where she sat, where the stair turned.

He stood there.

Then, from the north end of the island, from the dark under the oaks, very low, there came a sound. A rumble, deep down, under everything. She felt it in the stones of the stair under her. She heard the man at the foot of the stair hear it too, heard his breath stop.

The lantern went away down the stair and out under the arch, fast.

"Leave it," she heard the Captain say, a while later, from the paved road outside. Not shouting. Not even loud. "Leave it. Whoever she is. We'll not find her in the dark, not with that thing awake and walking about out there. I'm not losing more men for a girl with a hammer." There was a pause. "We'll come back. With men enough to take this whole island apart stone from stone, and her with it. And the cat."

"When?"

"When I say."

They went. She heard the raft push off, and the poles, and the voices going away across the river toward the Saltreach bank, until there was nothing at all but the water.

She sat in the dark at the top of the broken stair, with the hammer in her lap, and her arm on fire under the linen, and her whole body shaking. Toward morning, from the north end, the breathing came back.

Short, still. Shallow. Hurt.

But there.

She counted it until she fell asleep.
