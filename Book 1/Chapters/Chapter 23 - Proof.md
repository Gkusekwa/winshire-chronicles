---

There's a thing they don't tell you about proof, at Greyhold, in all the lessons about evidence and witnesses and the chain of custody of a seized beast. They don't tell you that proof is always something you've taken. A lock of hair. A feather. A tooth. A body. You can't prove you've seen a thing without carrying a piece of it away. That's what proof is. It's a theft that other people agree to believe.

---

The fever came on in the night after the spit.

She'd known it would. She'd known since she'd washed the cuts in the hunters' camp and seen the edges, white and puckered and angry. On the river watch you saw it every year: a hook in the hand, a gash from a rowlock, a dog bite. Nothing, the first day. Hot and red the second. And on the third, the shaking.

She shook. She lay in the tower on her blanket with her back against the wall and shook so hard her teeth knocked, and was hot, and then cold, and then so hot she crawled out under the arch in the night and lay on the paving of the old road with her face against the stone. The stars went round. The breathing from the north end went in and out, short and shallow, the way it had been since the chain, and she counted it, and lost count, and counted again, and in the end she wasn't sure which of them was breathing like that, the lion or her.

In the grey of the tenth morning she crawled to the hunters' camp.

She didn't remember most of the way. She remembered the notches on the trees, one by one, and her good hand on each of them. She remembered the gravel under the bluff. She remembered pulling the tarp off the stores and going through them on her knees, sacks and bundles and crates, looking for she didn't know what. Salt. Vinegar. Spirit. Anything.

There was a crock of honey.

A brown clay crock with a waxed cloth tied over it, tucked in among the salt meat. Somebody's luxury. Somebody who'd liked something sweet on his bread at the end of a long day of laying traps. Maybe the young one in the hollow. Maybe the one who'd run into the jaw-trap in the dark.

She sat on the gravel with the crock between her knees and unwound the linen from her arm, which took a long time and made her cry out, and looked at the cuts, and then took the honey in her fingers and laid it into them. Thick. Gold. Into each one, from the shoulder to the elbow. The way her mother did for burns on the ferry, when a passenger's child put its hand on the hot stove of the cabin, or a boatman caught a rope wrong. *Honey,* Liss always said. *It keeps out the rot. Nobody knows why. It just does.*

She bound it up again in fresh linen. She lay down on the gravel under the bluff where the hunters had slept, with the tarp over her, and slept, and woke shaking, and slept again.

When she woke for good it was dawn on the eleventh day, and the fever had broken, and she was so weak she couldn't stand, and so hungry that she ate a whole hand of salt meat lying on her side on the gravel like a dog.

By noon she could walk. She went back to the tower a few steps at a time, from tree to tree, and slept again.

---

She went to the stream at dusk on the eleventh day, because it was the nearest water to the tower and she couldn't go another hour without drinking.

She took a brand from her fire. She told herself it was for the dark under the trees. She carried it in her good hand, a split of pine from the hunters' woodpile, burning at the end, and the tin cup hooked on her belt beside the hammer, and went down through the trees, holding on.

The lion was there.

It was standing at the edge of the stream, where she'd found the first print, where the mud was soft and black and the water came down over the stones into the river. It had its great head down to the water and its forelegs braced, the hurt one held off the ground, and she could hear it lapping, a slow heavy sound, like a cart going over a wooden bridge.

She stopped.

It lifted its head and looked at her, water running from its jaw.

They stood there with the stream between them. Twenty paces. She could see the collar on its neck under the grey mane, the two halves of the iron with the pin hammered over, and the broken end of the chain hanging from it, the cut link she'd made, bright. She could see the loop still on the forefoot, sunk deep in the fur, black with dried blood. The foot was swollen. The pads were cracked.

She thought: *it knows me. It looked at me on the spit. It let me go.*

The lion's lips went back from its teeth.

She had time to see that. She had time to see the pale eyes go flat, the way they'd gone flat on the shore the night it woke. She had time to think, very clearly, *no, it doesn't, it doesn't know anything, it's a wild thing and it's hurt and I'm standing by its water.* Then it came through the stream at her.

It didn't come slowly. It came like a cat off a wall, all at once, three legs and the bad one held up, the water going up white on both sides of it, and the bank shook under her as it hit the near side, and she felt the shake go up through her boots. It was on her before she'd moved.

She moved.

Her body did it, the body that Greyhold's yard had made: she went sideways and down, the way Pell had made them go a thousand times in the sand, under the swing and not away from it, and the great forefoot went over her head with a sound like a sail filling and took the bark off the alder behind her in a long white strip. She came up on one knee with the brand still in her hand. She didn't strike with it. She held it out, between them, low, at the level of its face, and swept it once, sideways, and the fire went across the air in front of its eyes in a long orange arc.

The lion reared back. It threw its head up, away from the fire, and made a sound she'd never heard, a short hard coughing snarl, and the mud of the bank heaved under its feet as if the ground itself had flinched.

She ran.

Up through the trees, by the notches, in the dusk, with the brand streaming fire behind her and her arm screaming under the linen and something enormous coming after her through the undergrowth on three legs, not fast, not as fast as it should have been, crashing, lurching, the chain on its foot ringing on the roots. Up the slope. Through the west gate of the fort. Across the paved road. Under the arch of the tower, under the verse, into the dark.

The arch was narrow. She'd noticed it the first night. The tower's doorway had been made for men, not for anything else, and it was barely wider than her shoulders and not much taller than her head.

The lion hit it.

It hit it with its shoulder, the whole weight of it, and the tower rang like a bell. Dust came down. Grit. A stone fell out of the wall somewhere above her in the dark and smashed on the floor beside her blanket. She was against the far wall by then, with the brand held out in front of her, and she saw its head in the doorway, filling it, the grey mane jammed in the arch, the pale eyes, the teeth. It couldn't get through. It drew back and came again, and the whole tower shook, and she heard the ground under the tower groan, deep down, a long low grinding like a millstone, and a crack ran up the inside of the wall beside the arch, slow, through stone that had stood for a thousand years.

Then the forefoot came in.

The good one. Through the arch, low, reaching, the claws out, all four, long and pale and curved, raking at the floor of the tower a pace from her boots, tearing up the earth and the old stones and the bones of whatever small thing had died in here. Reaching for her.

She put the brand to it.

Not into it. She didn't drive it in. She held the burning end in front of the claws, close, so close that the fire licked at the fur between the toes, and held it there, and didn't strike. The foot snatched back. The claws went in. A sound came through the arch, out of the dark, that was so deep and so long that she felt it in her teeth, and in the stones at her back, and in the floor, and the crack in the wall beside the arch ran up another hand's breadth and stopped.

Then it was gone from the doorway.

She heard it outside. Pacing. Round the tower, slow, on three legs, the chain dragging on the paving of the old road. Round, and round. All night. She sat against the far wall with the brand burning down in her hand and fed it splinters from the hunters' firewood to keep it going, and didn't sleep, and listened to it go round. Once it stopped at the arch and breathed in, long, through the doorway, smelling her. Once, near dawn, it lay down outside, against the wall, so close that she could hear its breathing through the stones.

When the light came it had gone.

She went to the arch and looked out. In the earth of the old road outside, there was a ring worn into it all the way round the tower, a pace from the wall, where it had walked all night. On the stones by the doorway, where the foot had reached in, there was a little blood, from the bad foot, where it had put its weight down to reach.

She sat in the doorway in the grey light and shook.

*It doesn't know you,* she told herself. *It doesn't know anything about you. You let it go off a chain and it doesn't care. Why would it care? You smell of their smoke. You smell of Greyhold. You stood by its water with fire in your hand.*

She looked at the brand, burned down to a stub in her fist. She'd held it out. She'd swept it. She hadn't struck.

*It tried to kill you.*

*Yes.*

*And you didn't touch it.*

*No.*

If that meant anything to it, there was no telling.

---

She went back to the stream at dusk on the twelfth day.

She couldn't have said why. She'd sat in the tower all day telling herself not to. She'd drunk the rainwater from the hollow in the paving, which tasted of moss, rather than go down. At dusk she'd got up, and taken the cup, and gone. No brand this time. She'd thought about it, and left it. Something to do with the ring worn in the earth round the tower. Something to do with the fact that it had lain down, near dawn, against the wall, and breathed.

The lion was at the stream. It lifted its head and looked at her.

She didn't go any closer. She stopped at the top of the bank, on her side of the water, where she had stood the night before. Then, very slowly, she sat down. On the cold wet earth, with the tin cup in her lap. She didn't look at it straight. She looked at the water, mostly. Now and then she let her eyes go up to it, slowly, and away again.

It watched her. She watched the water. Her heart was going so hard she thought it must be able to hear it.

Its lip went back. She saw it, at the edge of her eye. She made herself not move. She sat there with every muscle in her body shouting at her and the cup in her lap, and looked at the stream going over the stones.

The lip came down.

It watched her a while longer. It put its head down and drank again.

When it had finished, it turned on three legs, and went away up the stream into the trees, north, without looking back.

She sat there until it was dark. She filled the cup, and drank, and filled it again, and went back to the tower, and lay on her blanket, and found that she was crying.

She thought about it in the dark, lying there. She'd come here to fetch proof. She had a deadline. The new moon was tomorrow, or the day after; she'd lost count in the fever, and had to work it out on her fingers by the days since the fair. She had nothing. A link of chain. A notebook. A lion that would have to be seen to be believed, that had tried to kill her twice.

Instead she'd gone down to its water and sat in the mud with a tin cup, being very careful not to do anything.

*Not doing anything,* she thought, *is a thing too.*

It sounded like something Pell would say. Or her father.

---

On the thirteenth day the lion slept in the sun.

She'd come down to the shore at noon to wash her arm in the river, and it was there. Not at the stream. On the open grass of the shore above the reeds, where she'd stood with Pell's sword on the night it woke. Where the noon sun lay warm on the grass. It was lying on its side, the way it had lain in the hollow, with its back to the trees and its legs stretched out and its great head on the grass, and its eyes were shut, and it was asleep.

Not the smoke-sleep of the hollow, with the eleven breaths. Something heavier than ordinary sleep, and worse. Its sides went up and down slow and shallow. A fly walked across its ear and it didn't stir. It was sleeping as a beast sleeps when it has bled for a week and has nothing left to stay awake with: sunk, gone right down, because it was warm, and hurt, and nothing on the island could do it any harm.

She stood at the edge of the trees and looked at it.

Then she walked out onto the grass.

She didn't decide to. That was beginning to be the way of things, on the island. She walked out slowly across the open grass toward it, in the sun, with the river going by below and the birds in the trees behind her. Ten paces. Five. Three. Until she was standing beside its head, by the great grey mane, close enough to touch.

It didn't wake.

She looked down at it. At the mane, so close now, iron grey and gold and almost white at the roots, coarse as a horse's tail, matted with the leaves of a hundred autumns and the moss of the hollow and, here and there, a burr, a twig, a long tangle of something like dry ivy. At the scars on its face. The collar under its jaw. The ear, as big as her two hands, with a notch out of the edge of it, old, healed long ago.

She took out her knife.

It was an ordinary knife. Her river-watch knife, that she'd had since she was seventeen, with a horn handle worn smooth. It was sharp. She kept it sharp. And it would take her a breath, less, to reach out and take a lock of that mane in her fist, and cut it, close, a hand's length of grey and gold, and step back. It would never know. It was asleep. And she would have it. Proof. A lock of the mane of the monster of the Old Ruin, as long as her forearm, iron grey, nearly white at the root, like nothing anyone at Greyhold had ever seen. She could lay it on Rowan's desk.

*Bring me proof.*

She stood there with the knife in her hand.

The lion opened one eye.

Nothing else about it moved. It just opened one eye, the near one, pale as river ice, and looked up at her from the grass, sideways. It looked at her face. Quite slowly, it looked at the knife.

Then back at her face.

It didn't growl. It didn't lift its lip. It didn't do anything at all. It just lay there in the sun with its one eye open and looked at her.

She thought about the hunters' smoke, and the chain going over its foot in the hollow while it slept. The stakes going in. The men creeping up on it in the dark, night after night, with their lanterns and their pots, because it was asleep and wouldn't know.

*It's a theft that other people agree to believe.*

She put the knife away.

She did it slowly, so it could see. She put the blade back in the sheath on her belt and let go of the handle and let her hand drop to her side. She stood there a moment longer, in the sun, beside its head.

She turned and walked away, back across the grass to the trees, not fast, not looking back, with her back to it the whole way and every hair on her neck standing up.

Behind her, nothing happened. Nothing at all. When she reached the trees and turned round, it had closed its eye.

---

They came for her before dawn on the fourteenth day.

She heard the oars. She was awake; she'd hardly slept. She heard the knock of oars in rowlocks out on the dark water, downstream, coming up against the current. Not a raft. Not poles. Oars, one pair, pulled in long slow strokes by someone who knew the river. She was on her feet and out under the arch with the hunters' hammer in her hand before she was properly awake.

A punt came round the south point in the dark. Small. Low in the water. A lantern hooded at the stern. Two figures in it.

One of them, at the oars, was old, and hunched, and rowed like a man who'd been doing it for sixty years and expected to do it for sixty more. The other sat in the stern by the lantern, very straight, very small, in a shawl.

The punt came in to the spit and grounded on the gravel with a long crunch.

The figure in the stern stood up, and climbed out over the side into the shallows, and walked up the gravel, and stopped at the foot of the paving where the old road came down. And put back her shawl.

"Maren," said her mother.

---

Liss didn't speak to her the whole way back. Not one word.

She'd said that one word, on the spit, in the dark. Then she'd looked at Maren's arm, bound up in white from the shoulder to the elbow, and at her face, and something had gone across her own face that Maren had never seen there before and hoped never to see again, and turned round and got back into the punt, and sat in the stern with her shawl round her and her hands in her lap, and waited.

Ottery had looked at Maren from the oars. He hadn't said anything either. He'd just jerked his head at the bow.

So she'd got in. She'd brought her pack, and the hammer, and nothing else. She'd sat in the bow, with her back to them both, looking upstream at the island as they pulled away from it. The stump of the tower against the first grey of the light. The trees, and the north end, where it was, under the oaks, sleeping. She couldn't hear it. The oars were too loud.

Ottery rowed. Liss sat. Nobody said anything. Down the long reach of the river in the dawn, past the mouth of Hollow Beck and the burned mill at Harrow's End, a black heap on the far bank with people moving about it, past Tolly's Staithe with its smoke going up and the guide rope of the ferry hanging across the water, and in toward the Greyhold landing.

At the landing steps, Ottery shipped his oars and the punt drifted in against the stone, and Liss got out, and stood on the bottom step. And turned round.

"Your father," she said, "came back from that island in one piece. And never went near it again as long as he lived." Her voice was quite steady. "You came back with your arm in a sling and a smith's hammer and no boat, and the whole river's been talking for a fortnight about the girl on the island who won't die." She looked at Maren. "And you're going back. Aren't you."

Maren looked at her.

She didn't answer. She didn't need to.

Liss's face did the thing again. Then it stopped.

"Well," she said. "I'm not losing my daughter to that thing because she was too proud to take her mother's boat." She turned and went up the steps. At the top, by the red door of the ferry-house, she stopped, without looking back. "You'll take the punt next time. Ottery's. He says you can. You'll go at night and come back at night and you'll tell me every time, or I'll row out there myself and drag you off it by the hair. Do you understand?"

"Yes, Ma."

"Good."

The red door shut.

Ottery sat in the punt and looked at the water.

"She was going to come by herself," he said at last. "Last night. In the dark. In the ferry's little skiff, the one with the leak. I caught her at it." He scratched his jaw. "Thought I'd better come."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me." He looked at her, then. Sharp, old, river eyes. "What's out there, girl?"

She looked at him.

"Something holed," she said. "That everybody wants."

"Then you leave it be," he said. "That's the law of it." He bent to his oars. "But you won't, will you. You never would."

---

Rowan's room was at the top of the north tower, up a stair so narrow and so steep that you had to go up it sideways, with your shoulder to the wall. Maren went up it with her arm in its sling and her pack over her good shoulder, and knocked, and went in.

It was as it had been a fortnight before. Small. Bare. A desk under the window with papers on it in neat stacks, and an inkwell, and a ledger. A chair. A shelf of books with no titles on their spines. Rowan sat at the desk with her back to the window and the grey light on her short grey hair, and watched her come in.

"Ashdown," she said. "You're back."

"Yes, Captain."

"On the day."

"Yes."

Rowan's eyes went to the sling, and stayed there a moment, and came back to Maren's face. They didn't change at all.

"Report," she said.

Maren reported.

She stood in front of the desk and told it, from the beginning, the way they were taught: plainly, in order, what she'd seen and what she'd done, and what she thought only when she was asked. The landing on the spit. The fort, the tower, the verse over the arch. She said it out loud, all of it that she'd copied, and saw Rowan's pen stop when she got to the last line. *Keep the watch below, and do not ask.* She went on. The cold fires. The cut chain. The traps. The tracks. The hunters' camp and everything in it: she took out the notebook and laid it on the desk, open at the drawing, and Rowan looked at it without touching it. Eleven coils of chain. Forty stakes. The cage. The raft.

The night the hunters came. Twelve of them. The Captain. The pots, and the sweet smoke, and what she thought it was for. The man who did nothing, with the forgettable face and the good boots with brass eyelets. *We won't need to.*

The hollow, and what was in it.

She told it all. The chains, and the stakes, and the young man, and the other one, and the trap. The fire in the leaves, and stamping it out. The skiff, cut adrift. The dragons screaming across the river. The roar.

She told about the lion. She used the word. She stood in Knight-Captain Rowan's room at the top of the north tower of Greyhold and said, "It's a lion, Captain," and heard how it sounded. Rowan didn't blink.

She told about the shore, and the sword, and the claws. About the chain round its neck on the spit, and the chisel, and what she'd done with it. She didn't leave that out. She'd thought, on the river, in the punt, about leaving it out. She didn't.

And the stream. And the sun.

When she'd finished, Rowan sat without speaking. Then she put out one hand, the one with the ink-stained finger, and laid it on the page beside the drawing, and read the lines under it, where Maren had written, in pencil, on the first morning in the tower: *Breathing. North end. Eleven to the quarter hour. Faster when the dragons called. Da heard this.*

She looked at that for a while.

She closed the notebook and looked up.

"Proof," she said.

Maren put her hand in her pocket and took out the cut link of chain from the cold fire on the first night, and put it on the desk. It lay there on the papers, dark, heavy, with its little ring of six.

"Proof that there are hunters," said Rowan. "That I knew. That's why I sent you." She didn't touch the link. "Proof of the beast."

"I haven't any."

"A hair. A claw. A print in clay. A drawing, even."

"I didn't draw it." She hadn't. She'd tried, once, in the tower, and stopped, and torn the page out. "I haven't anything, Captain."

Rowan looked at her.

"You were close enough," she said, "to cut a chain off its neck."

"Yes."

"Close enough to touch it."

"Yes."

"And you brought nothing back."

"No, Captain."

"Why?"

Maren stood in front of the desk with her arm in its sling. She thought about the sun on the grass. The one pale eye, opening. Looking at the knife.

"Because it would have been stealing," she said.

The room was very quiet. Outside the window, far below, she could hear the yard. Someone shouting orders. The clatter of a falcon's bells.

"Trial failed," said Rowan.

She said it quite simply, without any weight at all. She picked up her pen and wrote something in the ledger on the desk: one line, short. She put the pen down.

"You'll be reassigned," she said. "The Master of Stables will have your name by noon. You'll keep your place in the school; you'll lose your place in the year. You'll stand for your trial again in the spring, if you're let, and if the spring comes." She looked up. "That's the rule. I don't make it."

"Yes, Captain."

"Dismissed."

Maren turned to go. She'd got as far as the door, with her hand on the latch, when Rowan spoke again.

"Ashdown."

She turned.

Rowan hadn't moved. She was sitting at her desk with the grey light behind her, and her small neat hands flat on the papers on either side of the cut link of chain.

"Did you harm it?" she said.

Maren looked at her.

"No, Captain."

"It struck you." Rowan's eyes went to the sling. "Did it try again?"

"Yes." Maren heard her own voice, quite flat. "At the stream. And then at the tower door, all night."

"And you didn't harm it."

"No, Captain. Not once."

Rowan looked at her for a long moment, and picked up her pen again, and bent to the ledger, and wrote something else. Not on the line where she'd written before. In the margin. Small. Maren couldn't see what it was, from the door.

"Dismissed," said Rowan, without looking up.

She found Pell at the Lesson Wall.

He was sitting on the bench along its foot, alone, in the grey light, with his ruined hand on his knee and his good hand round a cup of something hot. The yard was empty. Everyone was at drill. He looked up as she came across the cobbles and saw the sling and the pack and her face, and he didn't get up.

She stopped in front of him.

"I lost your sword," she said.

She'd been going to say it better. She'd been going to say it on the river, in the punt, in a dozen different ways, and in the end that was all that came out.

Pell looked at her.

"Where?"

"In the river. The west channel. Off the island." She swallowed. "I was holding it when something hit me, and then I wasn't."

"Something."

"A lion."

He didn't laugh. He didn't do anything. He looked at her with his tired old soldier's face, and then at the sling, and back at her face.

"Were you using it?" he said.

"No."

"Why not?"

"I had it up. It was right there. Its throat was right there." She heard her voice crack and didn't care. "And I put it down. And it hit me anyway. And I lost your sword."

Pell was quiet for a while. He drank from his cup. He set it down on the bench beside him.

"Do you remember," he said, "what I told you once, in your first month? About the two ways to stand?"

"Yes."

"You found a third."

"I don't know what I found."

"No. Nobody does, the first time." He looked at his ruined hand on his knee. The thumb and the two fingers, and the smooth places where the other two had been. "Did you lose it," he said, "doing something you can live with?"

She thought about it. She thought about it properly, standing there in the empty yard, the way he'd taught her to think about things. The shore. The sword coming down. The claws. The river.

"Yes," she said.

"Then it's well lost," said Pell.

He picked up his cup again.

"Go and get your arm seen to," he said. "Properly. By someone who isn't you. And then go and find the Master of Stables, before he finds you. He doesn't like waiting." He drank. "And Ashdown."

"Yes?"

"I'll want to hear about the lion. All of it. When you're ready." He didn't look at her. "Not today."

She heard it everywhere that day. In the infirmary, while the old surgeon unwound the linen and looked at her arm and swore and asked who'd put honey in it and then said, grudgingly, that whoever it was had likely saved her the arm. He said the closing-words over the cuts after that, one cut at a time, with his licence tag swinging from his apron string, and she felt the edges itch and draw a little way together, as far as they'd have gone on their own in a week and no further. In the passage outside the refectory. In the stable yard, where she went at noon to find the Master of Stables and found him, and was given a fork and a barrow and the long low barn where the bonded beasts were kept, and the midden behind it.

Everybody was talking about the same thing, and it wasn't the girl on the island. That was old news already. Something else, from across the river.

*Three dragons. In a field. All three, the Clan's three, alive, and their riders dead.*

*Bonded. To a farmer.*

*All three.*

They said it, and then they laughed. Every time. She heard it go round the yard like a joke everybody knew the end of. *A farmer. With three dragons. Overnight. And next they'll say he can fly.*

She heard it again in the evening, in the yard by the stable door, from Haverly.

He'd passed. She'd known he would. He came across the yard toward her in the dusk while she was forking muck into the barrow, and he had a new cloak on, the grey of a passed student, with the silver pin at the shoulder, and Kesh on his wrist with her hood off, and he looked tired and older than he had a fortnight ago. He stopped a few paces off. He looked at the sling, and the barrow, and the fork.

"I heard," he said.

"So did I. Congratulations."

"Maren…"

"You counted them, didn't you." She went on forking. "The Clan. Both halves. From a hilltop. And came back with a list."

"Three days on the ridge above the Drover's Way, below the hold." He didn't sound proud. He sounded like someone who'd been very cold for a long time. "I counted them going out from the hold. And the other lot, coming over the Scarfell from the east. I counted the horns. I counted the fires." He was quiet a moment. "And the night of the fight at the mill I saw the fire from up there, and rode down the Way toward it in the dark. By first light I was on the hill above it. And I saw them."

She stopped forking.

"In a field," said Haverly. "Above that village, Harrow's End. A burned field. Three of them. The big bronze one, and the red one, and the little green one. Lying there in the stubble, all round a man. Just sitting there. A farmer. With three dragons round him like dogs round a fire." He looked at her. "I saw it. Kesh saw it. I put it in my report, every word."

"And?"

"And Rowan read the whole thing, and passed me on the count, and the count was good. Six hundred and forty spears, two factions, three bonded war dragons, and a cliff full that won't fly." He looked away across the yard. "And she didn't say anything at all about the last page. And nobody else believes it. They think I'd been on the ridge too long. They laugh."

Maren leaned on her fork and looked at him.

"I believe you," she said.

He looked back at her, surprised.

"Why?"

She thought about the dragons screaming across the river. Three voices, going up and up, and stopping all at once. And a voice on the gravel of the spit, saying *bonded, the boy says, to the farmer*, and a man with a face like water going across the river the hour he heard, faster than anyone had ever seen him move.

"Because nobody believes me either," she said, and went back to the muck.
