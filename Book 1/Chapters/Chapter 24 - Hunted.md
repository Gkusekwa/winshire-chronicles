There's a word the Clan uses for a beast that everybody wants. It's an old word and it doesn't have a proper meaning any more, only a feeling. *Hearth-lost.* A beast that's hearth-lost has been fought over so long that it doesn't belong anywhere. Every hold has a claim on it and none of them has a fire for it. It goes from hand to hand, and from hold to hold, and it's never home, and in the end, the old men say, it goes into the woods and doesn't come out, and that's the only place that ever really wanted it.

Teo had told him that too. Teo had told him all sorts of things, on the cliff path and at the gate of the top field. He hadn't known, at the time, that he was being given things to keep.

---

The first of them came on the thirteenth day, in the afternoon. Gorm Askell, Arn's steward, the long sour man who'd counted the barley sacks every autumn for ten years. He came up the lane with four riders behind him in grey with the red thread, and stopped his horse at the gate, and didn't look at the field, where Kharrak lay with his head up watching him. He had the look of a man who couldn't make himself.

"Arn Varr," he said, "chief of the Varr by right of blood, sends to Calder Fenn of Harrow's End."

"I know who he is."

Gorm Askell went on as if he hadn't heard. "The beasts in your field are Clan beasts. Bred in the Clan's caves. Raised by Clan riders. Named by the Clan. They belong to the hold, as they have belonged to the hold since there was a hold." He was reading it. He had it on a paper in his hand, and he was reading it off, in his flat counting-house voice. "Arn Varr asks that they be brought to Varrhold within three days. He asks that you come with them, since it seems they will not come without you. You will have a place in the hold as their keeper, and a wage, and the protection of the Varr."

*Keeper,* said Kharrak. *Keeper.* The red tide came up so fast that Calder had to put his hand on the gatepost. *I'll keep him. I'll keep him in my teeth.*

*Be still.*

"And if I don't?" Calder said.

Gorm Askell folded his paper.

"Then Arn Varr will come and fetch them," he said. "And you." He looked at the field at last, quickly, and away. "He'd rather not. He says you covered his brother's face. He remembers that. But he'll do it."

---

The second came on the fourteenth day, in the morning, down the Drover's Way off the moor. Ulric's red-bearded man, with six behind him this time, and a pack-horse. He got down off his horse at the gate, which Gorm Askell hadn't, and leaned his arms on the top bar, and looked up at the three dragons in the field the way you'd look at a fine view.

"Sten Raske," he said. He hadn't given his name on the green. "I'll not keep you, Fenn. You know what I'm here for."

"Same as the last one."

"Not the same at all." He grinned through the red beard. "Arn wants them back in the caves, on chains, with you mucking out after them for a wage. Ulric's not a fool. Ulric's seen what you are." He nodded at the field. "There's never been one like you. Three. Three bonds. There's not a hold in the six kingdoms has seen that, nor heard of it. That's not a keeper, Fenn. That's a champion."

He flicked the cloth off the pack-horse. Another chest. He didn't bother to open it this time.

"Silver," he said. "For the barley I'll not be getting from that mill. For your trouble. And for you, a chair at Ulric's table at Skarrow, the champion's chair, the one Sigrun Holm sat in, gods rest her, with those three behind you." He leaned on the gate. "Think about it. Arn's got two hundred spears. Ulric's got four hundred and fifty. And every bonded pair in the Roosts has shut itself in rather than fight kin, so there's three dragons in this war, and you've got all of them. Bring them east and the war's over by midwinter. You'd save lives, Fenn. You'd save more lives than any man in the Marches."

*He's not lying,* said Aurveth, very quietly. *That is the trouble with him. He is not lying. Ulric would win, with us. And the war would end. And there would be a great many fewer pyres on the Scarfell this winter.*

*Then go,* said Kharrak. *Go east. Burn Arn. Burn them all. Burn…*

*Ulric's champion took Dag off your back, child. Sigrun took him. I took him. I have his blood in my teeth still. And you took Sigrun. Whose side would you like to be on?*

Kharrak said nothing at all. Calder felt the red tide go still, and something underneath it, much worse, come up.

*Sorry,* said Aurveth. Very low. *I am sorry. That was cruel. I am more tired than I knew.*

"No," Calder said, aloud.

Sten Raske raised his eyebrows.

"I'm not saying no to you," said Calder. "I'm saying no to all of it. I'm not taking them east and I'm not taking them west. They're not going on anybody's chains and they're not burning anybody's village for anybody's chair." He heard his own voice, and it didn't sound like him. "Tell Ulric I'm sorry about his nephew. Tell him I don't think he did it. And tell him no."

Sten Raske looked at him. The grin had gone.

"You don't think he did it," he said.

"No."

"Then who?"

Calder said nothing. He stood at his own gate with his hand on the post and his best coat on, because it was the only one not torn, and the weight of the chain against his hip.

Sten Raske straightened up off the gate. He threw the cloth back over the chest.

"He'll not take that for an answer, Fenn," he said. "You know that. Nobody will. Not from a barley farmer with three war dragons in his field." He swung up onto his horse. "Everybody wants them. And there's no middle of the river."

---

The third came that afternoon, from the south, up the Drover's Way from Cotter's Cross, and he was a stranger.

Not Clan. Saltreach, by his voice, coast Saltreach, long vowels and short temper. He was a young man in a good leather coat with a sword at his hip and a hooded hawk on his fist, and he had a paper too, like Gorm Askell, but this one had a seal on it. A big red seal, with a ship on it, and a wave.

"Lieutenant Corrin Ash," he said. "Of Captain Idris Vey's company, out of Wendmouth. Under contract to the Tide Council at Corsa." He held up the paper so Calder could see the seal. "The Captain's heard about your field, Master Fenn. Word gets to the hiring yard at Cotter's Cross fast, when it's dragons. And the Captain holds the Council's commission for the Marches. The Council takes a long view of things. The Council thinks the Clan's war is the Clan's business. The Council will think three war dragons in the Marches are everybody's." He smiled. He had very good teeth. "The Captain's authorised to offer you a contract. A wage. A good one. Better than either Varr. You'd come south, you and the beasts, to Wendmouth, and serve the Council."

"Serve it how?"

"However the Council needs." The smile didn't change. "There's a great deal of coast, Master Fenn. And a great many ships. And people who'd think twice, about a great many things, if they knew the Council had three dragons."

*No,* said Lisska, from the roof of the byre, before Calder could say anything. She'd been very quiet all day. *No. Not the sea. I don't want to go to the sea. Imra said the sea people put hoods on everything. Look at that bird. It's got a hood on. It can't see. Why has it got a hood on? I don't want a hood.*

"No," said Calder.

The lieutenant's smile went, all at once, the way a candle goes out.

"The Captain doesn't usually ask twice," he said. "I'll tell him you were asked once."

---

The fourth came at dusk on the fourteenth day, alone, on foot, and Calder didn't hear him come.

He was in the yard. He'd been sitting on the mounting block by the byre with his leg out stiff in front of him, watching the light go off the hill and thinking about the new moon, which was tonight, and the Ashby widow's face. When he looked up, there was a man standing at the gate.

He was a soft-spoken man. Middle years, middle height, in a good dark coat of plain cloth, with a pleasant open face and grey eyes and a way of standing very still. He didn't look at the dragons. He looked at Calder, and smiled, and nodded, as if they'd met before.

"Master Fenn," he said. "Forgive me. I didn't mean to startle you."

"Who are you?"

"Nobody of any importance." He said it so simply that it might even have been true. "A man who represents some people who'd like very much to help you."

"Everybody wants to help me."

"Yes. I'd heard." The smile widened, ruefully. "Arn wants you as a stable-hand, Ulric wants you as a sword, the Tide Council wants you as a threat. I don't imagine any of them asked what *you* wanted." He leaned on the gate, gently. "The people I speak for would. They'd ask. They'd listen. They believe a man like you, a man who's been given something no one has ever been given, ought to have a say in what's done with it. They believe a great many things are going to change, Master Fenn, very soon, all over Winshire. And they believe you could be part of that. Not anyone's keeper. Not anyone's sword. Part of it."

He reached into his coat, and took out a purse, and set it on the top bar of the gate.

"That's not a payment," he said. "It's a courtesy. For the trouble of listening to me." He took his hand away. "Think about it. I'll come again."

He went. Calder didn't see which way. One moment he was at the gate, and the next the lane was empty, and the dusk was coming down, and the purse was sitting on the top bar of the gate.

*I don't like him,* said Lisska. Very small. *I don't like him at all. He smells cold. Why does he smell cold?*

Calder got up off the mounting block and went to the gate on his crutch and picked up the purse. It was heavy. He opened it.

Silver. New silver, bright, unworn, the coins so fresh from the die that they still had a little burr at the edge. He took one out and turned it over in his fingers. A ship on one side, Saltreach coin, ordinary. A wave on the other. On the edge, very small, struck into the rim where you'd never see it unless you were looking, the way you'd never see it on a pommel unless the light from a stable door was on it:

A ring of six dots.

He stood at the gate with the coin in his fingers.

*That's it,* said Lisska. *That's the smell. That's the cold. It's the same as the thing in your coat. Isn't it? The heavy thing you won't tell me about. It's the same.*

"Yes," said Calder.

*You promised you'd tell me.*

"I know."

*Is it later now?*

He put the coin back in the purse. He drew the string tight. He didn't take it into the house. He walked out into the lane on his crutch, and down it a little way to where the beck went under the road in a stone culvert, and dropped the purse into the water, and stood and watched it go down into the dark.

"Yes," he said. "It's later now."

---

He told her. He told all three of them, sitting in the stubble in the dark at the top of the burned field with his back against Aurveth's foreleg, the way he'd sat the night his mother came out with the lantern.

He told them about the chain. He took it out of his coat pocket and held it up so they could see it, though it was too dark to see anything. He told them about Upper Rush, the morning after the raid, and finding it in the ashes. About the ring of six on every link. About the man on the bucket at the stable door at Varrhold, with a face you forgot while you were looking at it, and soft hands with a pale scar across the knuckles, and good boots, and a knife with a ring of six dots on the pommel. About Teo turning round on the last step of the cliff path and saying it out loud, the hour and the place, three paces from the bucket.

About the empty bucket, on the night of the pyre. *Drew his wages the night before the ford. Off before light. Down the Way, toward the river.*

About the eleven words he'd carried in his pocket for more than a week and never said.

He told it badly. He'd never told anything well. He told it in bits, going back, starting again. All the time, through the marks, he could feel them listening. Not the way people listen. All the way down. Feeling it as he felt it, the cold of the chain, the bucket, the guilt, the long slow sick weight of it that he'd been carrying since the ford. He felt it land in them, one after another.

When he'd finished, nobody said anything.

Then Kharrak lifted his head.

*Him,* he said.

The red tide had gone. What came instead was cold, and very still, and very clear, and Calder had never felt anything like it in his life. Not rage. Past rage. The place rage goes to, after.

*Him. The bucket man. The cold man. Him.*

"I don't know," said Calder. "I don't know it was him."

*You know.*

"I don't…"

*You know. I can feel you know.* Kharrak's great head came round in the dark until it was close to Calder's face, so close the breath went over him, hot. *You knew at the ford. With the boy in your arms. You knew. You've known all along. And you didn't tell the boy. And you didn't tell Arn. And the boy is dead, and Dag is dead, and Sigrun, and Imra, and the hold is at war with itself, and it was him. And you knew.*

There was nothing to say. It was true.

*Kharrak,* said Aurveth.

*Don't.*

*Kharrak. He did not know. He feared. That is not the same. You can feel the difference as well as I can, if you will only stop shouting long enough to feel it.* The great bronze head came down on the other side of him. *He was afraid, and he waited, and he was wrong to wait. And the boy might have died anyway. The cold man might have found another hour and another ford. We will never know. And this one has been carrying that, every hour since, alone, with no one to put it down in front of.* A long breath went out of her. *Well. He has put it down now. In front of us. That is something, I think. That is a thing that is hard to do.*

Kharrak didn't answer. But he didn't take his head away.

From the bottom of the field, where she'd been lying all through it, Lisska said, very small:

*Thank you for telling me.*

Calder put his face in his hands.

*Is that the coin man too?* said Lisska, after a while. *The man at the gate. The soft man. Is he the same as the bucket man?*

"No. A different one. The same mark."

*So there's more than one of them.*

"Yes."

*How many?*

"I don't know."

*Then we'll find out,* said Kharrak. Low. Cold. *All of them.*

Nobody told him not to.

---

They planned it at the kitchen table that night, Calder and Ada, with Wren asleep upstairs and the shutters closed.

It was the new moon. The elders' day. He'd said he'd go, and he'd go: tomorrow night, when they'd had a day to get ready. But not up the lane through the village, in front of everybody, and not by daylight, with Gorm Askell's riders and Sten Raske's and Corrin Ash's all watching the roads.

"Up the Drover's Way," he said. "Tomorrow night, late. Past the ford, up onto the moor, and over toward the Scarfell. There's high ground up there nobody farms. Caves. Kharrak can walk. Aurveth can glide, a bit, from height. We'll go slow." He looked at his mother. "And you and Wren won't be here."

"Calder."

"You won't, Ma. When they come for the dragons and find them gone, they'll come to the house. They'll want to know where. I don't want you here."

Ada looked at him across the table.

"Upper Rush," she said at last. "Bel's. Your sister'll take us. Joss'd never turn anybody away."

"Hob'll drive you. In the cart. He's said he would." He'd asked Hob that morning. He'd gone down to the black heap of the mill on his crutch and found Hob raking through the ashes for nails, and asked him, and Hob had looked at him a long time and then said *all right* in a strange flat voice and gone back to raking. "He'll come up at midnight. You'll go by the river lane, the back way, up along the water to Upper Rush. Nobody'll be watching that road. They'll be watching the Way, and the farm."

"And you'll be on the Way."

"Yes."

"With three dragons."

"Yes."

Ada looked at him a while longer. She got up from the table and went to the dresser and began, very slowly and deliberately, to take down things and wrap them in cloths: the good knife, the salt box, the little brass candlestick that had been her mother's. Packing. Not crying. He'd never seen her cry. He thought, sitting at the table, that perhaps she'd done all her crying before he was born, and had none left.

"Hob," she said, with her back to him.

"I know."

"He's not been right since the mill."

"I know, Ma. There's nobody else with a cart."

She went on wrapping things.

---

Lisska came back at dusk the next day with blood on her muzzle and no deer.

She came in fast, low, over the Fellwood, the way she'd come in over the hedge on the night of the battle, and he felt her coming before he saw her, a high thin frightened clamour in the green mark like a bell rung too hard. She landed in the yard so badly that she went over on her side, and scrambled up, and came to him on the step with her wings still half open.

*Men,* she said. *Men. In the trees. On the Way. Above the ford. Where you're going. Where you said you're going. Lots of them. Horses. Hidden. Waiting. In the dark under the trees on both sides of the Way. I saw them. I was hunting. I saw them and they didn't see me. Lots.*

He sat down on the step.

*And before. Before that. At the mill. The black place. I went over it on the way out, to see, I always go over it, I like the smell of the ash, I don't know why.* She was shaking. He could feel it through the mark. *And the miller was there. The one who doesn't like you. Hob. The soft man was there too. The coin man. The cold one. Standing by the mill in the black. And the soft man gave Hob a bag. It smelled cold. Like the coin. Like the chain. And Hob took it.*

The yard was very quiet.

*Did I do right?* said Lisska. *To tell you? Did I do right?*

"Yes," said Calder. "You did right, little one."

He sat on the step in the dusk.

He thought of Hob at the fair, with his elbows on the trestle and a cup of his own ale, laughing at old Garrow's knees. Hob on the cart on the way up to the ford, *somebody's got to witness properly.* Hob crying in the bracken with his face in his arm. Hob on the green, walking out to the chest, *we've got a mill that needs a new stone and a bridge that's fallen in.* Hob at the gate with the elders. *I lost my mill, Calder. My father's mill. And you came out of it with three dragons.*

*All right,* Hob had said, raking the ashes. In a strange flat voice.

He thought: *he knows the road. He knows the hour. He knows Ma and Wren will be in his cart.*

He got up off the step and went in.

---

"You're not going," he said to Ada. "Neither of you. Not to Bel's. Not in Hob's cart. Not anywhere."

Ada stood in the middle of the kitchen with the brass candlestick in her hands, wrapped in its cloth.

He told her. She listened without moving. When he'd finished she put the candlestick down on the table, very carefully, as if it might break.

"Hob," she said.

"Yes."

"For silver."

"For silver. And because of the mill. And because he's frightened, Ma, and angry, and he thinks it's my fault, and maybe it is." He sat down. "It doesn't matter. They're waiting on the Way. They think I'm going up it tonight at midnight with three dragons, and they think you and Wren will be in a cart on the river lane where anybody can stop it and hold it till I come back for you." He looked at her. "So you're staying here. In the house. Both of you. With the door barred. And when they come, and they find the dragons gone and me gone, you'll tell them the truth. That you don't know where. Because you won't."

"Calder."

"It's me they want. Not you. Not her. Without me here, you're just an old woman and a girl in a farmhouse, and they'll leave you be." He said it as if he believed it. He did, mostly. "With me here, you're the thing they hold till I come out."

Ada looked at him.

"Where will you go?" she said.

"Not up the Way."

"Where?"

He looked at the shutters. At the dark beyond them, where the field was, and above the field the long black shoulder of the hill, and above that the forest.

"In," he said.

---

Wren knew. She came down the stairs in her nightgown with her shawl round her before he'd gone up to wake her, and stood on the bottom step and looked at his face, the way she had on the night of the ford.

"You're going," she said.

"Yes, chick."

"With them."

"Yes."

"Without me."

He couldn't answer that. He went to her and knelt down on the floor of the kitchen in front of her, which hurt his leg and his ribs so badly he had to stop halfway, and took her hands.

"It's not safe," he said. "For you. With me. Not now. People want them. Bad people. They'd use you to get to me. Do you understand?"

She looked at him. She was nine. She understood perfectly.

"Will you come back?"

"Yes."

"Promise?"

"Promise."

"Lisska says that," said Wren. "*Promise.* She says it all the time. She doesn't know what it means." Her chin was going. She held it still. "Does she know what it means now?"

He thought about it.

"Yes," he said. "I think she's learning."

Wren looked at him a moment longer, and pulled her hands out of his and went past him, quickly, to the door, and unbarred it, and went out into the yard in her nightgown, in the dark, without her boots.

He went after her on his crutch.

Lisska was in the yard. She'd been waiting by the step. Wren went straight to her, across the cobbles in her bare feet, and put her arms round the long green neck, as far as they'd go, which wasn't very far, and put her face against the scales.

Lisska went absolutely still. Calder felt it through the mark: a stillness so complete it was like holding your breath under water.

"You look after him," said Wren, into the scales. "You hear me? You look after him. You bring him back."

*I will,* said Lisska. In his head. *I will. Tell her I will. Tell her I promise. Tell her I know what it means.*

"She says she will," said Calder. His voice wasn't working very well. "She says she promises. She says she knows what it means."

Wren let go. She stood back. She touched the blue ribbon at the base of the green horn, once, with one finger.

She turned round and walked back across the yard past Calder without looking at him, into the house, to her grandmother, and the door shut.

---

They went into the Fellwood at midnight, the four of them.

Not up the Way. East. Straight up the slope of the burned field behind the farm, over the top hedge, across the high pasture where his father had grazed the ewes, and in under the first of the great oaks where the forest began. Kharrak walked, on all fours, with his torn wing folded tight and dragging a little, and his breath coming hard. Lisska went ahead, low, flitting from tree to tree like a bat, seeing. And Aurveth carried him.

He hadn't known she would. He'd thought he'd walk. He'd thought he'd hobble along on his crutch in the dark beside them till his leg gave out. But at the top of the field she'd stopped, and lowered her great head to the ground in front of him, and stretched out her neck along the stubble like a bridge.

*Up,* she said. *Behind my head. Where the neck is narrowest. There's a ridge there, between the plates. Sigrun used to sit there sometimes, when she was tired, and wanted to talk to me without the saddle between us. Hold on to the ridge. Don't hold my horns; I don't like it. I'll not go fast.*

He'd never been on a dragon. He'd never been on anything bigger than a dray-mare. He climbed up onto her neck in the dark with his crutch and his bad leg and his broken ribs, and found the ridge between the plates behind her head, and sat astride it, and held on, and she lifted her head, slowly, and the ground went away below him.

He was higher than the roof of the house. Higher than the top of the hedge. He could see the whole valley spread out below him in the starlight: the farm, the house with its shutters closed and a crack of light at the edge, the black field, the village, the black heap of the mill, the river going silver beyond it, the island in the middle of the river, dark.

And up on the Way, above the ford, on the far shoulder of the hill, nothing. Just the dark under the trees. Where the men were waiting.

*Ready?* said Aurveth.

He looked at the crack of light at the edge of the shutters.

"Yes," he said.

She turned, and went into the forest.

---

The Fellwood took them like water.

He'd been told about it all his life. He'd never been in it, not properly, not further than the edge where the charcoal burners worked and the swine-herds took their pigs. Nobody went further. Now he went further. Aurveth went in under the oaks with her great head low and her wings folded tight against her sides, and the oaks closed over them, and the stars went out.

It was so big. That was what he'd remember. The trees were so big. He'd known the oaks at the edge of the forest, the ones you could see from the farm, and thought they were big. They weren't. In here, a league in, the oaks were wider than his house. Their trunks went up into the dark like the pillars of a hall, so far apart that Aurveth could walk between them without folding her wings, so high that he couldn't see where the branches began. The ground under them was soft and deep with leaves and moss, and silent. Nothing grew down here but ferns as tall as a man, in great pale drifts, and toadstools as big as plates, glowing faintly green in the dark. The air smelled of rot and earth and something sweet, like old honey.

They went on all night. East, and up. Kharrak walking behind, heavily, breathing hard. Lisska ahead, always ahead, a flicker in the dark, calling back. *This way. This way. Mind the stream. There's a fallen one, go round. This way.*

Once, in the deep of the night, something moved out in the dark under the trees to their left. Something big. He heard it. A slow heavy tread, and the crack of a branch, and a long deep snuffling breath. It stopped. Whatever it was, it had smelled them. Three dragons. It stood out there in the dark and he felt it think about it, the way you feel weather. Then it went away.

*Bear,* said Lisska, from ahead. *A big one. A very big one. Nearly as big as me. It went away. They always go away from Aurveth.*

*Sensible,* said Kharrak, behind.

Calder sat on Aurveth's neck, in the dark, holding the ridge between the plates, and found that he was crying again, quietly, the way he seemed to be doing all the time now. For the farm, and the crack of light at the shutters. For Wren in her bare feet in the yard. For Hob, raking the ashes. For all of it.

He felt the three of them feel it. Aurveth went a little slower, and Kharrak came up close behind, so close Calder could feel the heat of him, and Lisska came back out of the dark ahead and flew beside him for a while, low, silent, with her wingtip almost brushing his knee.

---

They came to Crow Scar at dawn.

It stood up out of the Fellwood the way a rock stands up out of a river. A great black crag of stone, alone, rearing up out of the treetops two leagues east of the farm and far up the slope of the Scarfell, so high its top was in the cloud. The forest went right up round its foot and stopped there, and the rock went on up, bare and black and streaked with white where the crows had been nesting on its ledges for a thousand years. They were there now, hundreds of them, going up off the ledges in a great black clamour as the dragons came out from under the trees.

High on its face, three-quarters of the way up, there was a cave. A great dark mouth in the rock, wide and tall, with a ledge in front of it like a lip.

*There,* said Lisska. *I found it. When I was hunting. It's dry. It goes back a long way. Nobody's been in it, not for years and years, only bats. And a bear, once, but the bear's dead. A long time ago. There's bones.* She went up to the ledge in a long climbing spiral and landed on it and turned round, very pleased. *See? Big enough for Aurveth. Big enough for all of us. And nobody can get up. Not without wings.*

Aurveth looked up at it for a long time.

*It will do,* she said.

She went up. Not flying; she couldn't fly that far, not yet. Climbing. She put her great foreclaws into the rock at the foot of the crag and climbed it, slowly, the way a cat climbs a wall, with her wings half open for balance and Calder flat on her neck with his arms round the ridge and his eyes shut. Kharrak came behind, with his good wing out and his torn one tight, cursing in fragments the whole way up. At last they were on the ledge, and in the mouth of the cave, and in the dark, and out of the wind.

He slid off Aurveth's neck onto cold stone and lay there.

Below them, far below, the Fellwood went away in every direction, green and black and endless, a sea of treetops under the morning mist, as far as he could see. West, very far off, he could see the edge of it, and the line of the hill, and a thread of smoke that might have been Harrow's End. That was all. Everything else was trees.

Nobody would find them here. Nobody could. You could lose an army in the Fellwood. You could lose three dragons in it, and a man, and never see them again.

*Hearth-lost,* he thought.

---

They lay up in Crow Scar for three days. He slept most of it. When he wasn't sleeping he lay on the floor of the cave in the mouth, in the light, with his leg up on a stone and his ribs aching, and watched the forest, and listened to them.

Aurveth slept too, mostly, long and deep, with her torn back to the wall of the cave, and he could feel it drawing closed through the mark, slowly, like a seam being sewn. Kharrak didn't sleep. He lay in the mouth of the cave with his torn wing stretched out in the sun, and flexed it, over and over, and swore when it hurt, and flexed it again. Lisska hunted, and brought back deer, and went out again, further each time, ranging, the way she couldn't seem to stop doing.

On the third day she came back at dusk without any deer at all.

*There's a girl,* she said.

He was half asleep. He opened his eyes.

*On the island. The one in the river. The old stone one. I went out a long way, right out to the edge of the trees and over the hills to the river, to see, I like to see, and I went down the river and there's the island, and there's a girl on it.* Lisska settled on the ledge, folding her wings, quick and bright. *A grey one. River grey, like the boats. With her arm all tied up in white. Sitting on the bank of a stream, all by herself, at the end of the day.*

Calder sat up.

*And there's a thing,* said Lisska. *On the other side of the stream. In the trees. A cat. A big cat. A huge cat, grey, with all its fur round its head like a cloud, and something round its neck, iron, and it's hurt, it's holding its foot up. It's just standing there. Looking at her. Twenty paces. She's just sitting there. Looking at it. Not moving. Neither of them. Not doing anything at all.*

The cave was very quiet.

*It didn't eat her,* said Lisska. *Why didn't it eat her? It's big enough. It's bigger than a bear. It could eat her in one bite. I would have. I mean, I wouldn't. But I could have. And it just looked at her. Then it went away into the trees. She went away too, the other way, up to the stone tower.* She cocked her head. *Is that the old thing? The one that roared? The night we… the night of the fire? Is that it?*

Calder looked out of the mouth of the cave, west, over the endless dark sea of the Fellwood, toward where the river must be, a long way off, beyond the hills.

*Old,* something had said, in the field.

"I don't know," he said. "Maybe."

*Can I go back and see?*

"No."

*Why not?*

He sat in the mouth of the cave and thought about a girl in river grey, alone, with her arm bound up in white, sitting on a stream bank twenty paces from something that had roared like the end of the world. Not doing anything at all.

*She must be very brave,* said Lisska. *Or very stupid.*

"Imra said those were mostly the same," said Calder.

Lisska went very still.

*Yes,* she said, after a long time, very quietly. *Yes. She did. You remembered.*

He hadn't known he would.
