Grieve met Maren in the yard at dawn on her first morning, with his hands on his hips and his bald head shining in the cold, and before he told her anything else he told her about the midden.

"Older than the school," he said. "First knights who came to the river built their wall round a heap of dung that was already here. Where there's a midden there's beasts. Where there's beasts there's people who need watching." When they dug the foundations for anything new in the north yard, he said, they still found the old midden underneath, black and rich and three hundred years deep. He said it as if it were the most important thing about the place. It may have been.

The Long Barn stood against the north wall, on the inner side, where the sun never reached. It was the oldest building in the school after the north tower. Long and low and stone, with a slate roof that sagged in the middle like an old horse's back, and great double doors at each end, and inside, in the dark, the stalls.

She'd been in it before. Every student had, in the first year, to learn the beasts. But she'd been in it the way a visitor goes into a house: through the door, round the stalls, out again. She'd never been in it the way the people who cleaned it were. Down on the floor. In the straw. At the back of every stall, with a fork.

There were thirty-one bonded beasts at Greyhold that autumn. Grieve told her that next.

"Thirty-one," he said. "And every one of them belongs to somebody who thinks they're better than you. And every one of them knows it." He handed her a fork. "So you'll muck out after thirty-one beasts that know you're nobody, and you'll do it every morning before the bell and every evening after drill, and you'll do it properly, or I'll know. Beasts don't lie about muck."

"Yes, Master Grieve."

"Don't call me master. I'm not a knight. I'm a man who cleans up after knights' animals." He looked at her sling. "Can you do it one-handed?"

"Yes."

He looked at her for a while longer, the way a man looks at a horse he's been sold and isn't sure of.

"We'll see," he said.

---

She could do it one-handed. Badly, at first. Slowly. The fork was heavy and the barrow was heavier, and the left arm, in its sling, kept trying to help and then remembering that it couldn't, so that she'd go to grip with it and the cuts would pull and she'd have to stop and stand in the dark of a stall with her eyes shut until the world came back.

Nobody helped her. That was the rule, Grieve said. The students on midden duty were being punished, and you didn't help someone being punished. So she did it alone, in the dark before the bell, by the light of one lantern on a hook. She'd start at the east end and work west, stall by stall, fork and barrow, and wheel it out the west door and round the back of the barn to the midden against the old wall and tip it, and wheel it back. Thirty-one stalls. Then the yard. Then the water troughs. By the time the bell went for drill her shirt was soaked through and she stank, and she went to drill stinking, and stood at the back of a year that wasn't hers any more, and nobody stood next to her.

She heard them, of course. In the yard, in the refectory, on the stair. Not saying it to her face. Saying it near her, so she'd hear.

*The lion girl.*

*Failed her trial for a monster nobody's seen.*

*Lost her captain's sword in the river and came home with a hammer.*

*Says it's a lion. A lion. In the river.*

She didn't answer. She mucked out.

---

The beasts didn't care. That was the thing she found, in the first days, that she hadn't expected. The students cared. The instructors cared. Greyhold cared, in its grey stone way, that she'd failed. The beasts didn't care at all.

They looked at her, at first, the way Grieve had said they would. Past her. Through her. She'd come into a stall with the fork and the beast in it would shift its weight and look at the wall, or at the door, or at nothing, and wait for her to finish, and go back to whatever it had been thinking about before.

After a few days, some of them started to look at her.

She'd come into a stall in the dark before the bell, and a great head would turn on its neck in the straw and watch her for a moment, the whole time she was forking, with the lantern light in its eyes. She'd go out with the barrow and come back and it would still be watching. What it saw there, if anything, she couldn't tell.

Grieve noticed. He just stood at the end of the barn one morning with his arms folded and watched her muck out the stall of the Darrow girl's hound, the big brindled one that took the arm off a poacher two winters ago and wouldn't let anybody into its stall but the Darrow girl and Grieve himself, who had never paid for a licensed word in his life and said the beasts could tell a bought word from a meant one, and watched the hound lie in the straw with its chin on its paws and its eyes on her the whole time, and not get up.

When she came out, he was gone.

---

Warden Osric Hale came to the midden on the third evening.

She didn't hear him come. She was at the back of the barn, against the old wall, in the dusk, tipping the barrow, and when she turned round with it there was a man standing at the corner of the barn, leaning on a stick, watching her.

She knew who he was. Everyone did. She'd never spoken to him.

He was old. Past sixty, she thought, though it was hard to tell; he had the kind of face that had looked old for a long time and would go on looking the same until it stopped. Tall, and heavy, and stooped, the way big men stoop when they've spent their lives going through doors too low for them. His beard was white and cut short. He leaned on a stick, a plain ash stick with an iron foot, and he stood with his weight off his left hip. He wore the plain grey coat of the inner circle, buttoned to the throat, and at his belt there was a ring of keys: a great many keys, iron and brass, old and new, and among them one longer than the others, black, heavy, plain, that looked older than the rest put together.

She put the barrow down and stood up straight.

"Warden."

"Ashdown," said Hale. His voice was deep and slow and kind, and very tired, the way a voice is when it's been reading by candlelight for forty years. "Don't stop on my account. I've come to look at the midden."

She looked at him.

"It's the oldest thing in Greyhold," said Hale. "Did Grieve tell you that? It's true. I keep the archive. I've read every word ever written inside these walls, and the midden's older than all of them." He came a little way along the back of the barn with the stick, and stopped by the heap, and looked at it with what seemed like real interest. "The first knights built their wall round it. Because where there's a midden there's beasts. And where there's beasts there's people."

"Who need watching," said Maren.

He looked at her.

"Grieve said that," she said.

"Grieve's quite right." Hale smiled. It changed his face completely, for a moment. "He usually is."

He stood there by the midden in the dusk, leaning on his stick. She picked up the barrow handles and stood holding them.

"Captain Rowan," said Hale at last, "showed me your notebook."

She went still.

"I asked her to. I'd heard about the island. Everybody had." He was looking at the midden, not at her. "She showed me the drawing of the camp, and the list of the chains, and the verse. You copied it very well, for a girl who's forgotten most of her father's letters. I could read nearly all of it." He was quiet a moment. "And the last page."

*Breathing. North end. Eleven to the quarter hour. Da heard this.*

"I knew your father a little," said Hale. "Edric Ashdown. Not well. He was a river man. He didn't come up to the archive. But I knew him."

She held the barrow handles.

"I'd like to ask you about it," said Hale. "The thing on the island. If you'll let me." He looked at her, then, properly, for the first time, from under his white eyebrows. "Not as a Warden. Not for any report. Just an old man who reads too much and has been wanting, all his life, to talk to someone who's actually *seen* something." He smiled again, a little. "You can say no. You can go on tipping your barrow and I'll go back to my archive."

She looked at him. He looked back. He didn't seem to mind waiting.

She put the barrow down.

---

He asked a great many questions. She'd thought he'd ask the ones everyone asked, if they asked anything at all. How big? How many teeth? How did you get away? He didn't. He asked other things. He sat down on the edge of the midden wall with his stick across his knees, as if it were a bench in a garden, and asked them one at a time, and listened to every answer, and thought about it before he asked the next.

"Its eyes," he said. "What colour?"

"Pale. Almost no colour. Like river ice, with the light coming through."

"And the mane?"

"Grey. Iron grey. Grey-gold on the body, but the mane's grey, nearly white at the roots. Like an old man's beard."

He nodded slowly.

"Where does it sleep?"

"The north end. Under the oaks. In a hollow." She thought about it. "Not just a hollow. A dip in the ground, with banks of earth all round it, and roots growing through the banks. The trees lean in over it." She hadn't thought about it before. She thought about it now, in the dusk, by the midden. "It's not natural. The hollow. I didn't think about it at the time. But the banks are earth, not rock, and the earth in them is all different colours, in layers, like…"

She stopped.

"Like what?" said Hale. Very quietly.

"Like the side of a grave," said Maren. "When they dig a new one in the river-watch ground, next to an old one. You can see the old one in the side of the new. The different earth. Like that. Like a thing that's been dug, and filled, and dug again."

Hale was silent.

The dusk had come down over the north yard. Somewhere in the Long Barn a beast shifted in its straw. Up on the wall a sentry called the hour.

"When it moves," said Hale at last. "When it walks. When it… roars, perhaps. Does the ground do anything?"

She stared at him.

"Yes," she said. "How did you know?"

He didn't answer that. "What does it do?"

"It… moves. Shakes. Not like a heavy thing walking. Like it's decided to." She'd never said it out loud. It sounded mad. She said it anyway. "When it turned over in the hollow, the earth heaved like a breath. A stone cracked by my face. When it roared on the shore the pebbles jumped and a stone in the old bridge split across, clean, like someone drew it with a nail. And when it stood up off the chains on the spit the paving lifted and the stakes came out of the gravel on their own." She swallowed. "And at the tower, when it was trying to get at me, the ground groaned. Under the tower. Deep down. And a crack went up the wall."

"Trying to get at you," said Hale.

"It's tried to kill me three times." She said it flatly. "On the shore, the night it woke. At the stream. And at the tower door, all night, after."

"And you're still alive."

"Yes."

"Why?"

She thought about it. She'd thought about it every night since, lying in the student barracks with her arm aching.

"The first time, it pulled the blow. It didn't put its weight behind it. I don't know why." She looked at the midden. "The second time, I had fire. I didn't strike it with it. I just held it up. And at the tower, it couldn't get through the door."

"You held fire up and didn't strike it with it."

"Yes."

"Why not?"

"Because I didn't want to hurt it," said Maren. "I'd only just let it go."

Hale looked at her from under his white eyebrows, in the dusk.

"Does it dig?" he said.

She opened her mouth to say no, and stopped.

She thought about the hollow. The banks of earth, layer on layer. The worn path going in under the oaks. The breathing, coming up out of the hollow at night like something at the bottom of a sleep, very far down.

"I don't know," she said. "I've never seen it. But I don't know."

Hale nodded. He got up off the midden wall, with the stick, carefully, putting his weight on his good hip. He stood there in the dusk looking down at her, a big tired stooped old man with a ring of keys at his belt, and that one long black key among them, older than the rest.

"Thank you," he said. "You've told me a great deal more than you know." He hesitated. "The verse. Over the arch. The last line."

"*Keep the watch below, and do not ask.*"

"We say it at the noon bell. Every knight of every order in Hearthmere says it, and has for three hundred years, and none of them knows what it means." He looked away, across the dark yard, toward the old grey stones of the inner wall. "I've spent forty years in that archive trying to find out who first swore it. And here it is, cut in stone in the middle of the river, in letters older than any order, by people who were gone before Greyhold was built." He was quiet a moment. "I'd like to see it. One day. Before I die."

"You could come," said Maren, before she could stop herself.

Hale looked back at her, and for a moment his face was very strange.

"Perhaps," he said. "Perhaps I will." He turned on the stick. "Goodnight, Ashdown. Mind your arm."

She watched him go back along the side of the barn, limping, with the keys at his belt chiming softly at every step, and round the corner, and away.

---

She went back to the island on the eighteenth night.

She'd told her mother. She'd gone down to the ferry-house after evening muck, with the stink still on her, and stood in the kitchen while Liss chopped onions, and said it. *Tonight. Ottery's punt. Back before the bell.* Liss had gone on chopping, and not looked up, and said, *Mind the current at the west channel; it's high this week,* and that was all.

She'd saddled Turnip, who hadn't been asked for anything in a fortnight and seemed to take it personally, and ridden him up the River Road to Redwatch in the last of the light.

Ottery had the punt at the boathouse steps. He didn't say anything either. He just handed her the pole and sat down in the stern and lit his pipe.

"You're coming?" she said.

"I'm sitting in my own boat," said Ottery. "Where you take it's your business."

She poled them out across the west channel in the dusk, close under the reeds. It wasn't far from Redwatch, but it took a long time with one arm. Ottery didn't offer to help. At the island she brought the punt in to the spit, where she'd landed the first time, a lifetime ago, eighteen days ago, and Ottery stayed in it with his pipe, and she went up the gravel and through the fort and down through the trees to the west shore, to the stream.

The lion was there.

She'd known it would be. It was the hour. It was standing at the edge of the stream with its head down, drinking, the hurt foot held off the ground. The foot looked better. Less swollen. The loop of chain was still on it, sunk in the fur. The collar was still on its neck, with the broken end of the chain hanging.

It lifted its head and looked at her.

She stopped at the top of the bank, in her place, and sat down.

Its lip went back. She saw the teeth. She saw the pale eyes go flat, and the great shoulders bunch, the way they had at the stream a week ago, before it came across. Her whole body went cold. She sat in the mud with her good hand flat on the ground beside her, and didn't move, didn't look at it straight, looked at the water.

The lip came down.

It drank.

She sat there till it had gone, and then a long while after. Somewhere far off, very high, a bird went over in the dusk, a big one, going fast, west to east. She didn't see it properly. Just a shape against the last of the light, too big for a heron, wrong for a goose. She thought nothing of it.

She went back to the punt, and Ottery knocked out his pipe without a word, and she poled them home in the dark.

---

Hale came again on the nineteenth day.

Not to the midden this time. He sent for her, at noon, after drill: a boy came to the yard with a message that the Warden would see Ashdown in the archive, if she'd wash first. She washed. She went up.

The archive was under the north tower, below Rowan's room and two floors down, in a long vaulted chamber that had been a storeroom once and was now so full of books and boxes and rolled papers and ledgers that you had to go through it sideways, the way you went up Rowan's stair. It smelled of dust and leather and lamp oil and cold stone. There were no windows. There was one long table under a hanging lamp, and Hale sat at it with a ledger open in front of him and his stick against his chair.

He didn't ask her to sit. He didn't ask her anything at all for a while. He turned pages in the ledger, and closed it.

"I've been reading," he said, "about the old orders. The ones before ours. Before Greyhold." He looked at her. "I'd like to tell you about them, one day. If you're interested."

"I am."

"Good. Good." He nodded. He seemed pleased. He was quiet again, and she waited.

"There's a story going round the yard," he said. "You'll have heard it."

She didn't need to ask which.

"Young Haverly," said Hale, "came back from the Scarfell with a very good count of the Clan's spears and a very strange last page. A farmer, in a burned field above Harrow's End, sitting among three dragons. The Clan's three. Their riders dead." He turned his stick a little against the chair, with one finger. "Nobody believes it. Nobody believes the boy."

"I do," said Maren.

Hale looked at her.

"Do you," he said. It wasn't a question.

She waited.

"The night of the fight at the mill," said Hale. He said it slowly, as if he were choosing each word off a shelf and looking at it before he put it down. "You were on the island. Across the river from Harrow's End. You saw the fire. You'll have heard the dragons."

"Yes."

"Did you see anything? From the island? Anything at all, of what happened in those fields?" He paused. "Of him?"

"No. Only the fire. And the dragons fighting. And then they fell. And then they screamed." She looked at him. "I heard the hunters say it, the next day. On the spit. That the dragons were bonded to a farmer. All three. They'd heard it from a Clan boy at the Eelweir."

"The hunters said so." Hale was very still.

"Yes."

"And did they say anything else? About him?"

She thought of the Captain on the gravel, drinking from a leather bottle. *The man's gone across the river. He's after something else now.* She thought of the forgettable face, and the boots with brass eyelets, and a voice on the raft in the dark. *No. We won't need to.*

"They said someone was going after him," she said. "The man who was with them. The one I wrote down. With the boots." She watched Hale's face. "They said the people he works for would pay twenty times as much for the dragons as for the lion."

Hale didn't move at all. Not a muscle. But she saw something go across his face, very fast, behind the white beard, and go again, and she didn't know what it was.

"Thank you," he said. "Ashdown."

"Warden."

"If you should hear anything." He said it very lightly. Much too lightly. As if it hardly mattered. "On the river. From your mother's ferry, or the fishermen, or the island. About him. The dragon farmer." He was turning the stick against the chair again, with one finger, not looking at her. "Where he's gone. Who's with him. Who's looking for him." He paused. "Whether you've seen him. You'd tell me?"

She looked at the old man at the long table under the lamp, with his ring of keys, and the one long black key among them.

"Yes, Warden," she said.

"Good," said Hale. "Good." He opened the ledger again. "Go and get your dinner."

She went. On the narrow stair outside the archive door she stopped, and stood for a moment with her good hand on the cold stone wall, and thought about the way he'd asked it. The way you'd ask about a key you'd lost, that you didn't want anyone to know you'd lost.

*Whether you've seen him.*

She hadn't. She'd only heard of him. A farmer, in a burned field, among three dragons.

She went down to her dinner, and found she wasn't hungry.
