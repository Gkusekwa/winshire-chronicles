---

His father used to say that the worst question a man can ask is *why me,* because there's always an answer and it's never the one you want. The world doesn't pick you. You're just standing where it happens.

He'd believed that all his life. He was trying to believe it now.

---

The days at Crow Scar had a shape, after a while, as days do anywhere if you stay long enough.

He woke at first light because the crows did. Hundreds of them, on the ledges above and below the cave, all going up at once into the grey with a noise like the whole crag tearing. He lay in the mouth of the cave on his blanket and watched the mist go off the Fellwood as a sheet comes off a bed, and the treetops come up out of it, green and black and endless, all the way to the edge of the sky.

Aurveth slept against the back wall. Kharrak lay in the mouth of the cave with his torn wing stretched out in the sun. Lisska went out before dawn and came back at sunrise with something in her claws, a deer, a hare, once a great grey owl that she'd been very proud of and that nobody would eat. They ate. He ate the bread and cheese he'd bought from the charcoal burners, and drank from the spring that came out of the rock at the back of the cave, cold as iron. He slept again, mostly, through the middle of the day, and his ribs mended, and his leg.

And they talked.

Not all at once. He'd learned, in the first days, that if they all talked at once he couldn't bear it. It was like being in a room with three people shouting, except the room was the inside of his own head and there was no door. He'd sat up on the second night in the dark of the cave with blood running out of his nose and his hands over his ears, which did nothing, and shouted *stop, stop, one at a time, please,* and they'd stopped. Shocked. He'd felt it. Three huge startled silences. Then, one by one, carefully, as if they were stepping round something on the floor, they'd started again.

One at a time.

It helped. It wasn't enough. Some days his head ached from waking to sleeping, a deep slow pounding behind the eyes like a forge going. Some nights he dreamed. He'd be standing up on a dragon's back in the wind and the fire with an axe in his hand, or sitting very straight on a dragon's neck over a burning valley with a long line of fire down his back, or lying in the stubble with something cold in his throat, thinking *Teo.* He'd wake in the dark with his heart going, and lie there, and not know for a long moment whose heart it was.

Some days, the bad days, he couldn't tell whose anger was whose. He'd be sitting in the mouth of the cave watching the forest and feel it come up in him, red and hot, and want to break something, and not know if it was his or Kharrak's, or if there was any difference any more.

*There is,* said Aurveth, on one of those days, from the back of the cave, when he'd thrown a stone so hard at the wall that it shattered. *There is a difference. You can find it, if you look. His is hot and has no bottom. Yours has a bottom. Yours is about something. Look for the bottom.*

He looked for the bottom. It took a long time. It was there.

It was about Teo.

---

On the fourth day at Crow Scar he went down to the charcoal burners.

He went on Aurveth's neck, the way he'd come, down the face of the crag in the grey before dawn with his arms round the ridge and his eyes shut, and then through the forest on foot, on his crutch, the last half-league, alone, because nobody else should know they were there.

The charcoal burners lived in a clearing at the foot of the Scarfell where the Fellwood thinned, under the shoulder of Crow Scar, an hour's walk from the cave. Three turf huts and a great round mound of earth with smoke coming out of the top of it, and a dog, and a woodpile as big as a barn. An old man and his daughter. Hesketh Coley and Rue. He'd found them on the third day, following the smell of the smoke, and Rue had come out of the hut with a hatchet in her hand and looked at him, a stranger on a crutch with blood on his shirt coming out of the deep wood where nobody came from, and said, *You're him.*

He'd said yes. There didn't seem any point saying anything else.

She'd looked at him a while longer. She was about his age, thin and brown and hard, with black hands and black streaks up her arms from the charcoal, and a face that had been crying not long ago and had decided not to any more.

*My man was at the mill,* she'd said. *That night. Carting for Ulric. Two silver for a night's work.* She'd put the hatchet down. *He's under the stones at Cotter's Cross now.*

He'd said he was sorry.

*Wasn't you that did it,* she'd said. *Was them. Both of them. Arn and Ulric and their bloody chair.* And then she'd gone into the hut and come out with bread and cheese wrapped in a cloth, and held it out, and said, *You'll want to pay for that,* and he had.

After that he went back every two days. They didn't ask where he went. He didn't tell them. Old Hesketh, who was deaf and nearly blind and spent all day by the mound feeling the heat of it with his hands and saying the burner's words over it under his breath, the way his father had to keep a mound from flaring, nodded to him and said *the barley man* and went back to his mound. Rue sold him bread and cheese and, once, a pair of boots that had been her man's, and told him the news.

The news came up the charcoal road. Carters came for the charcoal once a week, and they talked, and Rue listened. The war: Arn had burned two of Ulric's farms on the east side and Ulric had burned three of Arn's. Nobody was winning. Gorm Askell had been up to Harrow's End again with riders and searched the Fenn farm, every room and the byre and the field, and found nothing, and gone. His mother and Wren were still in the house. They were well. Ada had stood in the door with her arms folded and told Gorm Askell to his face that she didn't know where her son was, and Gorm Askell had believed her, because it was true.

He'd sat down on Rue's woodpile when she told him that, and not been able to get up for a while.

---

"There's a girl," said Rue, on the fourth day, wrapping the cheese.

He was sitting on the woodpile. He looked up.

"On the island. In the river. The Old Ruin." She tied the cloth. "Weir's boy runs his mouth on both banks, and he's been running it about her for a fortnight. A Greyhold girl. A student. They sent her out there for her trial, to the island, to bring back proof of the monster." She held out the bundle. "And she didn't."

He took it.

"She came back on the new moon in an old man's punt with her arm all cut open and nothing in her hands," said Rue. "And they failed her. Put her to mucking out the stables. Weir's boy says she's the talk of the ferry." She sat down on a log across from him. "He says the monster's real. He says it's a lion, if you can credit that, a lion the size of a hay wain, and it went for her a dozen times, and she never once struck it back. Had a sword, Weir's boy says, and never used it." She shook her head. "And she could have taken a bit of it home, a hair or a claw or something, to show them, and she wouldn't. Wouldn't take anything off it. So they failed her."

Calder sat on the woodpile with the bread and cheese in his lap.

"Why?" he said.

"Why what?"

"Why wouldn't she?"

Rue shrugged.

"Weir's boy says it'd have been like robbing it, to her way of thinking. That's what they're saying on the ferry."

---

He walked back to Crow Scar through the forest on his crutch in the middle of the day, with the bread and cheese in the bundle over his shoulder, and thought about it the whole way.

He thought about a girl in river grey on a stream bank, not moving. He'd thought about her before. Since Fisk's jetty, and the thread of smoke from the tower. Since Lisska came back to the cave at dusk and said *it didn't eat her. Why didn't it eat her?*

Now he thought: *it tried.*

A dozen times, Rue said. And she'd stood there with a sword in her hand and never struck it back.

He'd never met her. He didn't know her name. She was a Hearthmere knight-student, and Hearthmere knights stood on their wall and watched while the Clan burned Saltreach villages and never crossed, and he'd never thought about one of them for more than a moment in his life. But he thought about her all the way back through the Fellwood, under the great oaks, in the green dark.

He thought about Kharrak in the field, dragging himself through the burning straw, and his own hand going out.

He thought: *she didn't take anything. She could have. And she didn't.*

He'd taken three.

---

Kharrak flew on the sixth day.

Calder didn't see him decide to. Nobody did. He'd been lying in the mouth of the cave all morning with the torn wing stretched out in the sun, flexing it, the way he did every day. Out and back. Out and back. Swearing in fragments every time it pulled. *Stiff. Rot. Burn it. Stiff.* And Calder had been half asleep on his blanket, feeling it through the rust-red mark, a deep sick pull at the root of the wing every time, less than yesterday, less than the day before.

Kharrak stood up, and walked to the edge of the ledge, and went off it.

He didn't open his wings. He just went. Over the lip of the ledge and down, head first, toward the treetops a hundred fathoms below, like a stone.

Calder was on his feet before he knew it, with the crutch gone and his leg screaming and his hands on the rock at the edge of the ledge, and he felt it, through the mark, all of it: the drop, the wind, the treetops coming up, the fear. Kharrak's fear. Huge and cold and naked, the fear of a thing that has been flying all its life and has forgotten, in a fortnight on the ground, whether it still can.

"Open them," Calder shouted. Out loud. Into the wind. "Open them. Open your wings, you great fool, open them…"

Kharrak opened them.

The torn one went out last. Calder felt it go. It went out with a shock of pain that went through the mark and into his own shoulder so that he cried out and went down on one knee on the ledge. But it went out. Both of them. They caught the air, and held.

Below him, a stone's throw above the treetops, so low that the wind of his wings tore the leaves off the tops of the oaks in a great green spray, Kharrak stopped falling.

And flew.

Not well. Not straight. He went off sideways, low, lurching, with the torn wing beating stiff and short and the good one too hard, so that he tipped and nearly went into the trees and caught himself and went on. Out over the Fellwood. A long low ragged circuit, round the foot of the crag, through the drifting smoke of the charcoal burners' mound, so low he must have gone right over Rue's clearing. Round, and up. Climbing, beating, the torn wing working in long hard strokes. Up past the ledges where the crows were going up in screaming clouds. Up past the cave mouth. Past Calder on his knees at the edge.

Up.

Calder felt it. Through the mark. The air under the wings. The wind on his face, that wasn't his face. The whole of the Fellwood going away below, green and black and endless. Something else, too, coming up through all of it, huge and wild and red, but not rage. He didn't have a word for it. He'd never felt it in his life. He'd never felt anything like it.

Joy.

*FLY,* said Kharrak, from somewhere very high above the crag, into his head, so loud that Calder put his hands over his ears. *FLY. Fly. I fly. Look. Look. I FLY.*

Behind him, in the cave, Calder heard Aurveth lift her head.

*Yes,* she said. Very quietly. *Yes, child. You do.*

From far off, over the forest, out of sight, Lisska, who had been hunting and felt it through her own mark and come racing back, was shouting too, high and thin and wild: *He's flying! He's flying! Kharrak's flying! Look! Is he? He is! Look!*

Calder knelt on the ledge with his hands over his ears and the three of them shouting in his head and the tears running down his face, and laughed until his ribs made him stop.

---

Kharrak was a different animal after that.

He'd never be gentle. But something had gone out of him, or come back into him, Calder couldn't tell which. He went out every day after that, at first light, alone, and flew. Low over the forest, and high over the crag, and once, far out, so far that his voice went faint in Calder's head and then went altogether, a long silence, a whole hour of it, and Calder sat on the ledge sick with fear until it came back. *Here. Here. Coming.* As if he'd only been in the next room.

"Where did you go?" Calder said, when he came back.

*East. Hills. Far.*

"I couldn't hear you."

*No.*

"Could you hear me?"

A silence.

*No. Could feel you. Frightened. Couldn't hear.*

They tested it, after that. Lisska did, mostly; Lisska would test anything. She flew out from the crag, straight, west, and talked the whole way, the way she always did, *can you hear me now? Now? Now?*, and he sat on the ledge and listened and said *yes, yes, yes,* and then her voice began to go thin. Thinner. A murmur. A whisper, like a voice in another house. And then nothing.

But she was still there. He could feel her. Far off, as you feel a fire in the next room through a wall. Lisska, out there, somewhere over the Fellwood, bright and curious and a little bit worried, wondering if he could still hear her.

*Three leagues,* said Aurveth, when Lisska came back. *Perhaps four. Speech goes further for some pairs than others. Sigrun and I could speak across the Scarfell, on a clear day. But that was after fifty years.* She was quiet a while. *The feeling goes further. The feeling, I think, goes everywhere. I felt Ragnald die from the other side of the mountain. I was asleep. I woke screaming.*

---

He asked them on the eighth night.

He'd been wanting to ask since the field. Since he'd woken up with the three of them in his head and the marks on his chest. He hadn't. He'd been afraid to. Not of the answer; of there not being one.

It was dark in the cave. Lisska lay curled along the cave floor with her head against his legs, the way she liked to sleep. Kharrak lay across the mouth of the cave, with his head on the ledge, looking out over the dark forest. Aurveth was at the back, a great shape against the rock, breathing slowly, and he could feel she wasn't asleep.

"Why me?" he said.

Nobody answered for a while.

"Why me," he said again. "That's what I want to know. That's all. You were dying. All three of you. There was a field full of people. There were Arn's men and Ulric's men all over the lanes. There was Wat Garrow, and Hob, and half the village. And you came to me. All three. Why?"

Silence.

*You talked,* said Kharrak at last.

Calder waited.

*In the field. After Dag. On the ground. Dying. You talked.* A long breath, out over the dark forest. *Nobody talks to a dying dragon. They run. Or watch. Or wait. You talked. Hey, you said. Hey. I know. I know, lad.* He was quiet. *Like Dag. When I was hatched. Like Dag said.*

Calder waited.

*You said come here,* said Lisska, against his legs. *That's why. I was by the hedge and Imra was… and everything was… and nobody said anything. Everybody was shouting but nobody said anything. And you said come here. Come on, little one. I'm here.* She tucked her head in tighter against his knee. *So I came. Wouldn't you? If someone said that?*

"Anybody could have said that."

*Nobody did.*

He looked at the back of the cave, at the great shape against the rock.

"Aurveth?"

When she answered at last, her voice was slower even than usual, as if each word were a stone she was lifting up out of deep water.

*I have been asking myself the same question,* she said, *every hour since it happened, and I have no answer to give you. I will tell you what I can. It is not much.*

*When Sigrun died, I felt it go. The way I felt Ragnald go, sixty years ago, from the other side of the mountain. It is like being torn in half down the middle, slowly, and then, after, there is the dark. The withering. You feel yourself start to go out, from the edges in, like a coal. I have felt it begin twice before, and both times it stopped, and no one has ever been able to tell me why.*

*And this time, as I went out, there was a pull.*

*I do not know how else to say it. It was like falling toward a fire in the cold. Not choosing. I did not choose you. I did not look down that field and think: that one. I did not think at all. I was dying, and I was cold, and there was a warmth at the bottom of the field, and I fell toward it.*

*You were open,* she said. *That is the nearest I can come. Most people are shut. Like a house at night. You can knock. A rider and a beast, when they bond, it is a long knocking, years, sometimes, and then one day a door opens a little, on both sides. But you were open. Like a door standing wide in a storm. Kharrak was already through it. And I fell toward it. And then Lisska.*

"Why?"

*I don't know.*

"Why was I open?"

*I don't know, Calder.* It was the first time she'd ever said his name. *I have been bonded three times before. I have seen a hundred bondings in the hold, and heard of a thousand. I have never heard of anything like this. No one has ever held more than one. No one has ever been open like that, to three, all at once, with three deaths coming through. It should have killed you. I think it nearly did. I think you were on your way to dying in that field when the old thing on the island roared, and something about that sound brought you back, and I do not know what, and I do not know why.*

The cave was very quiet.

*I am more than three hundred years old,* said Aurveth. *And I do not know.*

---

He lay awake after that.

"Your first," he said, at last, into the dark. "Your first rider. Before Ragnald. Sigrun said you'd never answer. You told me you've been bonded three times." He turned his head toward the back of the cave. "Who was it?"

The silence went on so long he thought she wasn't going to answer.

*I don't know,* said Aurveth.

He waited.

*That is the truth. I don't know. I have gone quiet for Sigrun, and the hold, and everyone who has ever asked. That was easier than the truth. The truth is that I can't.* Her voice had changed. It was still slow. But there was something under it that he'd never heard there before, and it took him a moment to know what it was. *I remember that there was one. I remember the bond, the shape of it, the warmth of it. It felt a long time. It felt longer than Ragnald and Sigrun together, and I cannot tell any more whether it was. I remember that he lived before the Clan was the Clan. Before the hold. Before the caves. I remember that we went along a road together, a great road, with stones in it, and over a bridge.*

*And I don't remember his name. Or his face. Or how he died, or when, or where. I reach for him and he goes down, like a stone in Coldtarn. Further every year.*

Fear. That was what was under her voice. The oldest thing he knew, three hundred years old, the biggest and slowest and calmest creature in the Marches, lying in the dark at the back of a cave on Crow Scar and afraid.

*I am forgetting him,* she said. *I think I have been forgetting him for a long time. And I think, since the field, since you, since that roar across the river, I am beginning to remember. A little. Pieces. The road. The bridge.* A long breath. *And I think whatever I am remembering is something I was meant to forget.*

Nobody said anything.

Against his legs, Lisska had gone very still. In the mouth of the cave, Kharrak had lifted his head off the ledge and turned it, and was looking back into the dark.

"The bridge," said Calder. "What bridge?"

*I don't know,* said Aurveth. *Yet.*

He lay on his back on the cold floor of the cave and looked up into the dark and thought about a road with stones in it, and a bridge, and an island in a river with the stumps of old piers drowned round it, and a girl in river grey sitting on a stream bank, not doing anything at all.

*Like robbing it,* Rue had said.
