---

His father had a rule for fire and a rule for men, and he said they were the same rule. *Don't fight it. Move what it wants.*

His father had never had three dragons. Calder thought, afterwards, that it might have changed his mind.

---

Lisska saw it first. Of course she did.

She was a long way out. Five leagues, she said afterwards, down over the reed beds along the river, where she'd gone to look at the tall white birds standing on one leg in the water. Too far for her voice to carry. But he felt her, through the green mark, suddenly, in the middle of the night: a jolt, a spike of fear so sharp it woke him on the floor of the cave with his heart going.

Then her voice, faint, coming in from very far off, like someone shouting from the bottom of a well, getting louder.

*Fire. Fire. Fire. Your village. Your village is on fire.*

He was on his feet before he knew it.

*Wren,* she said. Nearer now. Coming fast. *Is Wren there? Is she? Is it your house? I can't see. I can see it from the marsh. It's all fire.*

Kharrak was up. Aurveth's head had come round off the floor of the cave, and the one eye was open.

"Is it the house?" Calder said. "Lisska. Is it the house?"

*I don't know. I don't know. I'm coming. I'm nearly there. I'll look.*

He went to Aurveth. She'd already stretched out her neck along the floor of the cave toward him.

*Up,* she said.

---

They went off the ledge into the dark, the three of them, him on Aurveth's neck and Kharrak beside, and went west over the Fellwood faster than he'd ever gone. Aurveth didn't climb. She went low over the treetops, so low the wind of her wings tore the leaves off, beating hard, with her scarred back pulling and pulling under him. He felt it. He didn't care. Neither did she.

Before they were halfway, he saw the glow.

It was in the sky ahead, low down, beyond the edge of the forest and the shoulder of the hill. Orange. The underside of the clouds lit up like the inside of an oven. He'd seen it before. The night of the mill. He'd stood at the byre door with Bramble's halter in his hand and seen the sky go that colour.

*It's not your house,* said Lisska. Her voice was close now, sharp, clear. *It's not. I'm over it. Your house is dark. It's up the hill, it's all dark, there's no fire there. It's the lanes. It's the village. The bottom of the village. The houses by the mill. And the farm with the red cows. And men. Lots of men. Fighting. Two lots. Grey and grey.*

He didn't let his breath out. He couldn't.

*There's a light in your house,* said Lisska. *In the window. A little one. Someone's there.* A pause. *Someone's at the window. A little one. It's Wren. It's Wren! She's at the window! She's all right!*

He let his breath out.

*She's looking at the fire,* said Lisska. *Your mother's pulling her away from the window. She's all right. She's all right.*

*Then we will keep her so,* said Aurveth, under him, and beat her great wings, and went faster.

---

They came over the shoulder of the hill and saw it.

The whole bottom of the valley was burning. The lanes by the mill, where the black heap of the mill still stood from last time. The row of cottages along the beck. The Wheel, or what was left of it. Along the lane to the north, the Garrows' farm, house and byre and barn and stacks, all going up together in one great roaring sheet of yellow, with the smoke going up off it black into the orange sky.

In the lanes, in the light of it, men.

He could see them from the air, small and black against the fire, like ants on a burning log. Horses rearing. Swords. Axes. Two lots, Lisska had said, and he could see it: men coming down the lane from the ford, from the Drover's Way, riders, forty or more. Men already in the village, on foot, in among the houses, with torches. Meeting in the middle. At the green. Round the well. In the light of the burning houses.

Grey and grey. One lot with red in their armbands. One without.

*Ulric's,* said Kharrak, beside him, low. The red tide was coming up in him already; Calder felt it through the mark, hot, rising. *Ulric's, with the torches. Burning. Arn's coming down on them from the Way. Both. Both of them. In your village. Again.*

"I know."

*Let me…*

"No. Not men. You don't burn men. You hear me? Not one."

*They're burning the village…*

"I know what they're doing. Listen to me." He held on to the ridge on Aurveth's neck and made himself think, the way his father had taught him. About the fire, not the men. *Don't fight it. Move what it wants.*

He looked at the valley spread out below him. At the fire. Where it was, and where it was going.

The wind was from the west. Off the river. Pushing the fire east, up the valley, along the lane, toward the Garrows' stubble field, which was dry, which hadn't been ploughed yet, which ran up the hill toward the Ashby farm and the top fields and, past them, over the brow, the Fenn farm. His house. With the light in the window.

"Kharrak," he said. "The Garrows' field. Above their farm. The stubble. Burn it."

*What?*

"Burn it. A line. Right across the top of it, from the hedge to the beck. Before the fire gets there. Burn a strip so wide it can't jump. Then there's nothing for it to eat. It'll come up the field and hit the burned strip and stop." He heard his own voice. It sounded like his father. "Burn the field, not the men. Go."

There was a moment. A long one.

Kharrak turned, and went down.

Calder felt him go. Felt the red tide in him, the wanting, the *let me, let me,* turn. Like a river turned by a weir. Not stopping; going somewhere else. Kharrak went down over the Garrows' farm low and fast, over the burning house, through the smoke, and up the slope above it, over the stubble, and opened his mouth.

The fire came out of him in a long straight sheet, yellow-white, roaring, and went down across the top of the Garrows' field from the hedge to the beck in a single line, so bright that the whole slope lit up like noon. The stubble went up. It went up in a wall, crackling, and the wind took it and pushed it, not back down the field toward the farm, but up, east, into nothing, into the bare ploughed ground of the Ashby top field where there was nothing left to burn, and burned out, and left behind it a long black strip of ash across the top of the field, smoking, wider than the lane.

Below it, the fire from the Garrows' farm came up the field on the wind, through the stubble, and reached the black strip.

And stopped.

He felt Kharrak feel it. Through the mark. The fire coming up, and stopping. Something in the great rust-red mind went very still and very surprised.

*Oh,* said Kharrak.

---

*There's children,* said Lisska. *On a roof. In the lane by the beck. The little houses. The one in the middle's burning at the back. They're on the front. Three. On the roof. They can't get down. The front's burning now. Can I? Can I? Please?*

"Go."

She went. He watched her from Aurveth's neck, a small green shape going down through the smoke over the row of cottages along the beck, quick, slipping, the way she slipped between trees in the Fellwood at night. He saw the roof. Thatch, black and smoking at the back, with yellow coming up through it, and three small figures at the front of it, at the ridge, clinging, with the smoke going over them.

Lisska came down over the roof, and hung there, beating, her wings throwing the smoke away, and put out her forefeet.

He saw the first child grab. A boy, bigger than the others. He saw him grab one of the green forelegs and hang on, and Lisska lift, and swing out over the lane, and drop down, and let him go onto the grass by the beck, and go back up. Twice more. A girl. Another girl, the smallest, who wouldn't let go of the ridge, and Lisska hung there over the burning roof and put her head down to her and Calder heard her, in his head, saying *please, please, it's all right, it's me, I'm Wren's, please,* and the child let go and grabbed.

The roof fell in a moment after. He saw the ridge go, and the thatch, and the sparks go up in a great spout. Lisska was already down by the beck with the third child, setting her down on the grass with her brother and sister, and standing over them, wings half spread, the way a hen stands over chicks.

*I did it,* she said. *Did I? I did. Three. I did it.*

"You did it."

*They were frightened of me. And then they weren't.*

---

Aurveth went down into the lanes.

She didn't land. She came down low over the burning houses along the mill lane with her great wings spread, and she began to beat them. Slowly. Hard. Not to fly. To push. Every beat sent a wind down the lane like a winter storm, and the fire, which had been going from house to house on the west wind, bent away from it. Away from the lane. Away from the houses that hadn't caught yet. Back onto the ones that were already gone, and the black heap of the mill, and the bare river shore, where there was nothing left for it.

He felt what it cost her. The scars down her back were pulling with every stroke, and he felt it in his own spine, a long line of fire, so that he gasped and held on to the ridge with his teeth set. She didn't stop.

*Move what it wants,* she said, through her teeth, or what she had for teeth. *Your father was a wise man.*

Below them, in the light of the fires, the men were looking up.

---

They'd stopped fighting, as a crowd goes quiet at a fair when someone shouts. A few at a time, all over the green and the lanes and the bottom of the Drover's Way, they'd looked up, and seen what was in the air over them. A great bronze dragon beating the fire back off the houses. A rust-red one going over the Garrows' field in a sheet of flame. A small green one carrying children off a roof.

And a man on the bronze one's neck.

Calder saw them see him. He saw their faces turn up in the firelight, white, Ulric's men and Arn's men together, and the swords stop, and the axes come down.

*Now,* said Kharrak. He was coming back from the field, low, fast, over the green. *Now. They're all there. Both lots. Together. Look at them. Let me…*

"Land," said Calder. "Don't burn. Land. In the middle. All three of us."

*Why?*

"Because they're more afraid of you than they are of each other," said Calder. "Land."

They landed.

Kharrak first, in the middle of the green, by the well, so hard the ground shook and the men nearest were thrown off their feet. Then Aurveth, on the far side, by the burning Wheel, with her wings still half spread and the fire behind her and her one eye blazing gold. Then Lisska, last, on the roof of the well-house, small and green and furious, with her head down and her wings up and a sound coming out of her like a kettle about to burst.

Calder slid down off Aurveth's neck onto the trampled grass of the green, in the middle of forty of Ulric's men and fifty of Arn's, with three dragons round him, and stood there.

Nobody moved.

He could see Sten Raske. By the well, with his red beard black with soot and a sword in his hand, staring at Kharrak's head, which was a pace from his face, with the jaws a little open and the smoke coming out of them. He could see Gorm Askell, on a horse at the bottom of the Way, with his long sour face gone the colour of ash. He could see men he didn't know, all round the green, in grey with red thread and grey without, with their weapons in their hands and their mouths open.

"Go home," said Calder.

Nobody moved.

"Go *home,*" said Calder. He heard his own voice go out across the green. It didn't sound like him. It sounded like something much bigger. "All of you. Ulric's and Arn's. Back up the Way and back along the river lane. Now. Tonight. And tell them both. Tell Arn and tell Ulric. There's nothing here for them. There's no grain. There's no silver. There's no men. There's nobody in Harrow's End who's on anybody's side." He looked at Sten Raske. "There's just us."

Through the three marks on his chest, he felt the three of them, all at once, lift their heads.

Kharrak opened his jaws and roared, without fire. The sound went out across the green and the lanes and the burning houses like a wall falling, and the horses screamed and bolted, and the men nearest went down on their knees with their hands over their ears.

When it stopped, they ran.

Both lots. Ulric's men down the lane to the water and away along the river lane, on foot, throwing their torches down as they went. Arn's men back up the Drover's Way, the riders wheeling and spurring, Gorm Askell at the front. Running. All of them. Away from the green, away from the village, away from three dragons and a farmer, into the dark.

Calder stood in the middle of the green and watched them go.

*They ran,* said Lisska, from the roof of the well-house. Wonderingly. *They all ran. Both lots. Did you see?*

"I saw."

*We didn't burn anybody.*

"No."

*Kharrak didn't burn anybody.*

*Don't,* said Kharrak.

---

Not all of them ran.

He heard it before he saw it. Down the lane by the beck, past the row of cottages, beyond the place where Lisska had set the children down. A scream. A boy's.

He ran.

He didn't think. He was across the green and into the lane and down it on his bad leg before he knew he'd moved, through the smoke, past the burning cottages, and there they were. At the corner by the Garrows' gate, where the lane went down to the beck. Two of Ulric's men who hadn't run. One holding a horse. The other with a boy.

It was Wat Garrow's boy. Davy. Ten years old. Calder had taught him to tickle trout in the beck two summers ago. The man had him by the hair, bent back, and a knife at his throat, and he was shouting at the burning Garrow farmhouse, at the doorway, where Wat Garrow stood with a pitchfork in his hands and his face streaked black.

"…your silver, Garrow, Arn's silver, where is it, you sold to the hold, where's the silver he gave you, or I'll…"

Calder felt it come up.

It came up through the rust-red mark first, hot and huge, Kharrak's, the red tide, all the rage that had been turned like a river at a weir and had nowhere to go. Then through the others. Aurveth's, slow and deep and terrible, the rage of a thing that had been beating fire off houses with her torn back for an hour. Lisska's, high and thin and white-hot, *that's a child, that's a child, that's like Wren.* And under all three, his own. The bottom of it. *Teo. The old woman in her house. The ford. Hob in the ashes. Wren at the window.*

It came up all at once, all four, and filled him to the top, and went over.

There was a rail lying in the lane. Off the Garrows' fence, where a horse had gone through it. Oak, as long as he was tall. He picked it up.

He didn't remember crossing the lane. He remembered the man turning, too late, with the knife coming away from the boy's throat. He remembered the rail going round. He remembered the sound it made on the man's shoulder, and the man going down, and the knife going spinning off into the dark, and the boy running, screaming, to his father in the doorway.

And then he remembered the rail going up again. And down.

And up. And down. On the man on the ground, who had his arms over his head, who was screaming, who was trying to crawl away. He couldn't stop. He didn't want to stop. The red was in his eyes and his mouth and his hands, and it was good, it was so good, it was the best thing he'd ever felt, it was simple and hot and clean and he wanted to go on doing it forever. *Burn them. Burn them all. Every one. Who did it. Who. Find them. Burn.*

*Calder.*

Up. Down.

*Calder. Please.*

A voice. Small. Very small. In the middle of all the red, like a candle in a furnace.

*You're frightening me.*

He stopped.

He stood in the lane with the oak rail up over his head, in both hands, and the man on the ground under him, curled up, with his arms over his head and blood on them, sobbing. He stood there. The red was still in him. It was going round and round in him like a dog in a pen. He could feel it wanting.

*Please,* said Lisska. *You're frightening me. Please stop. Please. You're not you. You're like Kharrak. You're like Kharrak when he wanted to burn the hold. Please. I don't like it. Please be you.*

He put the rail down.

He didn't drop it. He put it down. He bent over and laid it on the ground in the lane, beside the man, very carefully, as if it might go off. He stood up and stepped back from it.

The man on the ground lay where he was.

The other one, the one with the horse, had already gone. Calder heard hooves going away down the lane toward the beck and the river lane.

He stood there. He was shaking. His hands were shaking. He looked at them and they didn't look like his.

*That was mine,* said Kharrak, very low, somewhere behind him on the green. *That was mine, in you. I felt it go in. I'm sorry. I'm…*

*It was all of ours,* said Aurveth. *And his. All four. He was full, and we filled him more, and it went over.* A long breath. *We must be careful. We must be very careful. With him. We are too many for one man to hold, and we are all very angry.*

The man on the ground groaned and rolled onto his side.

He was alive. His arm was wrong, and his face was a mess, and there was blood in the dust of the lane. But he was alive, and breathing, and after a while he got up onto his knees and looked up at Calder with his eyes huge and white in his black face. He got up and ran, stumbling, holding his arm, away down the lane after his friend.

Calder let him go.

---

Hob's house was burning when he came back up the lane.

It stood at the corner of the green, by the burned Wheel, a little stone house with a slate roof that Hob had built for his wife the year they married, and she was dead ten years now and he lived in it alone. The fire from the Wheel had gone into the back of it. The roof was going. Smoke was pouring out of the upstairs window. At the window, someone was shouting.

Calder stood on the green and looked at it.

He thought about Hob at the gate with the elders. *I lost my mill, Calder. And you came out of it with three dragons.* He thought about Hob raking the ashes for nails, and saying *all right* in a strange flat voice. He thought about a man in a good coat standing in the black heap of the mill and holding out a bag that smelled cold.

*He sold you,* said Kharrak. *He sold Wren. He'd have put her in the cart and driven her to them.*

"I know."

*Leave him.*

Calder stood on the green.

He went into the house.

The door was open. The ground floor was full of smoke, thick and grey and stinking, and there was fire at the back, in the kitchen, roaring. He put his sleeve over his face and went through it, bent double, to the stair, and up. The stair was hot under his hands. At the top, in the little room under the slates, Hob was on his knees at the window with his face out of it, coughing, and the room behind him was full of smoke and the roof above was beginning to glow.

Calder got him by the collar.

Hob looked round. His face was black and his eyes were streaming, and he saw who it was. And something went across his face that Calder would never forget. Not fear. Not shame. Something like a man looking down a well and seeing, at the bottom, his own face looking back.

"Come on," said Calder.

He got Hob's arm over his shoulder and dragged him. Down the stair, through the smoke, through the burning ground floor, out of the door onto the green, and let him go, and Hob went down on his hands and knees on the grass, coughing, retching, and behind them the roof of the little stone house fell in with a great rush of sparks.

Calder stood over him, breathing hard.

Hob didn't look up. He knelt on the grass with his head down and his hands flat on the ground and coughed until he stopped coughing. He stayed there. He didn't say thank you, or sorry, or anything at all. He just knelt there on the green with his face to the ground, in the light of his own house burning.

Calder turned and walked away.

---

The fires burned down toward dawn. Most of the bottom of the village was gone: the cottages by the mill, the Wheel, Hob's house, the Garrows' farm. The rest stood. The Ashby farm, behind its black strip of burned stubble. The top fields. The shrine at the crossroads. The houses along the upper lane.

The Fenn farm, up the hill, dark, with a light in the window.

People came out into the lanes. Slowly. From their cellars and their ditches and the back of the beck pasture where they'd hidden. They stood in the grey light among the smoking ruins and looked at what was left. They looked at him. Standing on the green, with three dragons round him.

Some of them came up to him.

Wat Garrow came first, with Davy holding his hand, and Tessa behind him with the baby. He stood in front of Calder with his face working, and put out his hand. Calder took it. Wat Garrow didn't say anything. He just held Calder's hand, hard, for a long moment, and let go, and went away with his family down the lane to look at what was left of his farm. Over the black gap where the byre door had been, the cow-charm his father had nailed up the year Calder was born still hung from its nail, rowan and red thread, scorched, turning a little in the smoke.

The children Lisska had carried off the roof came, with their mother. The smallest girl, the one who wouldn't let go of the ridge, came right up to Lisska where she lay on the green and stood in front of her with her thumb in her mouth and stared. Lisska put her head down until it was level with the child's face, carefully, and stayed very still.

*She's like Wren,* said Lisska. *Isn't she? A bit. Is she?*

"A bit," said Calder.

Old Garrow came, on his stick, and looked at him, and nodded once, and went away.

The rest didn't come.

He saw them, standing at the edges of the green, in the lanes, at their doors, keeping their distance. Watching him. Watching the dragons. Their faces in the grey light were the faces he'd seen at the hedge on the morning after the mill. Frightened. More frightened now than they'd been then.

*Why are they afraid?* said Lisska. *We helped. Didn't we? We saved them. Why are they afraid?*

"Because we could have done the other thing," said Calder.

---

He looked across the river as the sun came up.

He hadn't thought to, in the night. He hadn't thought of anything but the fire. But standing on the green in the grey light, with the smoke going up all round him, he looked out past the black heap of the mill and the river shore, across the water, to the Hearthmere bank.

There were knights there.

A line of them. At the water's edge on the far bank, opposite the village, where the Greyhold road came along the river. Twenty, maybe. Mounted, most of them on horses, and a greyhart or two among them, white and tall with their antlers spread, standing in a long line along the bank in the dawn with lanterns on poles beside them, Hearthmere lanterns that wanted no oil, still burning white and steady after the whole night, and the knights in grey sitting very still.

Watching.

They'd been there all night, he thought. They'd come along the road from Greyhold when the fire went up, and stopped at the water, and sat there on their horses, with their lanterns, and watched Harrow's End burn. Watched the fighting in the lanes. Watched the dragons come down out of the sky.

And hadn't crossed.

He stood on the green and looked at them across the river, and they looked back at him. The light came up. The lanterns went pale. Nobody moved.

*Why don't they come?* said Lisska. *They're knights. Imra said knights are for helping. Why don't they come over?*

"Because it's the river," said Calder.

*So?*

He didn't have an answer. He watched them sit there, in their long grey line, on the far bank, in the dawn. One of them, in the middle of the line, on a big grey horse, lifted a hand. Just lifted it. Not a wave. Something else. As if to say: *we see you.*

They turned, all together, and rode away along the River Road toward Greyhold, and the bank was empty.

Calder turned round and walked up the hill toward his mother's house, and the light in the window, and Wren.
