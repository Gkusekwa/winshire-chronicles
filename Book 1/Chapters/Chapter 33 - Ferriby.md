---

They put the second-years on the River Road three days after Holloway burned.

Not as knights. As horse-holders. Runners. Lantern-carriers. Somebody to stand at the back of the patrol with the spare mounts and gallop for help if it went wrong. Greyhold had doubled the patrols on the Hearthmere bank, every tower from Millbrook to Northwatch, and there weren't enough passed knights to ride them all. So the instructors took who they could get. Pell took Maren.

"You can ride," he said, in the yard, at dawn, while Grieve stood by the Long Barn with his arms folded and looked disgusted. "And your ribs are good enough. And I want you where I can see you." He looked at her. "Not on that island."

She didn't argue.

There were eight of them. Pell, at the front, on his old bay. Two passed knights from the gate, Callis and Wynn, both bonded, both with hounds trotting at their stirrups. Haverly, in his new grey cloak, with Kesh hooded on his fist. Ide Darrow, the quiet one from Maren's own year, with Bracken loping beside her pony, brindled and silent. Tobin, unbonded and sour, on a cob. Maren on Turnip, at the back, with the spare horses on a string and the dried turnip still knocking on Turnip's bridle where someone had tied it years ago and nobody had ever taken it off.

They rode the River Road north from Greyhold in the grey of the morning, past the landing, past the ferry-house where her mother was at the guide rope and lifted one hand and didn't wave, past the stretch of bank where the island lay out in the river with its broken arches and its stump of a tower. She looked at it as they went by. She couldn't help it. Somewhere on the west shore, under the trees, at the end of a stream, something was lying in the long grass with a collar on its neck.

She hadn't been in eight days. She'd wanted to every one of them.

"Ashdown," said Pell, without turning round.

She looked at the road.

---

They rode all day. North, to Redwatch, the tower on the bluff where Ottery had his post, and on along the bank, stopping at every hamlet and farm to ask. *Anything? Strangers? Boats?* And every hamlet and every farm said the same. *Nothing. Not yet.* And looked at the river, and then at the knights, with a look in their faces that Maren had never seen on Hearthmere faces before, though she'd seen it all her life on the Saltreach bank. The look of people who'd started to count the hours till dark.

At Redwatch they ate. Ottery came down from the tower and stood by Turnip's head while Maren ate her bread in the saddle, and gave the turnip on the bridle a pat. They rode on, inland, away from the river, to the last two places on Pell's list before dusk.

Ferriby was the second.

---

It was a little place. A dozen houses of grey stone with slate roofs, round a green with a pump. A chapel no bigger than a byre. A smithy at the bottom of the green with its doors open and the forge going. And beyond the smithy, a lane, going down between hedges to a creek: a slow brown creek with reeds along it that wound away east through the fields to the Wend, a league off. At the bottom of the lane, where the creek widened, there was a little stone landing, old, mossy, with a punt tied to it.

They had beasts in Ferriby. That was why it was on Pell's list. Old Hamm, who farmed the land along the creek, had an ox-beast, bonded, as old as he was, that he still ploughed with. The smith had a hound. And in a paddock behind the chapel, the crown kept a breeding pair of greyharts: a buck and a doe, white, nine feet at the shoulder, for the war-stables at Hallowmere. They'd been there since Maren was a child. The whole river knew about them.

They got there at dusk. The light was going grey and blue over the fields, and the smoke from the chimneys was going straight up in the still cold air, and somewhere a woman was calling children in. Pell rode into the green and stopped by the pump, and the smith came out of his forge, wiping his hands, and said *nothing, Instructor, not yet, thank the gods,* and Pell nodded and turned his bay to go.

Bracken lifted his head.

---

Maren saw it. She was at the back of the patrol, with the spare horses, by the chapel wall, and she was looking at the hound because she'd been mucking out his stall every morning for five weeks and knew every line of him. She saw his head come up. She saw his torn ear go flat. She saw his whole rough brindled body go still, the way it went still in the Long Barn when Captain Thorne's boar moved in its stall.

He was looking down the lane. Toward the creek.

Ide Darrow saw it too. Maren saw her face change.

"Instructor," said Ide. Very quietly. The first word Maren had ever heard her say out loud.

Pell turned his head.

From down the lane, from the creek, in the dusk, there came a sound. Faint. Wood on stone. Then another. Then a voice, low, cut off.

Boats.

---

They came up the lane out of the dusk like water coming up through a floor.

That was what it was like. No charge, no rush. One moment the lane was empty, grey, with the hedges on either side going dark. And the next there were men in it. Coming up from the creek, from the landing, in twos and threes, fast and quiet. Masked. Dark cloth over their faces, no colours, the way the raiders had been at Upper Rush, the way the roadblock men hadn't been. Thirty of them, perhaps. More. Coming up the lane and over the hedges and into the green from both sides at once.

And not a single one of them went for the patrol.

They didn't attack. They went round. They came up the lane and split, without a word, without a pause, as if they'd done it a hundred times: some to the left, behind the chapel, toward the greyhart paddock; some to the right, along the creek, toward old Hamm's farm; some straight across the green to the smithy. And four or five, only four or five, came and stood in the middle of the green between the patrol and everything else, with their swords out and their feet set, and did nothing at all.

They were in the way. That was all. They were standing exactly where the patrol would have to go.

"*Callis! Wynn! The paddock!*" Pell had his sword out. "*Haverly, the farm! Darrow, with me!*"

They went. Callis and Wynn spurred round the chapel toward the paddock with their hounds racing. Haverly flung up his fist and Kesh went off it, unhooded, screaming, toward the creek. Pell rode at the four men in the middle of the green, with Ide Darrow and Bracken at his side.

Maren sat on Turnip at the back by the chapel wall with the spare horses on their string, the way she'd been told to, and watched.

That was her job. The horse-holder. The one who watched, and galloped for help. So she watched. And because she watched, she saw the thing nobody else saw.

---

There was a man on the roof of the smithy.

He was standing on the ridge. Right up on the top, on the slates, at the end nearest the green, with his feet apart and his hands loose at his sides, as easy as if he were standing in a field. He wasn't masked. He wore a plain dark coat, buttoned, and no hat. And beside him, crouched on the ridge with one hand on the slates to steady himself, there was a boy. A thin boy of twelve or so, with a whistle on a cord round his neck.

The man wasn't doing anything. That was what she noticed first. Everyone else in Ferriby was moving, running, shouting. Pell's bay was rearing in the green as one of the four men in the middle caught at its bridle. Bracken had a raider down by the pump with his teeth in the man's arm. Kesh was stooping on something by the creek, screaming. Callis was shouting by the paddock. The smith had come out of his forge with a hammer. Somewhere a woman was screaming over and over, and a child.

And the man on the roof was standing still, looking at it all.

He lifted one hand. Just a little. Two fingers. And pointed. Toward the paddock.

The boy put the whistle to his mouth and blew. Two short notes.

Behind the chapel, the raiders at the paddock, who'd been fighting Callis and Wynn at the gate, broke off. All at once. And ran. Not away. Sideways. Along the paddock fence, toward the back of old Hamm's farm, where the other raiders were. Callis and Wynn went after them, the way anyone would, the way the hounds made them. And as they went, from behind the chapel, where nobody was now, three more raiders who'd been lying in the ditch came up out of it and went over the paddock fence, quiet, and had the greyharts' halters in their hands before Callis had even turned round.

The man on the roof lowered his hand.

He lifted it again. Three fingers. Toward the green.

The boy blew. One long note.

The four men in the middle of the green, who'd been holding Pell, gave way. Stepped back, and back, and opened, like a gate swinging. And Pell's bay went through the gap, the way a horse goes through a gap, toward the smithy. And the moment Pell was through, the four closed again, behind him, between him and Ide Darrow. And Pell was on the wrong side of them. Alone. At the smithy door. With his back to the forge.

Maren sat on Turnip by the chapel wall with her mouth open.

*It's a game,* she thought. *It's a game of stones.*

Pell taught it to the first-years every winter, in the hall, by the fire: nineteen lines by nineteen, black stones and white, the only game ever made, he said, that taught you how to lose ground and win. You didn't take the other player's stones by attacking them. You took them by putting your own where theirs needed to go, quietly, one at a time, until they had nowhere left to go. The best players, Pell said, never seemed to be doing anything at all. She'd never been any good at it. She always went straight for the other player's stones.

The man on the roof was playing them. All of them. The patrol and the raiders and the whole of Ferriby. Putting his pieces where theirs needed to go. Quietly. One at a time. Never seeming to do anything at all. Every time one of them moved, he'd already moved somewhere they'd have to go next.

He hadn't taken a single stone. He'd just made sure there was nowhere for anyone to go.

---

She looked at his face.

The smithy roof was lit from below by the forge, through the open doors, and by a barn that had begun to burn at the back of old Hamm's farm, and his face was in the light of both. Long. Pale. Clean-shaven. Dark hair cut short. Neither young nor old. A still, patient face, watching.

She knew it.

It was the face from the roadblock. The tall thin man by the wayside shrine on the River Road, two months and a lifetime ago, on her first patrol. The one who decided, while the square man talked. The one who'd looked at Turnip with his dried turnip on the bridle, and at her practice sword, and at the empty place at her shoulder where a beast should have been, and said, so only she could hear: *They'll never give you a horse worth riding, you know.*

*I know where they put you. I know why.*

She sat on Turnip and stared at him, and he stood on the ridge of the smithy roof with the fire under him, and lifted two fingers, and the boy blew, and somewhere along the creek the raiders who'd been fighting Haverly gave way all at once and let him through to the landing, where there was nothing left to save, because the barges were already loaded.

---

It was over in a quarter of an hour.

She'd never have believed it. She'd have said, if anyone had asked her, that a raid on a hamlet with eight of Greyhold's people in it would take half the night. It took a quarter of an hour. The man on the roof lifted his hand one last time, all five fingers spread, and the boy blew a long falling note like a curlew. And all over Ferriby the raiders let go of whatever they were fighting and went.

Down the lane. Down the creek. To the landing. Walking fast, not running, the way men walk away from a job that's done. And the patrol, which had been pulled about all over the hamlet for a quarter of an hour, chasing pieces, came back together in the green in ones and twos, bloodied and breathing hard, and found that there was nobody left to fight.

Nothing left to fight for, either. The greyharts were gone. Old Hamm's ox-beast was gone, out of its byre, with old Hamm sitting in the doorway in the straw with his face in his hands, alive. The smith's hound was gone. The barn behind Hamm's farm was burning. Nothing else. No houses. Not one.

They'd come for the beasts. They'd got the beasts. They'd let every person in Ferriby live.

Maren was off Turnip and running before she knew she'd moved.

Down the lane. Toward the creek. Past the hedges, in the dark, with the spare horses left tied to the chapel gate and her practice sword in her hand and her ribs screaming at every step. She didn't know what she was going to do. She was going to see. That was all. She'd seen him on the roof. She was going to see where he went.

She came round the last bend of the lane above the landing and stopped.

The barges were going. Two of them, long and low, flat river barges, the kind that carried flour down the Wend from Millbrook. Pushing off from the old stone landing into the brown creek, with men poling, and on the first one, in the dark, the shapes of beasts. Great white shapes, tall, with antlers. The greyharts, hobbled and blindfolded, standing very still on the deck. And something huge and humped behind them. Old Hamm's ox-beast.

And on the landing, alone, stepping down into the second barge, the man from the roof.

He stopped with one foot on the barge's edge.

He turned his head, and looked up the lane. Straight at her. As if he'd known exactly where she'd be. As if he'd put her there.

"Ashdown," he said.

He didn't raise his voice. He didn't need to. It came up the lane to her in the still cold air, quiet, clear, pleasant.

"I hear you sit with lions," he said.

He stepped down into the barge, and sat, and the polemen pushed off, and the barge went away down the creek into the dark toward the Wend, and the river, and wherever it was going.

She stood in the lane with her practice sword in her hand.

He knew her name.

---

They rode back to Redwatch in the dark.

Nobody talked. Pell rode at the front with a cut over his eye that Wynn had bound with a strip of his shirt. Callis had a slashed arm. Haverly's face was white and he held Kesh on his fist without her hood and stroked her, over and over, with one finger, as if he weren't sure she was there. Ide Darrow rode beside Maren at the back, silent, with Bracken loping at her stirrup, limping a little, with blood on his muzzle that wasn't his.

"He's all right," said Ide, when she saw Maren looking. "It's the raider's. He let go when I told him." She was quiet a moment. "He always lets go when I tell him."

Maren nodded.

"I'd have let him keep going," said Ide. Very low. "If it'd been me on the ground. I'd have let him."

At Redwatch, Ottery came down from the tower with a lantern and looked at their faces and didn't ask.

Maren asked him.

She got down off Turnip in the yard at the foot of the tower and went to him while the others were seeing to the horses, and stood close, and said, low: "The boats."

Ottery looked at her.

"There were barges," she said. "Two. On the Wend. Up the creek from Ferriby and back down again, with greyharts on the deck. In the dark." She looked at him. "Where were the boats?"

"In," Ottery said at last. "All of them. Every river-watch boat from here to Millbrook. Ordered in at dusk."

"Who by?"

"A rider." Ottery's voice was very flat. "Came along the bank at dusk on a grey horse. Stopped at every post. Showed a paper. Greyhold's seal on it. Orders: all boats in, all crews to the towers, the Captain's compliments, there's been word of raiders on the road and every man's wanted on land." He looked at her. "Same as the night of Upper Rush. Same as before."

"Same rider?"

"Couldn't say. Didn't see his face. Hood up." Ottery spat. "But I'll tell you what I can say. Twice now. Twice, a rider with Greyhold's seal has cleared this river on the night a barge needed it clear." He lifted the lantern and looked up at the tower above them, and then away down the dark bank toward Greyhold, three leagues off. "And I've been on this river forty years, and I've never once had an order from Greyhold that I didn't know the hand of. And these, I don't."

---

There was a scout at Redwatch that night.

A Hearthmere man, one of the licensed ones, who went over the river under the crown's paper to watch the Clan's war and came back to report. Lean and brown, in Saltreach clothes, with a beard he'd grown to look the part. He was sitting by the fire in the guardroom at the foot of the tower when they came in, eating stew, and he looked up at Pell's eye and Callis's arm and whistled.

"Ferriby," said Pell, and sat down.

The scout nodded. He'd heard. Everyone had heard.

"Bad week," he said. "On both banks." He went on eating. "You should hear what's happening over there. The Varrs'll have the whole of the west side burned out by midwinter. Ulric's men burned three more farms on the river lane two nights back. Arn's burned two of theirs the night after. I've stopped counting." He wiped his mouth. "And that's before you get to the dragons."

The guardroom went quiet.

"What dragons?" said the sergeant of the tower, a heavy man called Brask, by the fire. He was grinning. "The farmer's dragons? The barley man's?"

"Laugh if you like," said the scout. "I saw it. A week ago. I was in a ditch on the far side of Harrow's End, the night it went up the second time. I saw them come down. Out of the dark. Three of them. The big bronze one, the Clan's old war-leader's beast, and the red and the green. And fire." He put his spoon down. "Not fire on the village. Fire on the field above it. A line of it, across the stubble, so the village fire couldn't come up. And the big one fanning the flames back off the houses with her wings. And they landed on the green in the middle of the fighting, and the fighting stopped." He looked round the guardroom. "And there was a man on the bronze one's neck. A farmer. I saw him get down. I saw him stand on the green in the middle of ninety men and tell them to go home. And they went."

Brask laughed out loud.

"A farmer," he said. "On a dragon. Telling the Varrs to go home. Gods, you've been over there too long, man. You'll be telling us next he can walk on the river."

A few of the others laughed too. The scout shrugged and picked up his spoon.

Pell didn't laugh.

Maren looked at him. He was sitting by the fire with the bloody strip of Wynn's shirt round his head, and he was looking at the scout, and his face was very still.

Haverly didn't laugh either. He was looking at the floor.

Maren sat on the bench by the door with her ribs aching and her practice sword across her knees and thought about two men.

A tall thin one on a smithy roof with a boy and a whistle, playing a hamlet like a game of stones. Who'd looked up the lane at her in the dark and said her name.

And a farmer, on a dragon, coming down out of the dark onto a burning green with his coat flying, while twenty knights sat their horses on the other bank and watched.

*The thin man's playing a game. Everybody's a stone to him. The patrol. The raiders. Ferriby. Me.*

*But not the farmer. The farmer isn't on his board. The farmer just came down out of the sky and knocked the board over.*

*I hope he doesn't know my name too.*

Then, quite clearly, the other thing: *I'd like to meet him.*

She didn't know where that came from either.
