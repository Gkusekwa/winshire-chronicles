She wrote the report the night they rode back into Greyhold from Redwatch, by a stub of candle in the dormitory, the way she'd written the one about the roadblock. Fourteen pages. Every raider she'd counted, every signal the boy had blown, every move. The two fingers. The three fingers. The four men in the middle of the green opening like a gate and closing again behind Pell. The greyharts going over the paddock fence. The barges.

The man on the roof.

She wrote what he looked like, and where she'd seen him before. She wrote what he'd said. She wrote it exactly, in the middle of a page, on a line by itself, so nobody could miss it.

*Ashdown. I hear you sit with lions.*

And under it, the other thing. The boats. *All river-watch boats from Redwatch to Millbrook ordered in at dusk by a rider bearing Greyhold's seal. Sergeant Ottery does not know the hand. This is the second time.*

She gave it to Pell in the morning. Pell read it standing in the yard with the strip of Wynn's shirt still round his head. He read it all. He folded it up and looked at her.

"He said your name," he said.

"Yes."

Pell looked at the folded pages.

"I'll take it up myself," he said.

---

Rowan sent for her in the afternoon.

She went up the narrow stair sideways with her shoulder to the wall, and knocked, and went in. Rowan was at her desk under the window with the fourteen pages in front of her, open at the middle, and she didn't look up.

Maren stood in front of the desk and waited.

Rowan read the line in the middle of the page. Then she read it again. Maren watched her eyes go along it, and back, and along it again. *Ashdown. I hear you sit with lions.*

"You'd never spoken to this man," said Rowan.

"Once. At the roadblock. He spoke to me. He didn't know my name then."

"How do you know?"

"He'd have used it. He's the kind who'd use it." Maren thought about it. "He didn't. He just looked at Turnip and my sword and said they'd never give me a horse worth riding."

Rowan was quiet for a while.

"Your name isn't a secret," she said. "Your trial isn't a secret. The whole river knows a Greyhold student failed her trial on the island. The whole river knows she goes back." She looked up, then, for the first time. Her pale eyes were very still. "But the whole river doesn't know her name. The fishermen call you the girl on the island. The ferrymen call you Liss's girl. The beast-sellers, I'm told, call you the lion girl. Not one of them calls you Ashdown."

"No, Captain."

"So someone told him."

Maren said nothing.

Rowan looked at her a moment longer. She bent her head, and picked up her pen, and wrote something in the margin of the fourteenth page, small, beside the line about the boats. Maren couldn't see what.

"Dismissed," said Rowan.

---

Hale had heard. Of course he had. He was sitting at the long table in the archive under the hanging lamp when she came in the next afternoon, with his stick against his chair and the old black book open in front of him, and he looked up and said *Ashdown* in his deep tired voice, and then, before she'd even sat down:

"He said your name."

"Yes."

"On the landing. At Ferriby. By the barge."

"Yes, Warden."

He sat back in his chair and looked at her. She'd never seen him look like that. Not frightened. Something older than frightened. The look of a man who's been waiting a long time for a knock at the door and has just heard it.

"Sit," he said.

She sat.

He turned the pages of the black book with one big finger, not reading them, and stopped, and looked past her, down the long vaulted room, to the far end.

She'd never looked properly at the far end. The archive went back a long way under the north tower, stack after stack of books and boxes and ledgers, sideways through the narrow gaps, into the dark beyond the reach of the lamp. She'd thought it just went on. But she followed his eyes now, and saw that it didn't. It ended. At the very back, past the last stack, there were three stone steps going down. And at the bottom of them, set into the old grey wall of the tower's foundation, there was a door.

Low. Black. Oak, so old it had gone the colour of iron, and bound across with real iron in three great bands. No handle. Only a lock: an iron plate as big as a man's hand, with a keyhole in the middle of it, deep and dark.

Hale was looking at it.

Then he looked back at her, and saw that she'd seen him look, and something in his face closed.

"You wanted to know about the oath," he said.

---

He told her about the oath.

He'd told her about the Watch, weeks before, in the first of the afternoons. The list in the old black book, three hundred and eleven names in the old script, with a mark beside each. The heading at the top, *Watch.* The crown coming to the river three hundred years ago with its charter and its masons and building Greyhold on top of whatever the Watch had been keeping.

He told her the rest of it now.

"There's no date at the top," he said. He turned the black book round on the table so she could see. The first page. The heading, *Watch*, in its square spare letters with their little hooks. And above it, in the same hand, small, a line she hadn't noticed before. "There's no year. No king's name. Nothing to say when it was begun. Only that."

She leaned over it. She traced the letters in her mind, slowly, the way her father had traced the milestone.

"*From the…*" She stopped. Tried again. "*From the closing.*"

"*From the closing,*" said Hale. "That's all. Not *from the founding*. Not *from the coronation of*. *From the closing*." He looked at the line. "I've spent forty years trying to find out what was closed. I've found nothing. Nobody wrote it down. Or if they did, it isn't here."

He turned the pages. Name after name. Three hundred and eleven of them, each in a different hand, she saw now: the same script, but different hands, some neat and some rough, some cramped and some spread out. As if each Watcher had written their own name in the book when their turn came. And beside each name, the same mark. A small square, with a line through it.

"Like a door," said Maren.

Hale's finger stopped on the page.

"I've always thought so," he said, after a moment. "Yes."

He turned to the last page with writing on it. The three hundred and eleventh name. And under it, in a different hand altogether, a newer one, in the ordinary letters of Hearthmere that any student could read, there was a line.

*Given into the keeping of the crown.*

And a year. The year of the charter. The year Greyhold was built. Three hundred years ago.

"After that," said Hale, "there's nothing. The Watch stops. The list stops. And in the first book of the orders, the very first, the founding book, which is upstairs in Rowan's room and which she'll let you read if you ask her nicely and wash your hands, the oath appears. Near enough word for word. *I will keep the watch below, and I will not ask what I keep.* Sworn by every knight of every order, every day, at the noon bell. From that day to this." He closed the black book. "The crown took the Watch's oath, and gave it to the orders, and the Watch went away. And nobody ever wrote down what the watch was."

She looked at the closed book.

"Did they know?" she said. "The first knights. When they swore it. Did they know what they were keeping?"

"Some of them," said Hale. "I think. The first ones. The ones the crown chose." He looked at his hands on the table. "And then they died, and their children swore it, and their children's children. And each time, a little less was told. Until, in the end, nobody was told anything. Just the words. *Keep the watch below.* Said at noon, facing east, toward the old wall, by people who think it's a figure of speech."

"And nobody keeps it now? Not really? It's just words?"

"Of course they keep it," said Hale. "The inner circle keeps a watch nobody else is told…"

He stopped.

---

The archive was very quiet. Somewhere far above them, up through three floors of stone, a bell was ringing in the yard, faint, for the change of watch. The lamp hissed over the table.

Hale sat with his mouth still a little open on the word he hadn't said.

He shut it, and picked up his stick from where it leaned against the chair, and looked at it, and put it down again.

"Keeps the old customs," he said. "I meant. The inner circle keeps the old customs. The noon bell. The facing east. The words." He turned a page of the black book that didn't need turning. "That's what I meant."

It was the worst lie she'd ever heard. She'd heard better from first-years caught out of bed after the bell. He knew it, too. She could see him know it. He sat there at the table under the lamp with his big tired face gone grey, and didn't look at her, and didn't look at the door at the end of the archive either. Very carefully didn't look at it.

She didn't say anything.

Afterwards she'd wonder why she didn't. She'd been on the river watch since she was seventeen; she knew how to catch a man in a lie and hold him in it until he gave you the rest. She could have. She could see him waiting for her to.

She didn't. Because of his face. Because he was an old man with a bad hip who'd been kind to her when no one else at Greyhold had, and who'd told her about the gate, and who'd said *I'd like to see it, one day, before I die.* And because the look on his face wasn't a liar's look. It was the look of a man carrying something very heavy up a very long stair, who's just stumbled, and caught himself, and is standing there with his heart going, waiting to see if it's going to fall.

"Thank you, Warden," she said. "For telling me about the oath."

Hale looked at her.

"Ashdown," he said. Very quietly.

"Yes?"

He didn't say anything else. He shook his head and picked up the black book, and held it closed in his big hands.

"Go to your muck," he said.

Over the inside of the archive door, where you only saw it on your way out, someone had carved a line, three hundred years ago. *What is written here is true. What is true is not all written here.* The first archivist, Hale said, as a joke. He said it had taken him thirty years to understand it wasn't.

---

She went to the island that night.

She hadn't meant to. She'd gone to her muck, and done it, thirty-one stalls by lantern light, and been to supper, and sat on her cot in the dormitory with the shutters closed and the others asleep. And she'd thought about the door at the end of the archive, black oak and iron, with no handle. And the line at the top of the first page. *From the closing.* And the mark beside each name. A square, with a line through it. *Like a door.*

She'd got up, and put her coat on, and gone down to the landing.

Ottery was there with the punt. She hadn't sent for him. He was just there, at the bottom of the steps, sitting in the stern in the dark with his pipe.

"Your mother said you'd come," he said. "She said you had a face on you at supper." He handed her the pole. "She says to tell her when you're back."

She poled them up. Three leagues and more, against the current, in the dark. Her ribs had mended enough. She didn't light a lamp. She didn't need one; she knew the river now, every bend of it, every snag, the way she knew the stalls in the Long Barn. At the island Ottery took the punt into the reeds at the mouth of the west channel and sat there with the boathook across his knees, and she went up the stream.

She didn't bring anything. Not the hammer, not the chisel, not kindling, not a cup. Nothing. She'd thought about it on the river and decided. Empty hands. Empty pockets. Nothing it could see and think about.

She came to her place on the bank. She didn't sit there. She walked on, past it. Five paces. Toward the water.

She stopped at fifteen paces, and sat down.

---

The lion came down at the hour, the way it always did, out of the trees on the far side, and stopped at the edge of them, and looked at her.

It saw where she was. She saw it see. Its head came up, a little. It looked at the place on the bank where she always sat, twenty paces off, and at the place where she was now, fifteen, closer to the water, closer to it.

Its lip went back.

She didn't move. She'd known it would, and she'd decided, on the river, what she'd do when it did. Nothing. She sat with her hands open on her knees and looked at the water, and felt her whole body go cold the way it always did, and didn't move.

The lip stayed back. Longer than it had in weeks. She could hear it breathing, across the stream. She could hear the ground under her, very faint, tremble.

Then the lip came down.

It didn't drink. It stood at the edge of the trees and looked at her with its pale eyes. Looked at her empty hands. At her pockets. At the place by the water where her fire usually was, and the flat grey stone where she'd laid the iron, and the cold grass where it had lain down last time.

It came down to the water, slowly, and lay down.

Not at the edge of the trees, where it had lain before. Nearer. At the water's edge, on its own side, with its great forepaws almost in the stream, and its head up, facing her. With the collar on its neck, and the cut chain lying in the wet grass. Fifteen paces. The nearest it had ever chosen to be.

She sat there with her hands open, and it lay there, and between them the stream went over the stones in the dark.

She thought about the door at the end of the archive.

She sat on the bank of the stream on the island with the lion lying fifteen paces off and thought about a black oak door with three iron bands across it, and no handle, and a lock as big as a man's hand. And a line at the top of a page in the old script. *From the closing.*

The lion lifted its head.

She saw it. It lifted its head, sharply, the way a dog does when it hears its name called in another room, and turned it. Not toward her. Away. South-west, downriver, across the dark of the west channel and the Hearthmere bank beyond it. Toward Greyhold.

It held it there. Ears forward. Eyes on the dark.

Under her, the ground hummed. Faint. Very faint. Like a bowl of bronze that someone had touched with one finger, once, a long way off.

The lion lowered its head again and laid it on its forepaws at the edge of the stream, and looked at her.

She sat there until it got up and went.

---

Hale sent for her the next day.

Not to the archive, this time. To the Lesson Wall, at noon, after the bell. He was sitting on the stone bench along its foot, alone, in the thin cold sun, with his stick across his knees, and when she came across the yard he moved over without a word, and she sat down beside him.

They sat there for a while. The yard was empty. Everybody had gone in to dinner.

"I've had a report," said Hale at last. "From over the river. The inner circle has. I thought you'd want to know."

She waited.

"Vey's company," said Hale. "The Tide Council's sellswords. You'll have heard of them. They went into the Fellwood ten days ago, the great forest under the Scarfell, east of Harrow's End, a hundred and twenty men and two wyverns. After the dragon farmer." He was looking at the yard, not at her. "They found him. Up a crag in the middle of the forest, in a cave, with his three. They had him boxed in for three days."

Maren didn't breathe.

"And they lost him," said Hale. "In the rain. A week ago. Out of a cave with only one way out, past a hundred and twenty men, without a single one of them seeing. Vey went back to Wendmouth in a temper and the Council's furious." He paused. "And our scouts on the far bank say three dragons were seen that same night, high, in the rain. Going south. Toward the coast."

He turned the stick in his hands. "There's more in it, for what it's worth. Our man in Wendmouth says Vale spoke on the Tidesteps there at noon on the day of Ferriby. Three thousand heard him." He almost smiled. "The man in two places. The river was telling that one before you were born. I've a box of it in the archive: a new port every year, a new raid the same day, and always somebody who'll swear to the hour." He put it away with a shrug. "Crowds."

She thought of the face on the smithy roof, lit from below. She thought of the old drover on the River Road. *Unless he's not a man.* She let it go.

"South," said Maren.

"South. Out of the Marches. Away from the river." Hale let out a long breath. "He's gone."

She looked at him.

He'd said it with relief. She'd heard it. Real relief, deep and simple, like a man putting down a heavy bag at the end of a long road. *He's gone.* And then, as she watched, his face changed. The relief went out of it. And something came in behind it that she'd never seen on his face before, and it took her a moment to know it.

Shame.

He sat on the bench in the cold sun with his stick across his knees and looked at the yard, and was ashamed of being glad.

"Why?" she said.

He didn't answer.

"Why are you glad he's gone?" she said. "He saved Harrow's End, the scouts say. He got children off a roof. He stopped the fighting. Twenty of our knights sat on the bank and watched, and he crossed." She heard her voice go hard. "Why would anybody be glad he's gone?"

Hale didn't look at her.

"Because he's three," he said at last. Very quietly.

"What?"

"Because he's three. Three bonds. In one man. It's never happened." His big hands were tight on the stick. "And everything in that archive, Ashdown, everything I've read in forty years, every line in every book that's old enough to remember anything, says that when the old signs come all at once, beasts turning their heads to the ground and the earth answering and things that can't happen happening, the last time it was so…"

He stopped. Again.

She waited. She wasn't going to let him off this time. She sat beside him on the bench and waited, and he knew she was waiting, and the cold sun went behind a cloud, and the yard went grey.

"I'm an old man," said Hale. "And I read too much." He got up, slowly, with the stick, putting his weight on his good hip. He stood there for a moment looking down at her. "Go to your dinner."

He went away across the yard toward the north tower. Limping. With the keys at his belt chiming softly at every step, and among them, longer than the rest, black, heavy, plain, the one she knew now would fit a lock as big as a man's hand.

She sat on the bench along the foot of the Lesson Wall, alone, and thought about a farmer with three dragons, going south in the rain, toward the sea.

*Because he's three.*

*The last time it was so.*

*What happened?*

Nobody came to tell her.
