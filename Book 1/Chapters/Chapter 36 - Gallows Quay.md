---

When Calder was small there was a man who came round the Marches every spring with a dancing bear. He'd stop on the green at Harrow's End and play a pipe, and the bear would stand up on its back legs and turn round, slowly, with a chain from its nose-ring to the man's fist, and the children would throw coppers. Calder had thrown coppers. Everyone had. And then one spring his father had been on the green when the bear came, and had stood and watched it turn, and then walked over to the man and said something, quietly, that Calder didn't hear. And the man had gone white and packed up his pipe and gone, and never came again. Calder had asked his father what he'd said. His father wouldn't tell him. All he'd said was: *some things you don't throw coppers at.*

---

Market day on Gallows Quay was the day after the Tidesteps.

He went back in at dawn, down the drove road from the Wendwood, with Lisska's deer in his stomach and his hat pulled down. She'd brought it to him at the edge of the forest the night before, the whole haunch of a hind, torn off neat, and sat and watched him cook it on a fire of dry oak in a hollow nobody could see from the road, with her head on one side.

*You're going back,* she'd said.

"Yes."

*To the house that buys things.*

"Yes."

*Why?*

He'd turned the meat on the stick for a while before he answered.

"Because Vey said it bought two dray-beasts out of a farm on the river this summer," he said. "And I want to see if it's true."

*And if it is?*

He hadn't answered that.

---

It was true.

He knew it the moment he came out onto the quay. He'd have known it from the other end of the harbour. Market day on Gallows Quay was the noisiest place he'd ever been in his life, louder than the Tidesteps, louder than the battle, and in the middle of all of it, in a pen at the landward end of the quay with rope on her and a ticket tied to her halter, there was a grey dray-mare with a long soft sad face and small round ears, standing with her head down, and he'd have known her anywhere.

Mabbin.

Joss's Mabbin. Sorrel's mother. He'd seen her every week of his life at Upper Rush, in Joss's top paddock, with her head over the gate, waiting for him to come by with a crust. She was thin. Her ribs showed. There were galls on her neck from the rope, raw, and on her legs from the hobbles, and her grey coat was dull and dirty. But it was her.

He stopped at the pen. He couldn't help it.

She lifted her head and looked at him.

He saw her nostrils go. Wide. Smelling. Her great long sad face came round toward him, slowly, over the rail of the pen, and her nose went to his coat, to his hands, to his hair, breathing him in. He didn't know if she knew him. He didn't know if dray-beasts knew anybody. But she knew something. The Marches, maybe. The barley and the beck and the smoke of Upper Rush. She breathed it in off him, long and deep, and then she let it out in a great shaking sigh and put her head against his chest, and left it there.

He stood at the pen on Gallows Quay with his hand on her neck and his eyes shut.

*The old farmers swear their beasts understand them,* he thought. *I never believed it.*

"You buying, friend?"

A man at his elbow. A drover, by the look of him, with a stick and a ticket-book. Pleasant, busy, impatient.

"Fine mare, that," said the drover. "Marches-bred. Dray-beast. You'll not find better pulling. Bit thin, but she'll fatten. Had the standing-word said over her this morning, so she'll lead like a lamb. Lot thirty. Goes at noon." He looked Calder up and down, the hat, the coat, the burned-out boots. "Reserve's forty silver."

"Where'd she come from?"

The drover's face didn't change at all. "Marches," he said. "Like I said."

"Which farm?"

"Didn't ask." The drover shrugged. "Nobody asks. You buying?"

"No," said Calder.

"Then move along, friend. There's people want to look."

---

He moved along. He walked the whole quay, end to end, as you'd walk Cotter's Cross on market day, looking at everything. He made himself.

It was all cages. Pens, for the big ones. Iron cages for the dangerous ones. Wire boxes, stacked three high, for the small. Down both sides of the quay and along the middle, under awnings, with men standing by them shouting prices and men walking past them looking, and the noise of the beasts going up into the grey sky over the harbour, barking and howling and bellowing and screaming, so that the gulls kept away. Between the cages a man with a tray round his neck went up and down selling charms against bite and claw, warranted, he said, with the Council's stamp, and the buyers bought them like pies.

He saw the hounds. A dozen of them, in iron cages, thin and frightened and scarred. He saw martens going round and round in their wire. He saw the white humped ox from the evening he'd come, lying on its side in a cage too small for it, with its eyes shut and its great sides going in and out. He saw birds, hawks and falcons, hooded, on perches. He saw a cage with a sea-otter in it the size of a pony.

He saw the wyvern.

It was at the far end, near the red-and-gold building, in the biggest iron cage on the quay. Young. Smaller than Vey's two, but the same shape: black-green, lean, leathery, with no forelegs and a whiplash tail. Wild-caught, the ticket said. Saltreach coast. It was hooded in black leather, the way Vey's had been, and it crouched at the back of the cage with its wings folded tight and its hooded head turning, turning, at every sound.

Beside it, in a pen with iron bars and a double lock, the thing that stopped him dead.

---

At first he couldn't tell what it was. It looked like a deer. A young one, a yearling, lean and long-legged and fine-boned, standing in the corner of the pen with its back to the bars. But it was the wrong colour for a deer: grey, going to silver, going to black at the legs and the muzzle, like a storm cloud. And its feet were cloven, like a goat's. And on its forehead, between its eyes, there was a horn.

Just a stub. Short, no longer than his finger. Spiral, twisted like a shell, the colour of old bone.

He'd heard of them. Everyone had. The Bond-King's beasts, in Sarovel, far away to the west, beyond Hearthmere. The crowned beast. The rarest thing in the six kingdoms after the phoenix. You didn't see them. You didn't see them ever, unless you were a king.

*Unicorn.*

Something came through the green mark, very faint, very far off. Lisska. Not words; she was five leagues back in the forest, too far for words. Only a jolt of her, quick and bright and sharp-eared, the way she went when she'd seen something new. She'd felt him stop.

The colt turned its head and looked at him.

It had eyes like no beast he'd ever seen. Not a deer's, soft and dark and stupid. Not a horse's. Long, almond-shaped, silver-grey, with a darker ring round the edge. And there was something in them that he'd only ever seen once before: in Aurveth's one eye, high over the island in the moonlight, when the white bridge came up out of the dark of her.

An old thing, looking out of a young one.

It looked at him.

Then it bared its teeth, and threw up its head, and the stub of horn caught the light, and it screamed at him. A high, wild, furious scream, like a woman's. And lunged at the bars. He stepped back. The handler by the pen, a big man with a bandaged hand and a bandaged forearm, laughed sourly.

"Bit two of us already," he said. "Bit my mate's thumb clean off. Lovely temper." He nodded at the ticket on the pen. "Out of Sarovel. Smuggled. Don't ask how. Reserve's six hundred, and they say the Halloran house'll go to a thousand." He looked at the colt. "More fool them."

---

In the window of the red-and-gold building at the end of the quay, behind glass, there was a feather.

He almost didn't see it. Everybody else was looking at the cages. But he was standing by the unicorn's pen, a few paces from the window, and the light caught it, and he looked.

It was on a cushion. Dark red velvet, in a glass case on a stand in the middle of the window, all on its own. A single feather. Long, as long as his forearm. Red, going to gold at the tip, going to something brighter than gold that his eyes couldn't hold. It didn't move. There was no wind behind the glass. But it seemed to move. It seemed to be burning, very slowly, from the inside, as a coal burns when you take it out of the fire and hold it in the dark.

There was a little card in front of the case, lettered in gold. He couldn't read it. Beside the window a woman had stopped, a fishwife with a basket, and she put out her hand and laid it flat on the glass of the window, and took it away again quickly, and laughed, and shook her fingers.

"Warm," she said to nobody. "It's warm. The glass is warm."

He went and put his own hand on the window, where hers had been.

It was warm, not hot. Warm like a hearthstone in the morning after the fire's gone out. Warm all the way through the glass, from a single feather on a cushion.

"Phoenix," said a man beside him. A sailor. Wondering. "They say it never cools. Never. Been in that window three years and it's as warm as the day they plucked it." He shook his head. "Not for sale. Rusk won't sell it. He just likes people to look."

Calder took his hand off the glass.

---

The auction was at noon, on a wooden platform in front of the red-and-gold building. He stood at the back of the crowd and watched.

The factor came out to run it himself. Oswin Rusk. A plump, neat, soft-handed man of middle years in a good coat of dark red, with gold buttons, and a soft polite voice, and a little book in his hand in which he wrote everything down. He didn't shout the prices. He had a man to do that, a big brass-voiced man who stood on the edge of the platform and roared. Rusk just stood behind him, and nodded, and wrote.

Lot after lot. Hounds. Martens. Falcons. The sea-otter. The white ox. The buyers stood at the front of the crowd and lifted their hands, and the brass-voiced man roared, and Rusk wrote, and the money changed hands at a table at the side of the platform, counted out by a clerk. And it was all the same.

Silver. New silver. Bright, unworn, so fresh from the die that it shone in the grey light.

He watched a buyer at the front, a lean man in grey, pay for three hounds and the sea-otter. He watched the clerk count the coins. He was too far away to see the rims. He didn't need to.

*New silver,* Vey had said.

Lot thirty came next.

---

They led her up onto the platform on her rope. Mabbin. Thin and grey and dull and galled, with her long sad face and her small round ears, and her head down. She stood in front of the crowd on the wooden boards and didn't look at anything.

"Lot thirty," roared the brass-voiced man. "Dray-beast, mare, Marches-bred, sound in wind and limb, a bit light, will fatten. Pull a wain out of a bog. Reserve forty. Who'll give me forty?"

Nobody moved for a moment.

Then, at the front, the lean man in grey lifted his hand.

"Forty," roared the brass-voiced man. "Forty, I'm bid. Forty-five?"

Nobody.

"Forty, then. Forty, once. Forty, twice." He looked at Rusk. Rusk nodded and wrote. "Sold. To the gentleman for the north."

*The north.*

The lean man in grey went to the table at the side of the platform and counted out forty silver, new and shining, and the clerk took them and wrote. And they led Mabbin down off the platform, round the side of the red-and-gold building, out of sight.

Calder stood at the back of the crowd.

*For the north.*

Where was *the north*? The Marches? The Scarfell? Hearthmere? He thought of barges on the Wend, going up the river at night with their cargo. Going north. Up the river. Back to where the beasts had come from.

Why would anybody buy beasts out of the Marches, ship them all the way south to Wendmouth, and pay to ship them all the way north again?

He went round the side of the building, after Mabbin.

---

There was a yard behind the red-and-gold building, walled, with a gate. The gate was shut. He couldn't see into the yard. But along the side of the building, between it and the next one, there was a passage, narrow, dark, stinking, full of rubbish, and halfway along the passage there was a window.

It was shuttered. The shutter didn't fit. There was a crack at the edge of it, a finger wide, and light coming through it, and something else.

A smell.

He stopped in the passage. Sweet. Burned. He knew it, and couldn't think from where. It came out through the crack in the shutter in a thin grey thread and went up the passage and into his nose and the back of his throat, and his head went slow and thick for a moment.

It sat at the back of his throat like a word he couldn't find.

He put his eye to the crack.

---

It was a small room, bare, stone, with one lamp. There was a clay pot on the floor in the middle of it, with a lid on it, loose, and the smoke was coming out of the gap under the lid in a thick grey curl and filling the room.

There was a hound on the floor beside the pot. Big, rough, brindled. Lying on its side on the stones with its legs out. There was a collar on its neck. Iron. Heavy. And on the collar, small and neat, stamped into the iron, a ring of six dots.

Its eyes were open. They were empty.

It was alive. He could see it breathing. Slow. Very slow. But there was nothing in its eyes at all. Nothing he knew. They looked at the wall the way a window looks at the dark.

There was a young man kneeling beside it. Seventeen, perhaps. Thin. In a burned-out coat like his own. With an iron ring on a cord round his neck, a little dark ring the size of a thumbnail, hanging down in the smoke. He had his hand on the hound's head, flat, between its ears, and his eyes were shut and his face was wet, and he was shaking.

And standing over them both, with his back to the window, was a man in grey. A plain grey coat, a different grey from a knight's. He was speaking. Low and steady and slow, over and over, the same words, in a voice like a man saying a prayer. Calder couldn't hear the words through the shutter. Only the sound of them. Going on and on. Like the noon bell. Like a hatching-song in the caves.

The young man's face twisted. He cried out, not loud: a small, sharp, terrible sound, like someone who's been cut.

The hound's empty eyes moved. Toward him.

Something came into them. Not the hound. Not anything Calder knew. Something like a reflection in a window. Something borrowed. Not the young man, either; there was nothing of him in it, nothing of the wet face or the shaking. Only what he wanted. His will, put on the beast like a collar.

The man in grey stopped speaking. He put his hand on the young man's shoulder.

"There," he said. Calder heard that. Quite clearly. "There. You see? You're chosen now."

Calder stepped back from the crack in the shutter.

He stood in the stinking passage with his back against the wall of the next building and his hands flat on the stones behind him, and found that he was going to be sick. He was. He bent over in the rubbish and was sick, quietly, and stood up again, and wiped his mouth, and stood there shaking.

*Chosen.*

He thought of Kharrak in the field, dragging himself through the burning straw. The gold eye. *What. What is this. What have you done.* The root going down. The bond taking, on both sides, at the same terrible astonished moment, between two creatures who'd never asked for it and couldn't have stopped it.

*That's not what that was.*

He didn't know what it was. He didn't have a word for it. But he knew what it wasn't. He'd felt the real thing go into him. Three times. Like lightning that took a lifetime to hit. And whatever was happening in that little stone room with the smoke and the collar and the man in grey saying the same words over and over was something wearing its clothes.

He thought of the young man's face. Wet. Shaking. Crying out.

*You're chosen now.*

He went back along the passage, out onto the quay, past the cages, past Mabbin's empty pen, past the unicorn colt in its corner and the phoenix feather burning slowly in the window, and kept walking until he was off Gallows Quay and back in the streets, and then he stopped and leaned on a wall and shut his eyes.

He felt them. Five leagues off. All three. Frightened for him. He couldn't tell them. He didn't have the words yet.

He'd have them by dark.

---

He took the bar from a chandler's yard on the fish-docks.

It was the kind sailors used in the capstans of the big ships: a length of oak as long as he was tall, as thick as his wrist, shod with iron at both ends. There was a stack of them in the yard, leaning against a wall under a lean-to, and the yard had a gate that didn't shut properly, and nobody was watching. He waited till dusk and took one and walked out with it over his shoulder like a docker, and nobody looked.

He waited in the Low Lantern till the middle of the night. He sat on the straw against the wall with the bar across his knees and his eyes shut, and the woman from Ewe Hollow slept a few feet off with her children, and he didn't think about anything. He felt the three of them. He'd sent it to them, as best he could, what he was going to do. They couldn't hear words at five leagues, only the feeling. The shape of it. *Going back. Tonight. Not coming to you. Not yet. Stay. Stay where you are.*

He felt Kharrak's rage come back at him, hot, wordless, *no, no, alone, no.* He felt Aurveth's long slow sorrow, that was not quite agreement and not quite refusal. He felt Lisska's fear.

*Stay,* he sent. As hard as he could. *Please. Stay.*

Then he got up and went.

---

Gallows Quay at night was dark, and quiet, and stank. The cages were still full; the lots that hadn't sold, and the ones that had, waiting to be shipped. He could hear them in the dark. The hounds, whining. The martens, going round. The white ox, breathing. And something at the far end, by the red-and-gold building, a thin high hissing sound like a kettle on the hob: the wyvern, in its hood.

There was a man on the quay. One. A watchman, with a lantern and a cudgel, walking up and down slowly by the cages, the way Calder had walked his own byre at night in lambing time. He walked up the quay and down it and up again, and every time he got to the landward end he stopped by a brazier there and warmed his hands. Calder watched him do it three times from the shadow of a fish-shed.

On the fourth time, while the man stood at the brazier with his back to the quay, Calder went past him in the dark with the bar on his shoulder, quietly, along the edge of the water.

---

He started with the hounds.

The cages had iron bolts through iron hasps, with a padlock on each bolt. He'd watched them close them at dusk. He put the iron-shod end of the capstan bar through the hasp of the first cage, behind the bolt, and leaned on it.

The hasp came out of the wood of the cage-frame with a shriek. The hound inside, a thin grey bitch with a cut on her muzzle, stared at him. Then she went. Out of the open door, past him, down the quay, into the dark, so fast he hardly saw her.

The next. The next. He went down the row of hound-cages with the bar, levering out the hasps one after another, and the hounds went, every one of them, out and away down the quay into the night. The martens' wire boxes he just opened; they had wooden pegs, not locks. The martens went past his feet like water. The sea-otter wouldn't come out. He left its door open and went on.

The white ox. Its cage had a bigger bolt and a bigger lock. He put the bar through the hasp and leaned on it with all his weight and it didn't move. He leaned again. Something went in his side, an old pain, a rib not quite mended. He leaned again. The hasp came out with a crack like a shot, and somewhere down the quay the watchman shouted.

He didn't stop. He hauled the door of the cage open, and the white ox lifted its great head off the floor and looked at him with eyes like a sad old woman's, and got up, very slowly, and came out. It didn't run. It walked. Down the quay, toward the land, with its hump swaying, the way it must have walked once on a white bridge in the sun.

The watchman was running up the quay with his lantern. He was shouting. Other voices were answering, from the red-and-gold building. Doors.

Calder went to the unicorn.

---

Its pen had a double lock. Two bolts, two hasps, two padlocks, top and bottom. He put the bar through the top one and leaned on it, and the colt in the corner of the pen screamed at him, high and wild, the same scream as before.

"I know," said Calder. Through his teeth. Leaning. "I know. I know. Hate me later."

The top hasp came out. He moved the bar to the bottom one. The watchman was close now, his lantern swinging. Behind him there were three more men coming out of the red-and-gold building, big ones, with cudgels.

The bottom hasp came out.

The gate of the pen swung open.

The colt stayed where it was, in the corner of the pen with its back to the bars and its stub of horn down and its silver eyes on him. He stepped back from the gate. Two paces. Three. Away. To let it see the gate was open, and he wasn't in it.

*Go,* he thought at it. He knew it couldn't hear. He thought it anyway. *Go on. Go. It's open. Go home.*

It went.

It came out of the pen like a thrown spear. Past him, so close the wind of it went across his face, and its cloven hooves rang on the stones of the quay, and it went down the length of the quay toward the land, past the watchman, who threw himself sideways, past the three men with the cudgels, who threw themselves the other way, into the dark. Silver-black. Gone.

No. At the very end of the quay, where it met the land, by the brazier, it stopped.

He saw it in the light of the brazier. Standing there, on its long fine legs, with its head up. It had turned round. It was looking back. Back down the whole length of Gallows Quay, past the empty cages and the running men and the swinging lanterns. At him.

He'd never forget that look. Not gratitude; beasts don't do gratitude. As if the colt were looking at him and seeing something it had been told about, a long time ago, by its mother, or its mother's mother, in a wood in the west. As if it were remembering him.

Then it was gone.

---

The wyvern he freed last. He didn't mean to. He was running by then, with the men behind him, and its cage was the last one before the red-and-gold building, and he put the bar through its hasp as he went by without stopping, and wrenched, and the hasp came out, and the door swung, and he kept running.

Behind him the wyvern came out of its cage, blind, hooded, screaming. He heard it go. A thin high furious scream, going up, and the clap of leathery wings. It went up into the dark over the harbour, blind, and he heard it go round once over the quay and then away, out to sea, still screaming, until the screaming went into the sound of the waves.

He went round the side of the red-and-gold building, into the stinking passage, past the shuttered window with the light still coming through the crack, and the smell. He didn't look in. He came out at the back, at the yard gate.

It was barred on the inside. He put the capstan bar through the gap between the gate and the post, and leaned on it, and the bar of the gate came up out of its brackets on the other side and fell. He pushed the gate open.

There was a stable in the yard. Five stalls. In the end one, tied short, with her head down, thin and grey and dull, Mabbin.

She lifted her head when he came in. Her nostrils went. Wide.

He untied her. He put his hand on her neck and turned her and led her out of the stall and out of the yard through the open gate, at a walk, the way you'd lead a beast out of a burning byre. Steady. Nothing in it to be afraid of.

And came round the corner of the building onto the quay, and they were there.

---

Eight of them. Rusk's men, with cudgels and lanterns, and the watchman, and two more coming up behind with swords. In a line across the landward end of the quay, between him and the land, with the brazier behind them. They'd let the beasts go past. They'd been waiting for whoever had let them out.

He stopped. Mabbin stopped behind him, with her head against his back.

"That's Halloran property," said one of them. Big, with a beard. "That mare. Sold this noon. Bought and paid for." He lifted his cudgel. "Put her down, friend, and the bar, and come here."

Calder stood on Gallows Quay in the dark with the capstan bar in one hand and Mabbin's halter rope in the other, and looked at the eight of them, and knew he couldn't get past.

*Stay,* he'd told her. As hard as he could. *Please. Stay.*

He felt her coming before he heard her. A bright frantic hum through the green mark, getting louder, much louder, much too fast, the way it had on the night Harrow's End burned. Coming in from the north, over the dark. Over the drove road, over the Wendwood, over the roofs.

*I know you said stay. I know. I know. I'm sorry. I couldn't. I could feel you. You were so frightened. I'm sorry.*

And over the roofs of Wendmouth, over the harbour, out of the dark, low, fast, with her wings spread and the light of the brazier coming up under her and the blue ribbon gone from her horn, Lisska came down.

---

She didn't burn anything. She didn't touch anyone. She came down over the landward end of Gallows Quay so low that the wind of her wings knocked the brazier over and blew out every lantern on the quay at once, and opened her jaws, and screamed.

The eight men ran.

He didn't blame them. He'd have run. A dragon out of the dark, over a city that had never seen one, screaming, close enough to touch. They dropped their cudgels and their lanterns and their swords and ran, every one of them, back along the quay and into the streets, shouting. And all over Wendmouth, in the dark, he heard it start. Doors banging. Shutters. People shouting from windows. *Dragon. Dragon. A dragon over the harbour.* Bells, somewhere. A woman screaming, on and on.

Lisska landed on the stones of the quay in front of him, by the overturned brazier, with the coals scattered round her feet, and folded her wings, and looked at him.

*I know,* she said. *I know you said.*

"You little fool," said Calder.

*I know.*

"You lovely little fool."

He went to her and put his hand on her muzzle. She was shaking.

"They saw you," he said.

*I know.*

"They saw me. In your light. When you came down. The big one with the beard. He was looking right at me."

*I know. I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I couldn't let them.*

He stood there with his hand on her muzzle and Mabbin's rope in the other hand, and behind him, at the far end of the quay, in the dark, he heard the white ox lowing, and the sea-otter splashing into the harbour, and somewhere very far out over the water, very faint, the wyvern still screaming.

"Go," he said. "Go back. Go back to the others. I'll walk. Out the drove road. With her. Go."

*Promise you'll come?*

"Promise."

She went. Up, over the roofs, into the dark, north. He felt her go, the bright hum going away, getting smaller.

He turned and led Mabbin off Gallows Quay at a walk, into the streets, with the bells ringing all over Wendmouth and the shutters banging and the people shouting *dragon* from the windows, and nobody looked at a burned-out farmer leading a thin grey dray-mare, going north, as a thousand burned-out farmers were going north and south every day on the roads of the Marches.

He'd been right about that, at least. Nobody looked at him.

Not yet.

---

By morning there was a paper on the Tidesteps.

He didn't see it. He was five leagues up the drove road by then, in the Wendwood, under the oaks, with Mabbin grazing beside Aurveth as if she'd known dragons all her life, and Kharrak saying nothing at all to him, nothing, very loudly, and Lisska curled against his legs pretending to be asleep. He heard about it later, from a carter on the road north.

A paper. Nailed to the iron rail at the top of the Tidesteps, where Vale had stood. With the red and gold of the Halloran house at the top, and the ship and the wave of the Tide Council under it, and a likeness: a drawing, in ink, of a man with a long face and a hat pulled down and a coat with the collar up. Not good. Good enough.

*Wanted, alive. The Marches farmer called Calder Fenn, who keeps three dragons. For theft of Halloran property and breach of the Council's peace. Two hundred silver.*

"Two hundred," said the carter, who'd stopped to water his horse at the ford where Calder was sitting, and hardly looked at him. "For a barley farmer. Imagine." He spat. "And you should've heard them on the quay. All the stall-holders. Yelling at anyone who'd listen. *First some Hearthmere knight-girl smashing locks at Millbrook, freeing other folk's beasts, and now this Marches farmer letting out the whole of Gallows Quay in one night! Is nobody's property safe?*" He laughed. "*Property.* Gods. I've carted for them. I know what their property is."

He got back on his cart and clicked to his horse.

"Two hundred silver," he said, over his shoulder, as he went. "I'd not want to be him."

Calder sat at the ford and watched him go.

*Some Hearthmere knight-girl smashing locks at Millbrook.*

*Her too. She did this before me. In the summer. At Millbrook. For a duskcat in a cage. Before the island, before the lion, before any of it.*

He thought about the unicorn colt at the end of the quay, by the brazier, looking back.

He thought about the phoenix feather, burning slowly on its cushion behind the warm glass. He'd gone past it twice, last night, with the capstan bar in his hand. He could have put the bar through the window. He'd thought about it. And he'd thought about a girl in river grey on a stream bank, who could have taken a hair or a claw home and wouldn't.

*Like robbing it,* Rue had said.

He'd left the feather. Mabbin was Joss's. The feather wasn't anybody's. It was the phoenix's, and the phoenix hadn't sold it.

*Some things you don't throw coppers at.*

He got up and went to find Mabbin.
