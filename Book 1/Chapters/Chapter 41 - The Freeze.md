---

The ice came down from the north for twelve days before the river stopped.

Maren watched it from the top of the Redwatch tower, where Ottery let her stand after evening muck if she brought him a jug of something hot from the kitchen. Slabs of it, grey-white, broken off the edges of the river somewhere up in the hills, coming down the middle of the Wend in a long grinding procession. Some were the size of a table. Some were the size of a barn floor. They turned as they came and rode up on each other, and broke, and the sound of them came up to the top of the tower in the dark like a giant chewing.

For most of those days Ottery could still take her over at dusk in the punt, creeping along the slow water at the edges where the ice was thin, when the river let them. Then the big slabs came down, and no boat would go out in that, not the patrol boats and not Ottery's punt. The island lay out in the middle of the grinding white, a long dark shape with the stump of the tower standing up out of its trees, and nobody went near it, and Maren didn't see the lion.

She told herself it didn't matter. It had lived on that island long before her. It didn't need her. She'd sit on the top of the tower with Ottery's jug going cold in her hands and look at the dark shape out in the ice, and tell herself so, and not believe it.

"You'll freeze up here," Ottery would say, coming up the ladder at the change of watch.

"So will you."

"I'm paid to."

---

On the seventy-fourth night the ice jammed.

She heard it happen. Everyone at Redwatch did. Somewhere down below the island, at the bend where the river swung round toward Greyhold, the slabs coming down the middle met something they couldn't get past, a sandbar or a dead tree or just more ice, and stopped. The slabs behind them ran into them, and stopped, and the slabs behind those. It went on for an hour, a long rolling crash coming upriver in the dark like a door being slammed over and over, further and further away, until it reached somewhere beyond the island and stopped too.

After that, silence.

She'd lived on the river all her life. She'd never heard it silent. Even in the stillest summer night there was the sound of water, going past, under everything. That night there wasn't. She stood on the tower in the frost with the stars very sharp overhead and listened to the Wend not making any sound at all.

"That's it, then," said Ottery, beside her. Quietly. As if it were a death. "She's stopped."

---

It froze from the edges in.

Over three days she watched it happen from the tower. The jammed slabs stopped moving and the water between them went still and black and then grey, and then, in the mornings, white. A skin first, thin enough to see the black through. Thicker the next day. Along the edges where the water was slowest, by the reeds and the shallows, solid. Snow came on the third night and lay on it, and in the morning there was a road.

It ran from the Saltreach bank. You could see it from the tower: a long white causeway of jammed and frozen slabs, humped and broken but solid, going out from the Eelweir at the mouth of Hollow Beck to the island's eastern shore. On the western side of the island, between the island and Redwatch, another, shorter, going out from the reed beds at the stream mouth toward the shallows below the tower, and stopping short.

Between them, the island. Between the island and the Hearthmere bank, along the line where the current had always run fastest, the deep channel where the patrol boats used to go, there was still a strip of black water. Narrower every day. Steaming in the cold mornings. Skinned over at night with a thin grey ice that broke up again in the sun.

"Nobody goes on it," said Ottery, on the seventy-seventh morning, standing at the foot of the tower with the whole watch round him, all six of them. "Nobody. Not to the island, not across, not to the end of the reeds to have a look. You don't trust a river till she's been still a fortnight. She's been still three days." He looked at them one by one. Last of all at Maren. "Nobody."

She nodded.

---

She saw the dragon that afternoon.

She was on the tower, alone, with the jug, in the last of the light. The sun was going down behind her over Hearthmere and lying red along the white of the river, and she was looking upriver, past the island, toward Harrow's End, because that was where you looked, from Redwatch, when you wanted to see something that wasn't Greyhold.

Something came down out of the sky above the river.

Far off. A league and more, upriver, above the bend where Hollow Beck came in. A shape, dropping out of the grey cloud over the Saltreach hills in a long slant, with its wings half closed. Dark. Big. It came down onto the white of the river and landed, and she saw it, quite clearly, even from so far: a dragon, standing on the ice.

It stood there in the middle of the frozen river with its wings half open, very still. It lifted one foreleg and set it down again. Hard. She thought she heard it, a long way off, a dull crack. Then the other foreleg.

It went up all at once, beating, back into the cloud, and away north-east over the hills, and was gone.

She stood on the tower with the jug in her hands.

It had been dark red. She was nearly sure. The colour of rust. Not the big bronze one that had gone over the moon above the island, and not the little green one. The other one. The one Haverly had called the red.

*Testing it,* she thought. *It was testing the ice.*

She didn't tell Ottery. She couldn't have said why. She drank the cold tea and went down the ladder.

---

On the seventy-ninth morning there were people on the island.

She was in the boathouse when the shout went up. She'd come out from Greyhold before dawn on Turnip with Grieve's leave, to fetch two boathooks Ottery had promised the stables, and she was on her knees on the cold planks lashing them together when somebody on the tower bellowed, and then everyone was running.

She ran out onto the bank below the tower with the boathooks still in her hands.

The island was half a league off, across the white. The near edge of the ice was solid under the reeds, then the snow-covered slabs going out, then the black line of the channel, narrow, steaming, with a thin grey skin over it, then more white, then the island's shore.

On the island's shore, at the edge of the channel, there were people.

Four. A man and a woman, and a child, a boy, and something in the woman's arms that was a baby, wrapped in a shawl. Standing on the island's edge in the grey dawn with bundles on their backs, looking across the black channel at Redwatch. At the tower. At the people on the bank.

They'd come across the white road from the Saltreach side in the dark. She understood it at once. From Harrow's End, or Upper Rush, or one of the burned farms. Over the jammed ice to the island and through the island's trees, and now they were at the last of it. The channel. The only thing between them and Hearthmere.

The man stepped out onto the grey skin over the black water.

"Back!" Ottery was on the bank, his hands round his mouth. "Back! Get back off it! It won't hold! Go back!"

The man looked up. He heard. Maren saw him hear. He looked across at Ottery, and at the tower, and at the knights' grey on the people standing on the bank. He looked back at his wife, and said something, and took another step.

He didn't believe them. Or he didn't care. He'd come from the far bank, from whatever was burning over there this week, and he'd walked across a frozen river in the dark with his children, and he wasn't going back to that.

The woman came out after him, with the baby. Then the boy.

---

She didn't decide to go. She just found that she had the rope from the boathouse round her waist, and Ottery shouting at her, and the end of the rope in his hands because she'd shoved it there, and she was out on the ice.

Flat, the way the river watch taught every recruit their first winter. Never walking. Lying down, belly to the ice, arms and legs spread wide, so your weight went everywhere at once and nowhere very much. Pulling yourself along with your hands and the boathook. She'd done it a dozen times on the frozen ponds behind Greyhold, in drill, with Pell standing on the bank shouting. She'd never done it on the river.

The snow-covered slabs near the bank were solid. She went out over them fast, sliding, pulling, the boathook ahead of her. The cold came up through her coat. The channel came closer, black and narrow and steaming, with the grey skin over it.

The family was on the skin. All of them. The man in front, the woman behind him with the baby, the boy at the back. Walking. Upright. In a line. Halfway across.

She heard the ice before she saw it go. A sound like a whip, very sharp, very close. Then another.

"Down!" she screamed. "Lie down! Lie flat! Spread out!"

The woman heard her. The woman went down, at once, onto her knees and then onto her side, curled round the baby, on the grey ice. The boy went down beside her. The man turned round, and looked back at them, and the ice under him opened like a mouth, and he went in.

---

She got to the woman first.

She came off the edge of the solid slabs onto the grey skin and felt it give under her, bend like a plank, and didn't stop. She slid the boathook out ahead of her, as far as she could reach, and got the hook into the woman's shawl, and pulled. The woman came, sliding, on her side, with the baby screaming against her chest. Maren pulled her past, onto the snow-covered slabs behind, and let go, and turned.

The boy. Eight, nine. White-faced. Lying flat on the grey ice where his mother had pushed him, a few lengths from the black hole where his father had gone. Staring at it.

"Come here," said Maren. "Come here. To me. On your belly. Like a snake. Come on."

He came. He couldn't have been more frightened, and he came, wriggling across the ice to her on his stomach, the way she told him, and she got her hand in his collar and dragged him back past her to his mother.

The hole.

The man was in it. She could see him, his head and one arm, black against the black water, with the edge of the ice round him in a jagged ring. He had his hand on the edge. He was trying to pull himself out, and every time he put his weight on the edge it broke off in his hand, and he went under, and came up again, further from her.

She went to him. On her stomach. The ice bending under her. She could hear it, all round her, small sharp cracks like knuckles. She got to the edge of the hole and reached out with the boathook and he grabbed it.

He was heavy. The current was dragging at him. She lay on the ice with her toes dug in and the boathook in both hands and pulled, and he came, a little, toward her, and the ice she was lying on broke.

---

The cold hit her like a wall.

She went under with the boathook still in her hands. The black closed over her head. She felt the rope go tight round her waist, Ottery, on the bank, pulling, and then she felt it go slack. Cut. The edge of the ice had sawn through it like a knife.

The current took her.

She'd always known the Wend was fast in the deep channel. She'd never known it was this fast. It took her like a leaf. Down, under the ice, away from the hole, away from the light. She was under the ice. She could see it above her, grey, a ceiling, with the dawn coming through it faintly, and she put her hands up and pressed them flat against it, and it didn't move. It was thick here. It was solid. She was under it, in the black, going downstream, and there was no way up.

*This is how it happens.* The Widow's Frost. Men who sat down in the reeds for a moment's rest and didn't get up.

*Ma.*

Something hit the ice above her.

---

She felt it before she understood it. A shock, through the water, through her hands pressed flat against the underside of the ice. Like a hammer on an anvil. Again. Closer. Then a sound, coming down through the ice into the black water, deep, grinding, so low she felt it in her teeth: a millstone, turning. The ice above her groaned.

And broke.

It came apart over her head in great white pieces, and the grey light came down through the water in a shaft, and something came down through the light.

She never saw it. Not properly. Something dark. Something that went into the water above her and closed on her coat, at the shoulder, at the back of her neck, and pulled, close and hard and enormous, nothing like the rope from far away. She came up out of the black into the air so fast that the water went up her nose and into her mouth and she was choking and blind and there was a roaring in her ears that might have been the blood in them or might have been something else.

She was being dragged. Across ice. On her back. By the shoulder of her coat, with her heels bumping over the broken slabs and the snow going up round her in a spray. She felt heat, at her neck and shoulder, where it held her. Great heat. Breath. She smelled fur. Wet fur, and cold stone, and something underneath that she'd smelled before, in a tower, on her first night, and hadn't been able to place.

Fur in the sun.

Snow under her back instead of ice. Earth. Then nothing was holding her, and she was lying on her face on frozen ground, coughing up the river.

---

When she could see again, there was nobody there.

She was on the island's western shore, at the edge of the trees, below the reed beds at the stream mouth. On frozen earth with snow on it. She pushed herself up onto her hands and knees, and looked round, and there was nothing. The trees. The snow. The white of the river, and the black channel beyond it, steaming, and the bank at Redwatch beyond that, with tiny figures on it, running.

The shore ice beside her.

There was a hole in it. A second one, here, at the island's edge, where the river came in close under the trees. Broken from above. A great ragged hole in the shore ice the size of a cart, with the black water slopping in it and broken white slabs tipped up on end round its edges, as if something very heavy had come down on them all at once. Or come up through them.

Leading from the hole, across the snow on the shore and up into the trees, there were furrows. Long, deep, raked. Four of them, side by side, each one as wide as her hand.

She looked at them.

Beside her on the snow, someone coughed.

The man. Lying on his back on the shore with his arms out and his face blue, coughing up water. Alive. She didn't know how he'd got there. She hadn't had him. She'd lost the boathook in the water. She'd lost everything. Yet he was lying beside her on the island's shore, breathing.

His coat was torn at the shoulder. Torn open in a long ragged rip, as if something had taken hold of it there.

---

Ottery came an hour later, the long way, upriver along the Hearthmere bank to where the channel narrowed and had frozen hard at the edges in the night, and across, and down the island's shore, flat on his belly over every doubtful stretch, with two of his men and a sledge. They took the man first. Then they came back for her.

She was sitting on a fallen tree at the edge of the shore, wrapped in her own wet coat, shaking. She'd sat there all the while and looked at the furrows going up into the trees.

Ottery stood on the shore and looked at them too.

He looked at the hole in the shore ice the size of a cart. He looked at the furrows. He looked at her. At last he looked up into the trees, where the furrows went, into the dark under the snow-laden branches, and stood there a while with his old face quite still.

"The current threw us up," said Maren. Her teeth were going so hard she could barely get it out. "Under the ice. It's fast in the channel. It threw us up through a weak place. Onto the shore."

Ottery looked at her.

"The current," he said.

"Yes."

He looked at the furrows again. Four of them. Each as wide as a hand. Going up into the trees.

"Aye," he said at last. "The current." He bent down and got his arm under hers. "Up you get. On the sledge. Before you lose your toes."

He said no more about it. Not on the sledge, not across the ice, not in the guardroom at Redwatch with her feet in a bucket of water that felt like fire and the woman, Haddow she said they were called, crying in the corner with the baby and the boy and her husband wrapped in every blanket in the tower. He brought her tea. He put a warming-stone in her lap, one of the two the tower kept for the winter watch, and she held it against her stomach with both hands. He put more wood on the fire. He went up the tower.

At the door he stopped, with his hand on the frame, and looked back at her.

"Your mother," he said. "She'll hear about this by dark. Every soul on the river will."

"I know."

"I'll not tell her about the furrows," said Ottery. "You can."

He went up.

---

She sat by the fire with her feet in the bucket and her hands round the cup and shook.

*Something hit the ice above me.*

*Something came down through the light.*

She'd never seen it. Not its face, not its paw, not anything. A dark shape, and heat, and the smell of fur in the sun. And four furrows in the snow going up into the trees.

She thought of the hollow in the north end, and the hill that breathed. She thought of the shore on the night it woke, and the claws coming across her shoulder, half out. The stream. The tower door. The paw on her chest in the shallows, pressing down. The night it crossed the water and lay down on her side.

*It could have left me under the ice.*

*A week,* she thought. *The ice was running. I couldn't come, and it couldn't know why. Maybe it was watching from the trees when I came out on the ice.* There was no way to know, and no way to ask.

Across the guardroom, the Haddow boy was watching her. He'd stopped shivering. He was sitting on the floor by his mother with a blanket round him, staring at Maren with his eyes very wide.

"Was that a monster?" he said. In a whisper. "On the island? That pulled you out? Da says he felt it. He says something pulled him out by his coat."

Maren looked at him.

"It was the current," she said.

The boy looked at her a while longer.

"I don't think it was," he said.

Maren didn't answer. After a while he turned and looked at the window, toward the island, and kept looking.
