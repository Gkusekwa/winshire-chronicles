---

The paper was on the river gate before anyone was awake.

Maren saw it because she was the first one up. She was always the first one up, these days: out of the dormitory in the black before dawn and across the frozen yard to the Long Barn with the lantern, before even Grieve. That morning, midwinter morning, she went across the yard with the frost so hard it rang under her boots, and the lantern swung, and the light went across the inner face of the river gate, and there was a paper on it.

Nailed to the oak. At the height of a man's eyes. Four nails, one in each corner, driven in neatly.

She stopped.

The river gate was inside the outer wall. It led from the yard down to the landing steps, not out to the road, and you couldn't come at it from outside without passing the watch on the wall above, and the watch on the landing below, and the bar on the inside. Whoever had nailed a paper to the river gate in the night had done it from inside Greyhold.

She held up the lantern and read it.

---

She didn't take in all of it. She took in the first line, because it was in bigger letters than the rest, black and plain.

*TO THE PEOPLE OF HEARTHMERE: WHAT LIES BELOW.*

It was long. There was the ring of six joined hands at the top, the same woodcut that had been on Tobin's pamphlet a lifetime ago, and the two words under it. And there was the part in the middle that made her hand shake so that the lantern light jumped all over the paper and she had to put the lantern down on the frozen ground and hold the paper still with her other hand to read it again.

*Beneath the fortress-school of Greyhold, and running under the river Wend toward the island the river folk call the Old Ruin, the crown of Hearthmere keeps in chains the last of the great beasts of the old realm: the Titan of the songs, that lay down below when the old ones went into the light. It has been chained there three hundred years.*

*From it the crown draws the power that lights the lamps of Hallowmere without oil; that makes the licensed magic of the colleges and the orders; that brings in the harvests and fills the treasury. Most of what Hearthmere is, it took from a beast in chains, and told you it was its own.*

*Ask your knights what they swear at the noon bell. "Keep the watch below, and do not ask." It is a jailer's oath. Every knight in Hearthmere stands guard over a prisoner, and is not told its name.*

She stood in the frozen yard with her hand on the paper.

*The bonded sit in front, and the unchosen at the back, and all of them sit on top of a beast in chains. That is your kingdom.*

At the bottom, two words. *One again.*

---

By the time the bell went every student in Greyhold had read it.

She hadn't taken it down. She'd meant to. She'd stood there with her hand on it, and thought about pulling the nails, and hadn't, and while she was standing there the first of the grooms had come across the yard to the barn, and stopped, and read it over her shoulder. Then the next. The gate watch had come down the stair, and read it, and gone white, and run.

Rowan came herself. Small and neat in her grey coat, down the north tower stair and across the yard, and the crowd of students round the gate parted for her without a word. She stood in front of the paper and read it, all of it, top to bottom. Her face didn't change at all.

She reached up and pulled out the four nails, one after another, with her small neat fingers, and folded the paper in half, and in half again, and put it inside her coat.

"Breakfast," she said to the yard. Not loudly.

Nobody moved.

Rowan looked round at them all. At the grooms and the students and the knights of the gate, standing in the frozen yard in the dark. Last of all, at Maren, at the front, by the gate, with the lantern at her feet.

"Breakfast," said Rowan again. "And then the Millbrook detail will muster at the river gate at the ninth hour. Callis has the list."

She went back across the yard to the north tower. At the foot of the stair, she stopped, and looked up at the old grey stones of the inner wall, the oldest part of Greyhold, where the students faced at the noon bell to swear. She looked at it a moment, and went in.

---

Maren was on the Millbrook detail.

Twenty of them. Callis in command, with his bitten arm healed into a long pink scar. A dozen knights of the gate behind him, Wynn among them with his hound Ash at his knee. And students for runners and horse-holders: Maren, and Haverly with Kesh, and four others of her year, and Tobin, who wasn't there when they mustered at the river gate at the ninth hour, and wasn't in the dormitory, and wasn't in the stables, and whom nobody had seen since supper the night before.

"He'll be in the privy with his breakfast," said Callis, and spat. "Leave him."

They rode down the River Road to Millbrook in the bright cold, two leagues, with the frozen river on their left glittering white in the sun as far as they could see, and the smoke of a hundred fires going up from Millbrook ahead of them into the windless blue.

Nobody talked about the paper. Nobody talked at all.

---

Millbrook's midwinter fair was on the ice.

She'd been to it every year since she was small, except the last two. Everyone on the river had. The back channel below the mill froze every winter long before the main river did, shallow and still behind the weir, and on midwinter day the whole town went out onto it. There were braziers in iron baskets set out on the ice, glowing red, and stalls on sledges selling chestnuts and hot cider and pies and ribbons, and skaters going round and round in long lines with their arms linked, and children sliding, and dogs barking, and a fiddler on the mill steps, and over everything the smell of smoke and roasting and frost.

There was a bear on a chain at the far end, by the weir. A great shaggy brown thing as tall as a horse on its hind legs, with a ring in its nose, and men with dogs. The orders had banned bear-baiting at Millbrook fifteen years ago. Nobody had ever stopped it. Near it a hedge-woman had a tray of luck-knots and fair-weather words she had no licence for, and every time the knights' horses came along the bank she picked up her tray and drifted off among the stalls, and every time they'd passed she was back.

There were more people than she'd ever seen there. Seven hundred, eight. Millbrook folk, and folk from every village and farm for leagues along the Hearthmere bank, and the refugees. A great many refugees, from the Saltreach side, who'd been living in the barns and the mill lofts and the chapel porch since the autumn, and who stood about at the edges of the fair in their burned clothes with their hands under their arms, watching the skaters.

And iron rings. She saw them everywhere, once she started looking. On cords round young men's necks, out over their coats. On girls. On a baker's apron. On the fiddler.

Callis saw them too. She watched him see them. He walked his horse along the edge of the ice with the knights behind him and his face set, and kept his mouth shut.

---

Reede came out at noon.

She was by the mill, holding Callis's horse and two others, when the fiddler on the mill steps stopped playing. Stopped in the middle of a reel. The skaters coasted to a stop. People turned round.

A man had come out of the mill door onto the top of the steps. Grey-haired, slight, in a plain brown coat, with a bookbinder's ink-stained hands and a kind, tired, ordinary face. He stood at the top of the mill steps above the frozen channel with a paper in his hand and looked out at the fair.

Maren had never seen him. She knew him at once. Tam had described him on the end of the jetty in the autumn, glowing. *He doesn't shout. He just knows things.*

He didn't shout. He held up the paper so they could all see it, and seven hundred people on the ice went quiet.

"Some of you will have seen this already," said Absalom Reede. His voice was soft. It carried anyway, in the frozen air, over the ice. "It was on the chapel door this morning. And the door of the Kettle. And the gate of Greyhold." A little murmur went through the crowd at that. "I'm going to read it to you. All of it. Because some of you can't read, and some of you won't have been let near enough to see it, and all of you have a right to hear it." He looked down at the paper. "And then you can decide for yourselves whether it's true."

He read it.

---

He read it slowly, like something he was sorry to have to tell them.

*Beneath the fortress-school of Greyhold.*

She watched the faces. That was what she'd remember, more than the words; she'd read the words already, in the frozen yard, by lantern light. She watched the faces of seven hundred people on the ice hearing them for the first time. The Millbrook folk. The farmers from up the bank. The refugees at the edges. The children, held up on shoulders.

*The last of the great beasts of the old realm. The Titan of the songs.*

An old woman near the mill steps put her hand to her mouth. Maren knew why. Everyone on the river knew the lullaby. Every mother sang it. *The last great beast lay down below. Hush now. The last one's sleeping.*

*From it the crown draws the power that lights the lamps of Hallowmere without oil.*

A man in the crowd laughed, high and disbelieving. Nobody laughed with him.

*Ask your knights what they swear at the noon bell.*

Seven hundred faces turned, a few and then more, turning round on the ice, to the edge of the fair, by the mill, where twenty of the orders sat their horses in their grey. Callis. The knights of the gate. Haverly with his falcon on his fist. Maren on foot with three horses' reins in her hands.

*"Keep the watch below, and do not ask." It is a jailer's oath.*

She stood holding the horses with seven hundred people looking at her, and said it in her head. She couldn't stop herself. She'd said it at the noon bell yesterday, facing east toward the old grey stones of the inner wall, and every day before that. *Keep the watch below, and do not ask.*

*Every knight in Hearthmere stands guard over a prisoner, and is not told its name.*

She thought of a black door at the end of the archive, three steps down, with no handle. She thought of Hale's face going grey. *The Watch had a name for it.*

---

Reede lowered the paper.

"There's more," he said. "Not on the paper. Something I've seen. Something you've all heard of." He looked out over the ice. "The old songs say that when the last great beast lies down below, the old powers go to sleep with it. And the old songs say that when the old powers come back, you'll know them. By signs." He let that go out over the crowd. "There are signs."

The crowd was very quiet.

"In the Marches, across the river," said Reede, "there's a farmer. A barley farmer. No lord. No knight. One of the unchosen, all his life. And this autumn, three dragons of the Clan lost their riders in one night, and should have died, and didn't. They chose him instead. All three. In one night. In a burned field." He paused. "You've heard of him. There's two hundred silver on his head in Wendmouth, for the crime of being chosen. Nobody in the history of Winshire has ever been chosen by three. Not since the six. Not since the one realm."

A murmur. Bigger. *The dragon farmer.* She heard it go round the ice. *The farmer. Fenn. The one with three.*

"And on the island in the river," said Reede, "the island the river folk won't land on, the Old Ruin, where the old road crossed the water, there's a beast. A great beast. Something out of the old songs. And it's woken this year, after longer than anyone can remember. And there's a girl from Greyhold who goes out to it across the river, and sits with it, and it lets her."

He didn't look at her. He didn't need to. Seven hundred people did it for him.

"Things are waking," said Reede. Very gently. "The old powers are coming back. Not to the crown. Not to the orders. Not to the bonded on the front benches. To a farmer. To a girl they put in the stables." He folded the paper. "Ask yourselves why. Ask yourselves what's been kept below, and who's kept it, and what they've built on top of it. And then ask yourselves if you want to keep it there."

He lifted his hand.

"One again," said Absalom Reede.

And the ice answered him. Not all of it. A great deal of it. The young men with the rings and the girls with the rings and the refugees at the edges and the baker and the fiddler, and an old woman by the steps with her hand still at her mouth. *One again.* It went out over the frozen channel and back off the mill wall like a wave off a cliff.

Maren stood holding the horses with her mouth shut.

---

Callis moved.

She saw him do it and knew, as he did it, that it was wrong. He put his heels to his horse and went out onto the ice, toward the mill steps, with his hand up and his voice up, bellowing. "You! You on the steps! In the name of the crown and the orders, you're under arrest for sedition! Stand where you are!"

Two of the knights of the gate went after him. Their hounds went with them.

And the crowd closed.

It didn't attack, not at first. It closed, the way water closes. Seven hundred people on the ice, and the ones nearest the steps moved, a few paces, all together, until they were between Callis and the mill. Standing. Shoulder to shoulder. Young men with rings. Millbrook farmers. A woman with a baby.

Callis's horse slowed on the ice. Stopped. He sat there in front of the wall of people, with his hand still up, and his face going red.

"Stand aside!"

Nobody stood aside.

Somebody threw something. Maren didn't see who. A chestnut, still hot from a brazier, that hit Callis's horse on the neck and made it shy. A stone. Then a lot of things, all at once, from everywhere: chestnuts, snowballs with stones in them, a pie, a horseshoe. A brazier went over on the ice with a great hiss and a spray of red coals, and the crowd near it shrieked and scattered, and Callis's horse reared and slipped on the ice and went down, and Callis with it, and the hound Ash was barking and barking, and somebody screamed, and a skater who'd been going round with his arms linked in a line went over in the rush with a sound Maren heard from the mill, a sharp crack, and then a long high scream that went on and on.

She dropped the reins.

She didn't decide to. She dropped them and ran out onto the ice toward the scream, toward the skater, a boy of fifteen lying on the ice with his leg bent the wrong way under him and people running past him, over him. She knelt beside him and put her arm over him and her body between him and the running crowd and stayed there, with feet going past her on both sides and the ice shaking.

In the middle of it, a face.

A young man's. Fair, broad-shouldered, with soot on his cheek. Standing in the crowd by the overturned brazier with his feet planted and his fists clenched, staring at Callis on the ice. With an iron ring on a cord round his neck, out over his coat, where everybody could see it.

Tam.

He saw her. Across the ice, through the running crowd. She saw him see her: kneeling on the ice in her grey, with her arm over a boy with a broken leg. His face did three things at once, the way it had done in the smithy door. Then it closed.

He turned away. Into the crowd. Gone.

---

When it was over, Reede was gone too.

Nobody had seen him go. He'd been on the mill steps, and then the brazier went over and Callis went down, and then he wasn't on the mill steps. The mill door was shut. Nobody in Millbrook had seen him go through it, or anywhere else.

The knights got Callis up. His collarbone was broken and his face was white and furious. Wynn knelt on the ice and said the setting-word over it, the one the gate knights were licensed for, and Callis swore at him all the way through, and the bone took it. The skater's leg was broken in two places. A child had been burned by the brazier. The bear had got loose in the confusion and gone off across the ice and into the Brackenwood dragging its chain, and the bear-men were going after it with dogs, and nobody tried to stop them.

The fair was over. People were going home. Quietly. In twos and threes, across the ice and up the bank and away along the River Road, not looking at the knights. The braziers were going out one by one. The fiddler had gone. The chestnut-sellers had gone.

But the rings hadn't. She saw them, going away up the bank on the cords round the young men's necks. Out over their coats. Nobody had put them away.

---

The herald came at dusk.

From Ashford, eighteen leagues off, on the third lathered horse he'd had since morning, with the crown's badge on his breast and a scroll in his saddlebag. He came down the River Road into Millbrook as the last of the fair was going home, and reined in at the door of the Kettle, and stood up in his stirrups, and read it. To the empty street. To the few people still standing about. To the knights.

*By order of the crown of Hearthmere, in council with the orders.*

*Whereas there has been put about in the river towns a certain paper, slandering the crown and the knightly orders of Hearthmere with false and wicked tales of beasts beneath the earth.*

*The crown declares the said paper a lie, and a slander, and the work of enemies of the realm who would set the people against their protectors.*

*Any person found to repeat it, print it, post it, or read it aloud shall answer to the magistrate.*

*The gods keep the crown.*

He rolled up the scroll. He looked round at the empty street, at the knights, at the overturned braziers on the ice behind them with the smoke still going up. He turned his horse and rode on toward Greyhold, to read it at the gate.

So the paper had been on doors in Ashford too, before dawn, and the crown had its answer written by noon.

*A lie,* thought Maren.

She stood by the mill holding the horses in the dusk. She thought of a black door, three steps down, at the end of the archive, with no handle and a lock as big as a man's hand. She thought of a line in the old script at the top of a list of three hundred and eleven names. *From the closing.* She thought of Hale's big hand going to the ring of keys on his belt. *The Watch had a name for it.* She thought of the lion on the island, lifting its head, turning it, looking toward Greyhold, while the ground hummed under her like a struck bowl.

*It isn't a lie.*

---

They sang the oath on the wall at midnight.

They did it every midwinter. The whole of Greyhold, every knight and student and instructor and groom, up on the river wall in the dark, facing east toward the river, with torches, singing the oath to the old tune that was only ever used on midwinter night. It was the most beautiful thing she knew. She'd stood on the wall the last two midwinters with her throat aching, singing it.

She stood on the wall that night between Haverly and a second-year whose name she couldn't remember, with a torch in her hand and the frozen river below her, white under the full moon, going away upstream into the dark toward the island. Tobin's place in the line was empty. Callis's was empty. Nobody filled them.

The bell rang. The first voices started, down at the end of the wall, the instructors. Then the rest. The old tune, slow and deep, rising and falling.

*Keep the watch below.*

She opened her mouth and sang it, the way she had every day for two years. The way her father had every day of his life.

*And do not ask.*

The words came out of her mouth and they were different.

They were the same words. The same tune. The same cold air and the same torches and the same river. But they were different in her mouth. Heavier, like a rope when the thing on the end of it wakes.

*A jailer's oath.*

She sang it anyway. Everybody did. Every voice on the wall, all the way along. Louder than she'd ever heard it. As if they were all trying to drown something out.

Far upstream, three leagues and more, on the white river under the moon, past the island and the stump of the tower, the singing went out over the ice and away into the dark. She thought, for a moment, that she could feel it go. Out over the frozen river, toward the Saltreach bank, toward the hills, toward anyone who might be out there in the cold, listening.

*Keep the watch below, and do not ask.*

The singing stopped. All at once, the way it always did, at the end.

In the silence afterwards, Haverly turned his head and looked at her, beside him on the wall, in the torchlight. Kesh was on his fist with her hood off, very still, looking upriver.

Maren looked upriver too.

She thought of a scale wrapped in a shirt at the bottom of her chest, still warm.
