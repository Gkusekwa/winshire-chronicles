---

In the Marches, when a man dies away from home, you bring him back the way he went. Feet first, up the same road, past the same gates, so he knows the way and doesn't wander. Ada said it was nonsense. She said it was the kind of thing people told each other so they'd have something to do with their hands. She did it anyway, every time, for everybody. She stood at the top of the lane with a lantern and waited for the cart.

---

The Ashby girl came on the ninety-eighth day with her mother's basket over her arm and snow on her shawl.

She stood in Bel's kitchen and told it to Joss, not to Calder. Her mother had said to. She was eleven, and she told it all in one breath, looking at the floor: there was a new camp, below Ewe Hollow, at the foot of the valley where the Ewe Beck came down to the Wend. Soup and blankets and grey tents. A carter's boy had been through it and seen. And they had a beast pulling their water-sled up from the beck. A dray-beast. Big. Grey. With a split in its left ear.

Joss had been mending a harness at the table. He put the awl down.

Old Grey's left ear had been split by a gate-hook when he was a yearling. Calder had been there. He'd held the yearling's head while Joss stitched it, and the beast had stood for it with its eyes rolling, and when it was done it had shaken its great head and sprayed them both with blood and walked off to eat grass as if nothing had happened, and Joss had laughed till he had to sit down.

"Split how?" said Joss.

The Ashby girl showed him, with two fingers. A notch, at the tip. Like a swallow's tail.

Joss stood up.

---

"I'll go," he said, that night.

They were at the table. All of them: Bel, Ada, Calder, Joss, the children up in the loft. Snow outside, falling straight down out of a black sky. In the byre through the wall they could hear Mabbin shifting her weight, and the creak of the boards.

"Wait," said Calder.

"For what?"

"I'll think of a way."

"A way." Joss looked at him across the table. He had a big slow kind face, Joss. He'd had it all his life. Calder had never seen him angry, not properly, not once in twelve years of being his brother-in-law. He wasn't angry now. He was tired. "You've been thinking since the summer, Cal."

"It's a camp. There'll be a hundred men in it. And beasts."

"I know there'll be beasts. One of them's mine." Joss turned his cup in his hands. "You brought Mabbin home from Wendmouth. A hundred and twenty leagues on the drove road. On your own, with a price on you." He looked up. "I'm going four. I know the valley. I used to take the wain down to the Ewe Beck mill when I was a lad. I'll go at night and I'll cut him loose and I'll walk him home. He'll know me."

Calder looked at the table.

He thought of the camps on the drove road. The fields behind the soup tents. The hounds on their short chains, and the greyharts, and the wyvern on its perch, and the young men drilling in lines. And the eyes. He thought of the eyes. Like a byre with the door left open and nothing inside.

He hadn't told Joss about the eyes.

He'd come home on the sixty-third night with Mabbin, in the rain, and Joss had stood at the paddock gate with his arms round her neck and his face in her mane and couldn't speak, and Sorrel had come down the paddock to her mother at a run and stopped and put her nose to her mother's face. Calder had stood there in the rain and thought, *Not tonight.* And then *Not tomorrow.* The days had gone. He'd told them about the camps, the soup and the tents and the young men drilling. He'd never told them about the eyes, because to tell them about the eyes he'd have had to say *Old Grey might be like that now,* and he couldn't. He couldn't stand in Bel's kitchen and say it to Joss's face.

"Joss," he said.

Joss waited.

Calder opened his mouth. He thought, *Tell him.* He thought, *Tell him they don't know anybody. Tell him the beast won't know him. Tell him there's somebody inside its head now.*

"Wait," he said. "Two days. Let me think."

Joss looked at him a moment. Then he nodded and picked up his awl, and went back to the harness.

---

He was gone in the morning.

Bel found the peg by the door empty where the good halter hung, and Joss's knife gone from the shelf, and his coat. His boots. The snow had stopped in the night and his track went down the lane in the grey light before dawn, one man's prints, steady, not hurrying, toward the river lane and the south.

Bel stood at the door with her hand on the empty peg.

She didn't look at Calder. She went back in and put the porridge on, and woke the children, and Calder went up the hill.

---

*Find him,* he said. *Find Joss.*

Lisska went.

She went south, low, over the white hills, keeping to the folds of the land where the forest came down, the way he'd told her to go everywhere now since the paper. He felt her go. He sat in the snow on the hill above Bel's, with Aurveth beside him and Kharrak a little way off, and felt her voice thin out as she went, and go. After that there was only the feeling: the cold coming up through the green mark from her wings, and the hills going by under her.

She was back within reach a little after noon, her voice coming in fast before he could see her.

*I found it. Look. Look.*

She pushed it at him, the way she pushed her memories. A long white valley with a frozen beck down the middle of it and the Wend at the end, grey and solid. Tents. Rows of them, grey canvas, laid out in lines on the valley floor. Smoke from the cookfires going up straight in the still air. People. A great many. At the back, in a fenced field on the slope above the beck, the beast lines.

*That one,* said Lisska. *The big one. The grey.*

He saw it. Bigger than he'd remembered, or thinner, standing at the end of the lines with a sled harness on it, by the water-hole they'd cut in the beck ice. Grey, with the white coming through at the muzzle. A head like a barrel. The ear. The notch at the tip, like a swallow's tail.

The others in the line were stamping, blowing, shifting their weight in the cold. Old Grey stood in the snow with his head level, still as a gatepost. A man in grey stood beside him with a hand on his neck.

*His eyes,* said Lisska. Very small. *Calder. His eyes.*

*I know.*

*I couldn't see Joss. I couldn't see him anywhere. I went round and round, high, wide. There were too many. They all look the same from up there. They're all in grey.*

The window closed. She came down on the hill beside him and put her head against his shoulder and shook.

He sat on the hill.

He could go. He could get on Aurveth's neck and go now, in daylight, and be there in an hour, and come down out of the sky into that valley with three dragons. And then what? A camp full of refugees. Families with handcarts. Children. A hundred young men with rings on strings and spears, and a field of beasts that didn't feel pain. He'd burn it, or he'd stand there and be taken. And they'd know where he'd come from. They'd follow the line of his flight back up the river to Upper Rush, to Bel's kitchen, to Wren.

He told himself that. He sat on the hill all afternoon and told himself that, and it was true.

He didn't go.

---

The cart came at noon on the hundredth day.

He saw it from the hill. A small cart, one horse, a dark bay, coming up the river lane from the south at a walk, slow, the way you drive a cart when you don't want to jolt what's in it. Two men walking at the horse's head. A woman sitting on the tail. All three in grey.

He knew before it turned into the lane.

He came down off the hill at a run. He felt the dragons behind him come up off the snow, and he said, *No. Stay. Stay where you are,* and felt Kharrak's tide come up hot and red through the mark, and held it. He didn't know how.

Ada was at the top of the lane with a lantern. In broad noon. She'd seen it from the window and come out and lit it and stood there, at the top of the lane, holding the lantern up.

Bel came out of the house behind her, and stopped.

The cart came up the lane and stopped at the gate. The two men at the horse's head took their caps off. The woman got down from the tail. She was middle-aged, round-faced, with a kind, tired face and a ring on a string round her neck, outside her coat. She looked at Bel.

"Mistress," she said. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

There was a blanket in the cart. A grey one, a camp blanket. There was a man's shape under it. Big. One boot showed at the end, sticking out, with the snow melted on it.

Bel made a sound Calder had never heard a person make.

---

The men told it standing in the lane with their caps in their hands. One did most of the talking. He was young, with a soft beard and a camp man's coat, and he spoke gently, and Calder stood and listened to him and wanted to put his hands round his throat. When the wind came round there was a smell on the man's coat. Sweet and heavy, like honey burned in a pan, with crushed leaves under it. Calder knew it now.

The man came in the night, he said. Into the beast lines. With a knife. Nobody knew who he was. He came over the fence in the dark and went straight down the lines to the big grey at the end and started cutting at its tether. The grey's handler was asleep in the straw by the lines, and he woke, and called out. And the grey took fright.

"Took fright," said Calder.

"Reared." The young man looked at the ground. "He was right under it, cutting at the rope. It came down on him. It was dark. Nobody could have stopped it." He was quiet a moment. "He didn't suffer, mistress. It was quick."

The woman had gone to Bel and was holding her arm. Bel didn't seem to know she was there.

Calder went to the cart and lifted the blanket back.

Joss's face was all right. His face was all right, white and still and surprised, with his eyes shut and snow in his beard. It was his chest. His chest was wrong. It had gone in, the whole left side, in a round deep dent the size of a bucket's mouth, through his coat and his shirt and him. You could see the print of the hoof in the cloth. The edge of the shoe.

A dray-beast, eight feet at the shoulder and more, three times the weight of the biggest horse, coming down with all of it on one forefoot.

*It took fright.*

Bound beasts didn't take fright. He'd seen them. He'd seen a hound at Gallows Quay with a pole through its side walk on toward the man it was told to walk toward. He'd seen the silent dogs on the drove road. They didn't take fright. They didn't do anything at all that the man with his hand on their neck didn't want.

The handler had woken. And called out. And the grey had come down.

He put the blanket back.

"We've brought his knife," said the young man. He held it out, handle first. Joss's knife, with the horn grip worn pale where his thumb went. And the good halter, coiled. "And this. We thought you'd want them."

Calder took them.

"The beast's ours," the young man said. Still gently. "I'm sorry to say it today of all days. But it's ours. Bought fair, at Wendmouth, at the autumn sales. We've the papers. If he'd come to the camp in daylight and asked, we'd have shown him." He looked at Calder, then, properly, for the first time. His eyes went over Calder's face and stopped, and Calder saw him know it.

"You're Fenn," said the young man.

"I knew it was a Fenn farm. Up here. I didn't know…" The young man stopped. Then something came into his face that Calder didn't understand at first. It looked like gladness. Shy, and sorry, and glad. "I'm sorry it's this way. I'm sorry for your brother. Truly." He put his cap back on. "But the one realm grieves with you, Master Fenn. All of us. Whatever's happened. You should know that. Our door's open to you. It always will be."

On the hill above the farm, Calder felt Kharrak come up off the snow.

He felt it before he saw it. The whole red tide of him, all at once, hot, huge, coming up through the mark on his chest so hard that his hand went to it. Kharrak was on his feet on the hill with his wings open and his throat swelling and the heat building in him like a forge with the bellows going, and he was looking down at the lane, at the cart, at the two men in grey with their caps on and the woman with the ring on her string.

*Now,* said Kharrak. *Now. Let me. Let me.*

*No.*

*They killed him. They killed him and they brought him back and they're standing there saying sorry. Let me.*

*No. Not here. Not at Bel's gate. Not in front of the children.*

The children were at the window. He knew without looking. Pip and Nan and Wren, at the kitchen window, with their faces against the glass.

*Calder…*

*No.*

He held it. He stood in the lane with Joss's knife in one hand and the halter in the other and held it, the whole of that red weight, with nothing but his own will, and he felt it push against him and push and push, and he didn't let it through.

Kharrak folded his wings.

The young man was looking at him, puzzled. Calder realised he'd been standing there for a long moment, looking at nothing.

"Go," said Calder. "Now."

They went. They turned the bay round in the lane, and took the cart down to the gate, and lifted Joss out of it, the two men, carefully, and laid him on the snow by the gatepost with the blanket over him, and then they went. Down the lane at a walk, with the woman sitting on the tail of the empty cart, looking back. At the bottom of the lane she lifted her hand to Bel.

Ada stood at the top of the lane with the lantern, and didn't lower it, until they were out of sight.

---

Bel didn't speak to him until dark.

They carried Joss in. Calder and Wat Garrow, who'd come up from Harrow's End at a run when he heard, and old Hollin from the next farm. They laid him on the long table in the kitchen, and Ada and Bel washed him, and the children were sent to the Hollins'. Wren wouldn't go. She sat on the ladder to the loft with her knees up and her arms round them, and nobody had the heart to make her.

At dark Bel came out to the byre, where Calder was standing with Mabbin. He'd gone there because he couldn't stand in the kitchen. Mabbin had her long sad face over his shoulder. Sorrel was in the next stall, very still.

Bel stood in the byre door.

"You knew," she said.

He didn't turn round.

"You knew what was in those camps. You walked past three of them. You came home with Mabbin and you sat at my table and you told us about the soup. The tents. The boys with spears." Her voice was very low and very steady. "And you never told him. Did you? What they do to them. I saw your face when the girl came. I saw it. You knew he'd go in there and Old Grey wouldn't know him."

"Yes," said Calder.

"You let him go."

"I asked him to wait."

"You asked him to wait." She came into the byre. "Two days. For you to think. Like you've been thinking since the summer. Since you came up this lane the night of the raid and walked round in the ashes with the lanterns." She was close now. "I saw you, that night. I was at the door. You went off along the ruts past the light and bent down and picked something up and put it in your coat. I saw you. And you never said."

He turned round.

"What was it?" said Bel. "What did you pick up?"

He looked at his sister. At her face, which was their father's face, square and plain and stubborn, white now, with her eyes red and dry. She'd always been the one who said things. When they were children, when their father died, when Senna died and Calder sat in the dark for a week with a four-year-old and didn't light the fire. Bel had always been the one who stood in the door and said it, whatever it was, straight out, and he'd always been the one who didn't.

"Come inside," he said. "I'll tell you."

---

He told her at the table.

Not the long table, where Joss was. The small one, by the fire. Ada was in her chair by the hearth with her hands in her lap. Bel sat across from him. Wren was on the ladder. He saw her there, in the edge of the firelight, and thought about sending her out, and didn't. She'd been sent out of everything. She'd sat on that ladder for the verse. She could sit on it for this.

He took the chain out of his coat. The five links from the ruts at Upper Rush, in the summer, with the last link cut. He'd carried it every day since. Its weight had worn a hole in the lining of his pocket and he'd sewn it up twice. He laid it on the table between them.

He took the scarf out of the other side of his coat, and unwrapped it, and laid the other one beside it. The arm's length from the island spit. Eight links.

Bel looked at them.

"That's what I picked up," he said.

And then he told her.

He told her all of it. He started at the ruts and he didn't stop. The ring of six on the links. Carrying it to Varrhold in his pocket. The man on the bucket in the lower stable, three paces from the cliff path, the morning Teo said the time and place of the ford out loud for anybody to hear. The six on the man's knife. Gone before light. The ford. Teo in the water, and his knife in its sheath, and the grey on the alders. Saying nothing, at the ford, with the chain in his pocket, while Arn swore. Saying nothing at Varrhold a week ago, and nothing at Skarrow, with the man's name in his mouth.

He told her about the camps. All of it this time. The fields behind the soup tents. The hounds and the greyharts and the wyvern on its perch. The eyes. He made himself say it about the eyes: that there was nobody in them, or that somebody else was. The room behind the red-and-gold building on Gallows Quay, and the smoke, and the young man on his knees with his hand on the hound's head, crying out, and the man in grey saying *You're chosen now.* The hound that walked on with a pole through it.

He told her about the island. The spit, with the stake and the rusted chains. The collar-chain, cut clean at the stake. The arch with the old writing that Aurveth had read the first line of and then stopped. The great round prints in the snow. Something breathing in the dark under the oaks.

He told her about the dragons, and what they'd said, and what they wouldn't say. About Aurveth lying on the ice with her throat to it at midwinter, and getting up sick.

He told her about Ada's verse, though she'd heard it. He told her what he thought it meant.

It took a long time. The fire burned down and Ada got up and put wood on it without a word and sat down again. Bel sat across the table and listened, with her hands flat on the wood the way Ada sat when she sang, and her face didn't change. On the long table behind her, under the clean sheet, Joss lay with his hands folded.

When Calder stopped, his mouth was dry. He'd never said so many words together in his life. He'd never said any of it. Not to her. Not to anyone with a face. Only to the dragons, in the hills, in the autumn, inside his own head, where it didn't count.

He sat there.

He'd thought it would feel like setting down a load. It felt like having walked a long way and turned round and seen how far.

---

Bel looked at the chains.

She reached out and picked up the island length. She turned it over in her hands. It was rusted badly, orange and black, the frost had been at it all winter on the spit. The six was clear, on the outside of each link, but the rest of it was crusted.

"They're not the same," she said. "This one's thicker."

"It's the same mark."

"Lots of people could stamp six dots on a bit of iron." She got up. She went to the hearth and took a handful of cold ash from the edge of it, and came back, and sat down, and took the island chain in her lap and began to scrub it. With the ash and her thumb, hard, the way she'd scrub a black pot. Calder watched her do it. She didn't speak. She scrubbed the last link of the island chain, the one at the end, until the rust came off grey in the ash and the iron underneath came up dark and dull.

She stopped.

She turned the link toward the lamp.

On the inside of it, where the iron curved, where you'd never see it unless you were looking, there was a second mark. Very small. Under the ring of six. Struck with a punch, the way a smith strikes his own sign on his own work.

A hammer. Two hammers. Crossed.

Bel put the island chain down. She picked up the Upper Rush chain, the five links, and found the last one, the one that was cut. She took more ash. She scrubbed. It came up faster: it had been in Calder's pocket, not out in the frost.

She turned it to the lamp.

The ring of six. Under it, on the inside of the curve, small, struck with a punch.

Two hammers, crossed.

She laid them down side by side on the table, the two last links touching, the two little marks turned up to the light.

"Same smith," said Bel.

Calder looked at them.

He'd carried one in his pocket since the summer. He'd carried the other since the night he crossed the ice. He'd never once scrubbed them. The six was enough; the six was what he'd been looking at. He'd never looked underneath.

"The ones who came here," Bel said. "In the summer. Who took Mabbin and Old Grey, and burned the barns. They had chain from the same smith as the ones who chained up that thing on the island."

"Yes."

"The same people."

"Or people who buy from the same man."

"Calder." She looked at him. "The same people."

He looked at the two little hammers in the lamplight, side by side.

"Yes," he said.

---

She didn't forgive him. He hadn't thought she would.

She sat at the table a long while with her hands on either side of the chains. Then she said, without looking up:

"You should have said it in the summer."

"Yes."

"You should have said it to Joss."

"Yes."

"He'd still have gone." She said it very flatly. "He'd have gone anyway. You know he would. He'd have gone in there knowing and he'd have gone anyway, because that's what he was like." Her voice cracked on *was* and she stopped, and put her hand over her mouth, and took it away again. "But he'd have known. He'd have gone in knowing it wouldn't know him. He wouldn't have gone up to it in the dark with his hand out thinking it would put its head down."

Calder couldn't say anything.

"Get him out," said Bel.

He looked at her.

"Old Grey. Don't you leave him in that field. Don't you leave him in there with somebody else in his head." She stood up. She put her hand flat on the table between the two chains. "Joss went to bring him home. Bring him home."

She went to the long table where Joss lay, and sat down on the bench beside him, and put her hand on the sheet over his folded hands, and stayed there.

On the ladder, Wren hadn't moved at all.

---

They buried him on the hundred-and-first day, on the hill above the hollow, Marches fashion, with his feet to the river and his head to the hills, where the Hollins were, and Joss's own father and mother. The ground was iron. Wat Garrow and Calder and old Hollin took turns with the pick all morning to get down far enough. Half of what was left in Harrow's End came up the lane in the snow to stand round. Fisk, on his stick. The Ashby widow, with her girl. Nobody said much. There wasn't a priest; the nearest was at Cotter's Cross and wouldn't come. Ada said the words instead, the old ones, in her low voice, and they all said the last line with her.

Mabbin and Sorrel stood at the top of the paddock, at the gate, side by side, with their heads over it, looking up the hill toward the people. Nobody had brought them. They'd come up the paddock on their own and stood there, the old mare and her daughter, and they stayed there until it was done.

Afterwards, when the others had gone down, Calder stood by the raw mound with Joss's knife in his belt.

Bel had given it back to him. At dawn, without a word. She'd held it out to him handle first, the way the man in grey had, and he'd taken it, and she'd turned away.

On the ridge behind Bel's, half a league off, he felt the dragons. They hadn't come closer. He'd asked them not to. They were lying on the white hillside in a row, the three of them, with their heads up, facing the hollow. They'd been there all morning.

*Tell me when,* said Kharrak.

The red tide had gone down in the night. What was left was quieter, and harder, and very patient, like a dog lying at a rabbit hole.

Calder looked down the long white valley of the Wend, to the south, toward the mouth of the Ewe Beck and the camp at the foot of it.

He'd never chosen anything. Not the dragons. Not the price on his head. Not the paper, with his name at the bottom of it. Everything that had happened to him since the summer had come down out of the sky into his field and lain down round him, and he'd stood in the middle of it and said *I don't know*.

He thought of the young man in the lane, with his cap in his hands and his soft beard and the gladness in his face. *Our door's open to you.*

"The night after the dark of the moon," he said.
