When a dog loses its master, the river people say, it goes back. To wherever they were last together. The kennel, the road, the place on the riverbank where the water took him. It goes back and lies down there and won't be moved, and if you take it away it goes back again, and again, until it dies. Maren has seen it. There was a bitch at Redwatch, a plain watch-dog with no bond in her, when Maren was seventeen and new on the river watch, whose master drowned at the Ottery reach in a spring flood, and for a whole year that dog lay on the bank at the Ottery reach. Ottery fed it. In the end it went into the water.

---

The bell woke her.

It was the hundred-and-third night, a little after midnight. She'd been asleep less than an hour; she'd lain awake after the roar the night before until the morning bell and hadn't slept since. The bell came through the dormitory wall, fast and loud, on and on, and the whole room was out of its cots before it had rung ten strokes.

They went up on the wall. Everybody did. Students in their nightshirts with cloaks thrown over, grooms, knights half-armed, all crowding up the stairs onto the river wall in the cold to see what the bell was for.

It was across the river.

East, on the Saltreach bank, upriver of Tolly's Staithe, below the long bend at Harrow's End. Two leagues, perhaps. A glow on the clouds. Orange, low down, and under it, on the far bank, at the mouth of some valley that came down to the river, fire. A lot of it. A whole hillside burning in the dark, so bright that it lit the ice of the Wend in front of it and threw shadows of the reeds.

"What is it?" somebody said. "What's burning?"

Nobody knew.

Haverly was beside her on the wall. Kesh was on his fist, unhooded, with her feathers up, staring at the glow. He'd brought her up from the mews because she'd screamed when the bell went and wouldn't stop. She'd stopped now. She was very still.

"That's dragon-fire," said Haverly. Quietly. "Nothing else burns that colour. I saw it at Harrow's End in the autumn. It's white at the root."

Nobody on the wall said anything to that. Nobody said the name.

Maren stood on the wall in the cold and watched the hillside burn.

---

Rowan sent for her in the morning.

It was the first time since the trial. Since the long bare room at the top of the north tower, with the ledger and the fire and the window over the river, and Rowan writing something in the margin and not telling her what. Maren climbed the stair behind the boy who'd brought the message, up past the second floor, up the narrow winding last flight so tight you had to go sideways, and knocked.

"Come."

Rowan was at the window. She didn't turn round. She was looking out over the river toward the island, and then across the water, toward the black smear on the far bank where the hillside had burned. It was still smoking. You could see it from here, a long grey stain on the white.

"Ashdown," she said.

"Captain."

Rowan turned round. She looked the same as always. Small and neat in her grey coat buttoned to the throat, with her iron-grey hair cut short and her quiet face that never moved. She looked at Maren for a long moment.

"You'll go to Redwatch," she said. "Today. With Pell's patrol. Pell is to watch the island from the tower, day and night, and from the ice, and to report to me what's on it and what it does." She paused. "You'll go closer than anybody else. You've been closer than anybody else since the summer. Everybody in this school knows it, and so do I. You'll go as close as you've been, and you'll tell me what it does."

Maren waited.

"You'll not go further than you've been," said Rowan. "Not one step. You understand me?"

"Yes, Captain."

Rowan looked at her.

She didn't say why. She didn't say anything about a roar through the stone, or a chain, or three dragons answering across the ice, or a hillside burning. She didn't ask Maren whether she'd heard any of it. She stood at the window with her hands behind her back and her quiet face, and Maren stood in front of her and didn't ask, and the not-asking stood in the room between them like a third person.

"Go and get your horse," said Rowan.

---

Hale sent for her before she could.

She was in the Long Barn saddling Turnip when the boy came. *The Warden.* She tied Turnip short and went across the yard and down the north tower stair, and the cat wasn't on the bottom step. It hadn't been there since the night of the door.

Hale was at the long table, under the brass lamp, with the black book open in front of him at the very front, before the first page, where the boards were. He'd opened it wide, so wide the spine cracked, and he had a little reading glass in his hand that she'd never seen him use.

"Come here," he said. "Look."

She came round the table.

He'd opened it at the place he'd told her about. Where the oldest pages had been, about the closing. You could see them now, with the book bent back so far. The stubs. A row of them, in the gutter of the binding, where somebody had taken a knife to the pages long ago and cut them out close to the thread. Narrow brown strips of old paper, a finger wide, no more, the sewing still through them.

"I've been looking at them for forty years," said Hale. "I looked at them again last night. After you'd gone. After Thorne." He gave her the glass. "The fourth one. At the bottom. Look at the corner."

She bent over it with the glass.

The fourth stub was a little wider than the others. Whoever had cut it had let the knife wander at the bottom. In the bottom corner of it, where the knife had wandered, there was ink. A fragment. A curve, drawn in brown, and another curve, and lines.

The edge of a drawing.

She bent closer.

It was very small, what was left. A corner. But it had been drawn large, whatever it was; you could tell from the curve of the lines, the way they ran off the edge of the stub into the place where the rest of the page had been. Four curves. Side by side, splayed. And at the front of each, a hook, drawn in, sharp.

A forefoot. Toes, and claws, drawn in.

Under it, along the very bottom of the stub, half a word in the old script. She could read it now, a little. Hale had taught her. It was the end of a word, cut off at the front.

*…kin.*

"I don't know what it is," said Hale. "A beast's foot. Something drawn very big. I've never seen it before. I've been looking at those stubs for forty years and I never once looked at the bottom of the fourth." He was watching her. "Do you know what it is?"

She'd put her own hand in those prints. In the mud by the stream in the autumn, and in the snow at midwinter. She'd knelt in the snow on the island and laid her hand flat in one of them, and it hadn't been big enough to fill the pad. Four toes, splayed. The claws drawn in at the front.

She gave him back the glass.

"No," she said.

---

She rode to Redwatch that afternoon at the back of Pell's patrol, on Turnip, in the snow, and couldn't stop thinking about it.

*…kin.*

The end of a word. It could be anything. It could be half a dozen words in the old script; Hale had said so, once, about another stub, that the old tongue built its words from the back. It didn't have to mean what it meant in her own tongue.

She thought about it all the way along the frozen River Road, with the island going by on her right, low and dark on the white ice, and the oaks on it bare and black.

*A thing that was sleeping comes to the old place, and will not leave it.*

It had been there in the summer, asleep in the hollow at the north end when the hunters came. Asleep for how long? Nobody knew. The river had always told stories about the Old Ruin. *Something lives there.* Nobody had ever seen it. The hunters had come with their honey-smoke and their chains, and woken it.

It hadn't left. She'd thought it was hurt, at first. Later, that it was frightened. In the autumn, when it lay in the arch of the tower like a dog across a threshold, she'd thought it was keeping something. Guarding it.

She'd never asked herself what.

*Does it dig?* Hale had said. In the autumn, at the midden, with his stick in the straw. She'd said: the hollow at the north end. The earth banked up. *Like a thing that's been dug, and filled, and dug again.*

He'd gone quiet.

---

Pell put them in the Redwatch tower and told them what Rowan wanted. Two on the top of the tower day and night, with the glass. Two on the ice at the island's west shore at dusk and dawn. Nobody to set foot on the island itself.

"Except Ashdown," said Pell. He looked at her across the guardroom. "Captain's orders. You go as close as you've been. No further." He paused. "And you come back."

"Yes, sir."

"And you tell me what you see," said Pell. "All of it."

She said, "Yes, sir," again.

She went at dusk on the hundred-and-fourth day. Ottery walked her across the ice, the way he always did, with his pole and his lantern, and Pell and two of the patrol stood at the edge of the reeds on the Hearthmere side behind them and watched her go. She felt them watching all the way across. At the island's west shore Ottery sat down on the ice and she went on alone into the trees.

The lion wasn't at the stream.

It wasn't in the arch. She went to the tower and stood in front of the doorway under the verse in the last of the light, and the arch was empty, and the snow in front of it was smooth.

The ground hummed.

She stood still and listened to it. It came up through her boots. Under it, faint, from the north, from somewhere past the stream and up the island toward the bare oaks, she heard something else.

A sound like a spade. Like a great many spades. Earth, being moved. Stone grating on stone.

She went north.

---

She found it in the hollow.

She'd known she would. She'd seen the hollow in the summer, on her first night, before the hunters: a long shallow dip at the north end of the island among the oaks, with banks of raw earth round it. In the autumn it had been deeper. She'd noticed and not thought about it. She hadn't been up to the north end since the freeze.

It wasn't a hollow any more.

It was a pit. The size of a barn floor, and deep. Deeper than a man standing on another man's shoulders. Dug straight down through the frozen ground, through the black topsoil and the yellow clay under it and into something else, something grey and loose and old, that wasn't clay at all. Fill. The kind of earth you get when somebody's dug a hole a long time ago and filled it in again. The banks round the edge of the pit were piled higher than her head, frozen hard, with great claw-marks raked down the inside of them.

And at the bottom of the pit, in the grey fill, the lion was digging.

She stood at the top of the bank in the dusk and watched it.

It dug the way a dog digs, with its forefeet, one and then the other, throwing the earth back under its belly in great sprays. But it stood ten feet at the shoulder, and its forefeet were each as broad as a round shield, and every stroke tore out more earth than she could have shifted in an hour with a spade. The ground answered it. She felt it. Each time a forefoot went in, the ground round the pit shook, and the frozen banks shed little falls of earth, and the hum came up through her boots stronger. The earth was helping it. Moving for it. Loosening ahead of its claws like snow on a roof before it slides.

It didn't look up. It didn't know she was there, or it didn't care. It dug.

At the bottom of the pit, under its forefeet, there was stone.

---

She went down.

She didn't decide to. She found herself going sideways down the raw bank on her heels with her hand on the frozen earth, the way you'd go down into a quarry. Rowan had said *no further than you've been.* She'd never been here. She went down anyway.

She stopped on a ledge of grey fill a few paces above the bottom, not far from the lion, and crouched there, and looked.

The stone was dressed. Square blocks, cut and set, fitted close. Grey. Old. The edges worn round. Each block the size of a cart. She knew them. She'd looked at them every noon for two years in the yard at Greyhold without ever seeing them, and then, a week ago, in the noon light, she'd seen them. The same stone. The same grey, the same size, the same frost in the seams.

The inner wall.

The lion had dug down through the fill and found the top of something built of the same stone as the inner wall at Greyhold. A long low course of it, running away under the earth at the bottom of the pit. South and west. Under the river.

Toward Greyhold.

It was scraping at the stone with its claws. Slowly, now. Long strokes, one and then the other, the claws grating on the old grey blocks, as if it could get through them by wanting to. It couldn't. The stone didn't give. There were pale scratches all over the top of it, hundreds of them, where it had been scraping at it for days. For weeks. All winter.

The pit hadn't been dug, and filled, and dug again by anybody else. It had been dug by the lion. Over and over. In the summer before the hunters came. In the autumn. Now. Every time it came back to the old place and dug.

She crouched on the ledge and watched it scrape, and understood.

---

It wasn't guarding anything.

It had never been guarding anything. It had come to the old place, the way the dog at the Ottery reach came back to the bank. It wouldn't leave. It lay in the arch of the tower under the verse with its chin on the threshold, *the last sleeps below the stone,* and it looked at Greyhold, and when she'd put her hand on the black door and something far down had moved, it had roared.

And something far down had answered.

*A forefoot. Four toes, splayed. The claws drawn in.*

*…kin.*

It was trying to get down.

Under the stone, under the river, a long way down in the dark below the hill at Greyhold, there was something chained. Six chains, each link taller than a man. Something so big that her hand on the door had been a fly on the skin of a sleeper. Something the first knights had found asleep, and woken, and chained before it was awake enough to know what had been done to it. Three hundred years. Something with a forefoot like this one.

This one had come to the old place, and was trying to dig its way down to it through the stone with its claws, all winter, in the dark, alone.

Its own. Its kin. Its kind, or its blood, or whatever the word was for what a beast like this one had. She didn't know. She only knew that it was down there, and that the lion knew it was down there, and that the lion had been scraping at the stone over it since before she'd ever come to the island.

And she knew who'd chained it.

She did. Her kingdom. Her orders. The first knights on the hill, who were the first of *her.* Three hundred people in the yard at noon, turning east, with their voices on the chain. Her father, who'd sworn it every day of his life. Herself, for two years.

*Keep the watch below.*

---

The lion stopped scraping.

It stood at the bottom of the pit with its forefeet on the old grey stone and its head down, breathing hard. Its flanks were going like a bellows. Steam went up off it in the dusk, off its back and its great grey mane. She could see the collar, black iron, round its neck under the mane. The raw band. The scratches on the stone under its feet, hundreds of them, pale.

It turned its head, and looked at her.

It had known she was there. Of course it had. It had known from the moment she came through the oaks. It turned its great broad scarred head and looked at her with its pale eyes across the grey earth, in the bottom of the pit it had dug, with its feet on the stone over its kin, and it didn't move toward her and it didn't move away.

She looked back.

She'd been afraid of it for months. It had broken her ribs. It had stood over her with its foot on her chest. She'd stopped being afraid of it some time in the autumn, between the arch and the ice.

She couldn't look at its feet. She made herself.

"We did it," she said.

Out loud. Into the pit. Her voice sounded thin and strange in the cold.

"We did that. To yours. The people I belong to. They found it asleep and they woke it up and they chained it, and they've kept it chained for three hundred years, and they light their lamps with it. And I swore to keep it there. Every day. I said the words every day for two years." Her voice was shaking. "I didn't know. That's not… that doesn't make it different. I didn't know, and I said them anyway. I'm sorry."

The lion looked at her.

It couldn't understand a word. It stood in the bottom of the pit with its feet on the stone and looked at her, with the steam going up off its back, and she couldn't tell what it was thinking. She'd never been able to tell. There was no thread between them. Nothing for anything to go down.

It looked at her.

It turned back to the stone, and lowered its head, and began to scrape again. One forefoot, then the other. The claws on the old grey blocks.

She stayed.

She sat on the ledge of grey fill in the bottom of the pit, in the dark, while it scraped. She didn't light the lantern. She didn't go. She sat with it in the cold with her knees drawn up and listened to its claws on the stone, and the ground humming, and once, so far down she wasn't sure she'd heard it, iron drawing over stone, link over link.

The lion stopped when it heard that. It went very still, with its head down against the stone, listening.

Then it threw itself at the stone. Both forefeet, all its weight, and the ground came with it. On the far side of it from her, the fill heaved up into a hump as high as its shoulder, and the hump had a shape. A back. A head, bowed to the stone the way the lion's was. For the space of a breath there were two of them at the bottom of the pit, one of grey hide and one of grey earth.

Then the earth fell in on itself and ran back down into fill, and there was only the lion, with its flanks going.

She stayed where she was on the ledge. The bank had slumped, she told herself. Banks did, when you shook them hard enough. She'd seen a cutting slump on the River Road in a thaw once, and for a moment, in the rain, it had looked like a man.

The lion went on scraping.

She sat with it until Ottery's lantern came swinging through the oaks at full dark, calling her name.

---

Her report said the beast kept to the north end of the island, and dug.

That was true. Every word of it. She wrote it at the guardroom table in the Redwatch tower by candlelight. On the shelf over the hearth sat the warming-stone Ottery had put in her lap after the ice. It had been cold a fortnight, and nobody had moved it. Pell read it over her shoulder and nodded and initialled it and sent it to Greyhold with the morning rider.

It didn't say anything about the stone.

She lay on the guardroom floor that night in her cloak, by the fire, and thought about that. The lie to Pell at the Lesson Wall about the prints and the cut chain and the scale under her cot. *Nothing. Only the lion.* *I don't know,* to Hale, when he asked about the farmer. And now this. A pit the size of a barn floor and a course of old grey stone at the bottom of it running under the river toward Greyhold, and a beast's foot on a cut stub, and she'd written *it digs* and nothing else.

Three things. She'd never kept anything from anybody in her life. Not from Liss. Not from Pell. Not from the river watch. She'd been the one who wrote fourteen pages about a roadblock because every move mattered.

She was a liar now. She lay there in the dark and made herself think the word.

She thought of the lion standing in the pit with its feet on the stone and its head down, listening to its kin move in the dark under a mile of rock, and she thought: *Rowan's not having that. Not that. Not from me.*

---

On the hundred-and-fifth night they saw the fire on the far bank.

She was on the top of the Redwatch tower with Pell and the glass, at the end of the second watch, a little before midnight. The ice ran away below them white under the stars toward the Saltreach shore, and the island lay dark in the middle of it, and beyond the island, on the far bank, the hills above Harrow's End rose up black.

The fire came out of the hills.

High up. Above the village, inland, where the farms went up toward the forest. A sudden long tongue of it, white at the root, orange at the end, going across the side of a hill in the dark and lighting everything under it. Then another, from somewhere else. Then a third.

"Look," said Pell. "There. And there."

She had the glass. She looked.

Fire on the hillside, three tongues of it, from three places, coming and going in the dark. Under the fire, in its light, small and black and scattering, men. A lot of men. With torches. Coming up the hill in a line from the river lane, toward a farm, or a place, she couldn't tell, in the hills above Harrow's End. The fire going across in front of them, and the line breaking. Torches going everywhere. Torches dropped in the snow. Men running.

And over them, against the glow, for a moment, a shape. Huge. Wings. Going over low and turning, and fire coming out of it.

"Dragons," said Pell. He took the glass from her and looked. "Three. Dragon-fire. They're fighting." He lowered it. "Somebody's come for him in the night. After the Ewe Beck. With a hundred men."

Ottery had come up the stair behind them. He stood at the parapet in his old coat with his pipe, unlit, and looked across the ice at the far hills burning.

"Aye," he said. "And they're not getting him."

They watched it. The fire coming and going in the hills. The torches scattering down the slopes toward the river lane, and stopping, and going out, one by one. Then only the fire, dying down on the snow in long black-and-orange smears. Then nothing.

On the Hearthmere bank nobody moved. The patrol on the ice below the tower sat their horses at the edge of the reeds and watched. Up and down the river, at every tower, the lanterns burned, oil ones now, every one, and nobody went out on the ice, and nobody crossed.

Pell went down to the guardroom and wrote it in the report. *Fire on the Saltreach bank above Harrow's End, near midnight. Dragon-fire, three sources. A body of men, perhaps a hundred, with torches, put to flight.* He wrote it neatly, in his square hand, the way he wrote everything.

Maren stayed on the tower.

She looked at the far hills, where the last of the fire was going out on the snow. Above Harrow's End. Where three dragons had answered a roar across the ice two nights ago. Where a man in Marches boots had walked home across the river after kneeling by her fire. Where somebody had just tried to take him in the night with a hundred men, and the dragons had burned the hillside to stop them.

*They've come for him.*

She thought of the hillside at the mouth of the Ewe Beck burning, two nights ago. Somebody had answered that.

She looked down at the island, dark in the middle of the white ice, between the two banks. At the north end of it, among the oaks, where she couldn't see it, there was a pit with old stone at the bottom. She thought she could hear it. She couldn't. It was half a league off, across the ice. But she stood on the top of the Redwatch tower with her hands on the frozen parapet and thought she could hear claws on stone, one and then the other, all night, in the dark.
