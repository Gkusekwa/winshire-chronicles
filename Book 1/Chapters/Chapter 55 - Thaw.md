On the Wend they don't say the ice breaks. They say *the river's going out*, as if it had been home all winter, sitting by the fire, and has got up one morning and put on its coat and gone. Liss says you'll know it when it happens. You'll be asleep, she says, and it'll wake you, and you'll lie in the dark and think the world's ending. And then you'll remember, and go back to sleep, and in the morning there'll be water again.

---

She went every dusk. Ottery walked her over. The ice was still a hand and a half thick in the channel, and the river under it was black and silent, and the lion was always in the pit.

It didn't stop. Each time she came through the oaks at the north end of the island she heard it before she saw it: claws on stone, one and then the other. Each time she went down the frozen bank on her heels and sat on the ledge of grey fill, and it turned its head and looked at her, and went back to the stone. The scratches on the old grey blocks went deeper, a little each night. A hair's breadth. Pale lines in the grey, crossing and recrossing.

She sat with it, and didn't speak much now.

On the hundred-and-fifteenth day she brought the hammer and chisel.

She'd thought about it since the autumn. Since the stream, and its foot on her chest, and the hammer-butt in the soft of its nose. *She'd come at its neck with iron.* She'd thought about it every night on her cot with the scale and Tobin's sword in the chest under her. She'd thought: *not like that. Not ever like that again.*

She carried them over the ice wrapped in an old shirt, the way they'd lain in the chest. At the pit she didn't go down. She stood at the top of the bank, where the lion could see her, and took them out of the shirt and held them up. The hammer in one hand and the chisel in the other. So it could see what they were.

It stopped scraping.

It turned round in the bottom of the pit and looked at her. Its lip went back. A long way back, off the great yellow teeth, and the ground under her boots went hard and still, the way it went before it came at her. She stood there with the iron held up and didn't move.

She put them down. On a flat stone at the edge of the pit, on the shirt. She stepped back from them. Three paces. Five. Ten.

She sat down in the snow.

The lion came up out of the pit.

It came slowly, up the raw bank, with its lip still back, and stood over the hammer and the chisel on their shirt, and put its head down to them, and smelled them. A long time. Long slow breaths, in and out, so that the shirt stirred under them. She could hear it from where she sat.

It didn't look at her.

At last it lifted its head, and its lip came down, and it turned away and went back down into the pit, and began to scrape again.

She left them there. On the stone, on the shirt. She left them there every night after that, and every night when she came back they were where she'd left them, and the snow round them was trodden flat by great round feet.

---

On the hundred-and-seventeenth night the moon was full and the wind went round to the south.

She felt it on the tower at Redwatch, at the change of the watch. One moment the air was still and cold and dry, as it had been since the river stopped. Then it moved. Soft. Wet. From the south, up the river, from the sea, smelling of salt and mud and rain. It came over the parapet into her face and she stood there and breathed it.

By morning it was raining.

It rained all day. Warm, heavy, steady rain, out of a low grey sky, onto the snow. The snow went soft, and then grey, and then it was gone off the roofs and running in the gutters, and the yard at Redwatch was a lake of slush. The river ice went grey too. By noon it had a skin of water lying on it, a hand deep, grey and still under the rain, from bank to bank.

Ottery stood on the landing at Redwatch in his oilskin with the rain running off his hat brim and looked at it.

"Three days," he said. "Maybe two."

---

On the hundred-and-nineteenth day he said no.

She found him at the boathouse at dusk, with the punt pulled up on the slip and the pole in his hand, and he looked at her coming down the landing in her cloak and shook his head.

"Not tonight, Ashdown."

"It held last night."

"It held last night. It'll not hold tomorrow. Listen to it." He pointed the pole at the river.

She listened.

The ice was making a sound. She'd heard it all day without knowing she was hearing it. A long low groaning, far off, from upriver, rising and falling, like a great beast in pain. And under it, closer, little sharp cracks, here and there, out on the grey surface, like twigs breaking in a fire. The water on top of it had gone dark in long lines, where the ice underneath was opening.

"It's going," said Ottery. "Tonight or tomorrow. When it goes, it goes all at once, from up under the Scarfell all the way down to the sea, and you don't want to be on it. You don't want to be near it."

She stood on the landing in the rain.

She thought of the pit. The hammer and chisel on their shirt on the flat stone. The lion scraping. She thought of the ice going out, and the island cut off by the floes for days, maybe a week, until the river was clear enough for the punt. A week of it in the pit alone with nobody coming.

"I'm going," she said.

Ottery looked at her with the rain running off his hat.

He swore. Long and low and thoroughly, in the river-watch way, as her father used to, at her and the river and the rain and the Captain and the Watch and everything in between. He picked up the lantern. He set the foot of the pole on the ice below the slip and said the ice-word over it, under his breath, the one the river watch had always said before going out. Liss said it had never held up anybody who wasn't going to stay up anyway. He said it twice.

"To the reeds," he said. "And not one step past. And when I shout, you come. You come running. You hear?"

---

She was in the pit when it went.

She'd gone down the bank in the rain in the last of the light. The lion was there, at the bottom, at the stone. It had stopped scraping and stood very still, with its head up, and its ears forward, listening. Upriver. It didn't look at her when she came down. It was listening to something she couldn't hear yet.

She crouched on her ledge and listened too.

The groaning had stopped.

The long low groaning from upriver that had gone on all day. It had stopped. There was only the rain, hissing on the grey fill and the black water lying in the bottom of the pit. The hum in the ground, very faint. Far off, from the north, from somewhere beyond the Scarfell, from a long, long way up the river, a sound.

It didn't sound like anything she'd ever heard. It sounded like the wall of a city coming down. A mile of wall. All at once. Not one crash but a great long roaring tearing grinding rush of crashes, all running together, and coming nearer. Coming down the river. Fast. Faster than a horse could gallop. Faster than she could believe anything could come.

The lion's head went round toward it.

Then it was on them.

---

She felt it through the ground before she heard it properly. The whole island jumped. The frozen bank of the pit beside her shed a great fall of earth into the water at the bottom. The sound came over her like a wave, so loud she couldn't hear it any more, only feel it, in her chest, in her teeth, in the ledge of fill under her, a roaring and cracking and booming that went on and on and came from everywhere at once, from the west, from the east, from both sides of the island, as the ice of the Wend broke from bank to bank and heaved itself up and went.

She scrambled up the bank. To see. To get to Ottery. She got to the top on her hands and knees in the mud and the rain and stood up among the bare oaks and looked west through the trees toward the channel and the Hearthmere bank.

The river was moving.

All of it. The whole grey plain of ice she'd walked across every dusk since the eighty-first night, the white road Ottery had tested with his pole, was moving. Broken into pieces, slabs, some as small as a table, some as big as the Long Barn, grinding and heaving and turning over, piling up on each other, and going. South. Down the river. Faster than she could run. Slabs reared up on end in the dark and stood for a moment taller than a house and went over with a crash that shook the island. Black water showed between them, and was gone, and showed again. The whole channel between the island and the Hearthmere bank was a moving wall of broken ice, grinding past.

She couldn't see the reeds. She couldn't see Ottery.

She shouted. She couldn't hear herself shout.

Through the roaring, from the far side of the channel, faint, she heard him. A long hail. The river-watch call. *Hoy… oy…* She'd heard it a thousand times on patrol. *I'm here. I'm safe. Stay where you are.*

He'd got back to the reeds. He'd got off the ice before it went. He was on the far side of the west channel and all its moving floes, and so was the rest of the world.

She stood among the oaks in the rain and shook.

Behind her the lion came up out of the pit.

---

She went to the tower.

She didn't know where else to go. The pit was filling with water. The oaks were dripping and black. The noise of the river went on and on, on both sides of the island, east and west, grinding, booming, so that you couldn't hear anything else and couldn't think. She went south along the stream in the dark with her lantern, slipping in the mud, to the old broken tower in its ring of stones, and into the arch, under the verse, out of the rain.

She sat down in the arch with her back against the old stone.

She'd been in it before, at the edge, but never sat in it. It was where the lion lay. The stone under her was worn into a long smooth hollow by its body, and the hollow was dry, and it smelled of it. Warm fur and earth and something like hot stones. She sat in the hollow it had worn and pulled her wet cloak round her and set the lantern on the step beside her and listened to the river going out.

The lion came out of the dark.

It came along the stream from the north, slowly, through the rain, a huge dark shape against the dark trees, and stopped in front of the arch. Ten paces off. Where it always stopped. It stood there in the rain, with the water running off its mane, and looked at her sitting in its place.

She stayed where she was.

"I'm sorry," she said. "It's your place. I'll go if you want."

It couldn't understand her. She said it anyway.

It lay down.

In the mud, in the rain, facing the arch. It put its great head on its forepaws and looked at her, and the river roared, and the rain came down, and the lantern hissed on the step.

---

She didn't know when it moved closer.

She must have slept. She hadn't meant to. She'd been sitting in the arch with her knees up and her cloak round them, watching it, and the noise of the river had gone on so long it had become a kind of silence, and her eyes had closed. When they opened the lantern had burned low and the rain had eased to a drizzle, and the lion was closer.

Five paces.

It was lying across the front of the arch now, in the mud at the foot of the step. Its head was on its forepaws, turned toward her. Its eyes were open, catching the lantern light, pale. She could see its breath going out in long slow clouds. She could hear it breathe. She could feel the warmth of it, from five paces, coming off its great flank in the cold like the warmth off a banked kiln.

She sat very still.

It lay there. After a while its eyes closed.

She watched it sleep. The rain dripping off the arch. The river grinding past in the dark on both sides, a little quieter now, the great slabs gone through and the smaller stuff following, rushing and knocking. The lion's flank going up and down.

Then its eyes opened again, and it lifted its head, and looked at her, and put its head down again.

Closer.

She didn't see it move. She'd looked down at the lantern, and when she looked up it was there. Its great head was lying on the step of the arch, on the old worn stone, an arm's length from her knee. Its eyes were open. Its breath came out warm against her wet boots.

She didn't breathe.

She'd been this close once before. On the shore, in the summer, with the hunters' chain on it, when it had brought its head down until its face was an arm's length above hers and looked at her with one eye and then the other. She'd been on her back then. She'd thought she was going to die.

She wasn't going to die.

She put out her hand.

Slowly. So slowly. The way you'd put your hand out to a horse you didn't know, in a dark stall, palm down, fingers loose. Not toward its face. Toward its shoulder, where the mane began, behind the great head, where it ran thick and grey and wet over the top of the neck.

It watched her hand come.

Its lip didn't go back. The ground didn't go hard. It lay with its head on the step and its eyes on her hand and let it come, all the long way, a hand's breadth, half a hand, and she laid it down.

On its mane.

---

It was warm. Warm and wet and coarse, coarse as a horse's tail, thick, so thick her fingers went into it to the knuckle and didn't find the bottom. Under it, the great slab of muscle at the top of the shoulder, hard as wood, rising and falling with its breath.

Under that, through it, faint, the hum.

The ground's hum. The thing she'd felt through her boots on the island all winter, and through the stones of the archive, and through the black door. It was in the lion. It came up through its body into her hand as it had come up through the oak of the door. Not the same. The door had been something vast turning its attention, cold and far and terrible. This was close. This was warm. This was a beast lying in the rain with its head on a step, with the earth moving in it the way blood moves.

Nothing else came. No voice. No thread. Nothing like Haverly and Kesh, or her father and his hound. It didn't speak to her. She'd never hear it. There was nothing between them but her hand in its mane and the hum coming up through her palm.

It let her.

That was all. It let her leave her hand there.

She left it. She sat in the arch with her hand in the lion's mane and her heart going so hard it hurt, and the lion lay with its head on the step and its eyes half closed, and the river went out in the dark on either side of them, and neither of them moved.

Later she found she was crying. She didn't stop it. She sat there with the tears running down her face and her hand in its mane, and once the lion opened its eyes and looked at her, and closed them again.

She stayed. It stayed.

The lantern went out. She didn't relight it.

---

At dawn the rain had stopped.

The river was still running with ice on both sides. Floes, now, not slabs. Grey and white and broken, going past fast on the brown flood water, knocking, turning. But there was black water between them. Open leads. The worst of it had gone through.

She opened her eyes in the arch, stiff and cold, with her cloak soaked and her hand still in the lion's mane. She'd slept with it there. It had slept too. It opened its eyes when she moved.

She took her hand away. Slowly.

She got up. Her legs had gone dead and she nearly fell, and caught herself on the arch. She went out past the lion's head, round it, an arm's length from its jaws, and it watched her go, and she walked north along the stream in the grey light, to the pit.

It was half full of water now. Brown, still, with the grey stone under it. The hammer and the chisel were on their flat stone on the shirt, at the edge, where she'd left them, wet with the rain.

She picked them up.

She stood there holding them a long moment.

Then she went back.

---

The lion was still lying in front of the arch. It lifted its head when she came round the tower. It saw what she had in her hands.

She stopped. Ten paces.

She knelt down in the mud. She laid the shirt out flat in front of her and put the hammer and the chisel on it, side by side, the way she'd put them on the stone at the pit. She took her hands off them. She put her hands on her knees, open, empty, where it could see them.

She waited.

The lion looked at the iron.

It got up. It came across the ten paces and stood over her. She knelt in the mud and looked up at it, at the great scarred face, at the pale eyes. At the collar. Black iron, round its neck, under the grey mane, with the raw band of skin under it where it had rubbed all winter, and the short broken end of chain hanging from it. And at the front of it, under the jaw, the hinge, and the pin, hammered over, the way the hunters had hammered it while their hands shook.

It looked at her. Then it lowered its head.

It lowered its whole great head and neck, down, the way a horse puts its head down to be haltered, until its jaw was lying on the mud beside her knee, and its neck was stretched out along the ground in front of her, and the collar was there. The pin. A hand's breadth from her hands.

She picked up the chisel.

Her hands were shaking. She held the chisel against her knee until they stopped. She set the point of it to the head of the pin, where the hunters had peened it over, and picked up the hammer.

*If I'm wrong,* she thought, *I'll be dead before I feel it.*

She struck.

The ring of iron on iron went out across the island and the river. The lion flinched. The whole length of it. She felt the ground under her knees jump. It didn't move its head.

She struck again.

The pin moved. A hair. She saw it. She set the chisel again and struck a third time, hard, with everything she had, and the peened head of the pin sheared off and the pin went through and out the other side of the hinge and dropped in the mud.

The collar fell open.

It fell off the lion's neck in two halves, on its hinge, heavy, and lay in the mud between them with the broken chain still hanging off it.

---

The lion stood up.

It stood up all at once, to its full height over her, ten feet at the shoulder and more, and shook itself. From its head down to its tail, the way a dog shakes coming out of water, the great mane flying out round its head in a spray. And the ground shook with it. The whole island. The old tower behind it groaned and a stone came down off the top of the broken wall into the stream. She was thrown sideways in the mud.

It didn't look at her. It turned away and walked down the bank of the stream to the river, to the edge of the brown flood water where the floes were going past, and put its head down, and drank.

She lay in the mud and watched it.

It drank and drank. It lifted its head, with the water running off its jaw, and stood on the shore in the grey dawn with no collar on its neck, looking out across the river at the far bank.

She sat up. She looked at the collar lying in the mud in front of her.

The inside of it was black and greasy with the lion's fur and old blood. She picked it up. It was heavy, much heavier than she'd thought. She turned it over. On the outside, at the hinge, the ring of six, the way it was on every link. She'd known that. She turned it to the inside, to the curve where it had lain against the lion's neck, and rubbed at the grease with her thumb.

Under the six, on the inside of the curve, small, struck with a punch, there was a second mark: two hammers, crossed.

She knew it.

She'd known it all her life. It was on every horseshoe on the Millbrook road. On the pot-hook over Liss's fire. On the hinges of the ferry-house door. On the iron rings on the Kettle's yard gate, where Davin had stood splitting kindling when the knights came. Two little crossed hammers, struck neat and small, on every bit of iron that came out of the forge at the top of the Millbrook street.

Odo Marl's mark.

Tam's master.

She sat in the mud with the lion's collar in her lap and Odo Marl's two little hammers under her thumb, and the river going out round them both.

---

Ottery came for her at noon.

She saw the punt coming a long way off, nosing through a lead of open water between the floes on the west channel, with Ottery standing in the stern poling, and Pell sitting in the bow with a boathook fending off the ice. They'd waited all night on the reeds and all morning at Redwatch for the river to open enough. She stood on the shore below the tower and watched them come.

The lion had gone back to the pit. She could hear it from the shore. Claws on stone.

The punt came in through the reeds and grounded on the gravel. Ottery stepped out into the shallows and stood there and looked at her. At the mud all over her, and her soaked cloak, and the hammer and chisel in her belt, and the collar in her hands.

He didn't say anything.

He'd seen the lion. Coming in. She knew he had, because his face had gone the way it had gone in the autumn, the night he pulled her out of the stream with the boathook, the night he saw it on the bank ten feet at the shoulder in the dusk. He'd seen it at the edge of the oaks, going north, without the collar.

He took the collar out of her hands, and put it in the bottom of the punt, and handed her in.

All the way back to Redwatch, half a league that took them an hour through the floes, he didn't say a word. Nor did Pell.

---

At Redwatch, in the guardroom, Pell had a paper.

The morning rider had brought it from Millbrook with the Greyhold dispatches while they were out on the river, and left it on the guardroom table, under the old lamp on its bracket that had given nothing but a red glow since midwinter, beside the tallow candle they read by now. Pell picked it up when they came in, all three of them dripping, and read it, and pushed it across to her without a word.

It was a printed sheet. Not the old one, the trading houses' bill out of Wendmouth, with two hundred silver on him for the beasts at Gallows Quay. This one had the ring of six at the top. The six hands. The Restoration's sheet, from the same press as the paper on the river gate. Somebody had nailed it to the Millbrook jetty in the night.

TWO HUNDRED SILVER, it said.

FOR CALDER FENN, THE FARMER, OF HARROW'S END ON THE SALTREACH BANK. ENEMY OF THE ONE REALM. WHO CAME IN THE NIGHT WITH FIRE TO THE CAMP AT THE EWE BECK, WHERE THE ONE REALM FED THE HUNGRY, AND BURNED IT, AND MURDERED ITS KEEPERS. TO BE BROUGHT IN ALIVE.

She read it standing by the fire with the water running off her onto the flags. The same sum. Somebody's idea of a joke.

"They've turned on him," said Pell. "Their own sign. A month ago he was the one realm come again. On the gate. At the bottom of the paper." He tapped it. "Now they want him in a cage."

She thought of the hillside burning at the mouth of the Ewe Beck, on the night of the bell. Haverly on the wall. *That's dragon-fire.* She thought of the hills above Harrow's End two nights later, the torches scattering.

*Calder Fenn.* She'd known the name since the turn of the winter. Liss had told her, at supper. She'd never said it aloud.

"Ashdown," said Pell.

She looked up.

"The beast," said Pell. "I saw it from the punt. It's not wearing its collar. And you came down to the water with the thing in your hands."

She looked at him. At his square tired face, and his square neat hand that wrote down everything.

"Yes," she said. "I took it off. This morning. At the tower. With a hammer and a chisel." She put the hammer and chisel on the table beside the paper. "It let me."

Pell looked at them.

"It let you," he said.

"Yes."

He picked up his pen, and pulled the report book toward him, and opened it. He sat there with the pen over the page.

"Is there anything else?" he said.

She thought of the pit. The old grey stone at the bottom of it, running under the river toward Greyhold. The scratches.

"No, sir," she said. "That's all."

He wrote it down.
