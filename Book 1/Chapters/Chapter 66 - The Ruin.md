He sent them north on the hundred-and-thirty-ninth day.

He'd thought about it all night on the Scar, with the campfires coming out on the hill behind Harrow's End one after another like stars, and the carts on the Way. And in the morning he went into the cave where the three of them were lying, and told them.

"North," he said. "To the hatching caves. Above Coldtarn. Where we wintered."

Kharrak lifted his head off his forelegs.

*No.*

"They'll be on the island tomorrow," said Calder. "Thousands. With nets and chains and honey-smoke and bound hounds. If they see you over the island, if they see one of you, the whole Ram's Head comes down on it at once. On you. They want you. They want you more than anything on this side of the river." He looked at Kharrak. "And I can't let them take you. I can't. I've seen what they do."

*Then we'll burn them.*

"Thousands, Kharrak."

*Then we'll burn thousands.*

"And they'll bring the smoke. And the nets. And the collars." He made himself say it. "And one morning I'll look up and you'll be standing in a field at the Ram's Head with a man's hand on your neck and nothing in your eyes."

Kharrak didn't answer that.

*And you?* said Aurveth. *What will you do?*

"Go to the island."

The cave went very quiet.

"Alone," said Calder. "Before they get there. Tonight. I want to see it. The arch. The verse. Whatever's under the tower. I want to see it before they do." He looked at Aurveth. "You read the first line, in the freeze. Through me. And you stopped."

*Yes.*

"I'll bring you the rest."

*You can't read it.*

"I'll bring it anyway."

---

Kharrak wouldn't go.

He lay at the mouth of the cave with his wings half open and the red tide up and said *no* to everything, over and over, until Calder stopped talking. Then Aurveth got up. She got up in no hurry, and went to the mouth of the cave and stood over Kharrak, with her one gold eye looking down at him and her great bronze head lowered, and said something. Calder didn't hear it. It wasn't for him. It went between them, the way it did sometimes, kin to kin, and he only felt the edge of it through the marks, like the edge of a wind.

Kharrak folded his wings.

Lisska cried. She didn't make a sound. He felt it, through the green mark, all the way down. She put her head against his chest in the cave and he put his arms round as much of it as he could and they stayed like that until Aurveth said it was time.

*I'll feel you,* said Lisska. *Won't I? Even up there? Even if I can't hear you?*

"You'll feel me."

*And if something happens?*

"You'll feel that too."

*And then can we come?*

He didn't answer for a moment.

"Then you can come," he said.

They went up out of the Scar in the noon light, north, one after another, the bronze and the red and the green, low over the Fellwood toward the Scarfell, and he stood on the top of the black rock and watched them go until they were three specks over the white hills, and then two, and then nothing.

He felt them. All afternoon. Going away. Getting further. The voices first, going thin, the way they had on the drove road to Wendmouth: three leagues, four, and then nothing. And under it, where the voices had been, the other thing. The feeling. Like sheep on a far hill at night: you couldn't see them, but you knew where each one was. Three of them. Far off. Not happy. There.

---

He went down to the Eelweir at dusk.

The long way. Round through the Fellwood and down Hollow Beck, in the carter's coat, with Joss's knife in his belt and the two chains and the ring inside his coat, and a roll of Ada's paper and a stick of charcoal in his pocket. The paper with the six hands on the back.

He'd thought about the charcoal all morning. He couldn't read the old script. Nobody he knew could. But every farmer in the Marches knew how to take a rubbing of a boundary stone when there was a dispute: a sheet of paper over the cut letters, and a stick of charcoal, rubbed flat and hard, until the letters came up white out of the black. His father had done it once with the stone at the top of the Fenn field, in a quarrel with old Garrow about a hedge. You didn't have to read it. You just had to carry it to somebody who could.

---

The Eelweir was full of men.

He lay in the bracken on the slope above it at the edge of the Fellwood and watched them. The landing below, at the mouth of the beck, where Fisk kept his eel-boats and his traps: it was crowded with boats now. Long boats, flat boats, barges, dozens of them, run down off the carts and pulled up on the shingle in rows, with men working at them in the light of fires. More carts coming down the hill behind the village, and more boats coming off them. Men everywhere. In the lanes of Harrow's End. In the burned mill. In his own top field, he thought, though he couldn't see it from here. Campfires all the way up the hill.

Fisk's house was at the end of the landing. Dark. No light in the door.

*He's gone,* Calder thought. *They've had him out.*

Then something touched his shoulder, and he nearly cried out.

Fisk was lying in the bracken beside him. On his belly, in the dark, with his stick, and his old face close to Calder's, and his pipe, unlit, in his teeth.

"Took you long enough," said Fisk.

---

"There's a back way," said Fisk, very low. "Round the top of the island. The north channel, where the beck-water comes across and the reeds are thick. Nobody uses it. Too shallow for anything but an eel-boat, and too twisty for anybody who doesn't know it." He looked at Calder in the dark. "I know it."

"They'll see."

"They'll not see. They're all looking at the landing. And they don't know the river." Fisk spat. "Saltreach soup-sellers. They've been putting boats in the water since noon and they've lost two to the current already. I watched. Laughed till I hurt."

He had an eel-boat in the reeds at the mouth of the beck, above the landing. A little narrow thing, black, low in the water, that you could hardly see even close. He got Calder into it, lying flat in the bottom on the wet boards, and got in himself in the stern, and took up a short paddle, and pushed off.

Calder lay on his back in the bottom of the boat and looked up at the stars and listened to Fisk paddle.

It was slow going. Fisk went up the Saltreach shore in the dark, in the reeds, away from the landing and the fires, a long way, until the noise of the men at the Eelweir was faint behind them. Then out. Across. Into the main river, black and fast with the thaw, and the eel-boat swinging and Fisk grunting at the paddle. Then reeds again. Thick, tall, rattling dry over Calder's face. Twisting. Left and right and left. Fisk paddling one stroke and stopping and listening and paddling another.

Then the boat grounded, soft, on mud.

"North shore," breathed Fisk. "Under the oaks. You're on it." He didn't get out. "I'll be in the reeds. Here. If you need taking off. Three nights. After three nights I'll think you're dead and go home and get drunk."

"Fisk…"

"Go on," said Fisk. "Before I think better of it."

---

He went up through the oaks in the dark.

He'd been on the island once before. In the freeze, in the snow, with the dragons a league and a half off and their voices in his head. It was different now. No snow. Wet black earth and last year's leaves, and the oaks dripping, and the smell of the river and mud and green things starting. And no voices. Only the feeling, very far off, the three of them on their far hill.

He found the pit.

He didn't know it was there. He nearly walked into it. He came through the oaks at the north end of the island in the dark and the ground in front of him was suddenly gone, and he stopped with his toes at the edge of it and looked down.

A great hole. Black. He couldn't see the bottom. Raw banks of earth piled round it higher than his head, with the smell of wet clay. And at the bottom, when his eyes found it, a gleam of water. And under the water, something pale. Stone.

He crouched at the edge and put his hand down on the bank.

It was warm.

He felt along it. A long hollow, pressed down into the clay at the edge of the pit, the length of a cart. The shape of something that had lain there a long time. The clay at the bottom of it was warm under his hand, warm as a bed somebody's just got out of.

And beside it, against the side of it, a smaller hollow. A person's. Where somebody had sat with their back against something large. He put his hand in it.

Warm too. Less. But warm.

He crouched in the dark at the edge of the pit with one hand in each.

*Hours,* he thought. *Not more.*

---

He found the rest at the west shore.

He went down along the stream in the first grey, the way he'd gone in the snow, to the place where her fire had been. No fire now. But down at the water, where the stream came out into the reeds and the west channel, the mud was soft from the thaw, and it had kept everything.

A keel-mark. A skiff's. Pulled up into the reeds and pushed off again; the long groove of it in the mud, with the water just beginning to seep into the bottom of it.

Boot-prints. Narrow. A woman's. He knew them. He'd knelt by them in the snow. Going down to the skiff.

Beside them, going into the water, the others. Round. Each wider than both his hands spread. Four toes splayed and the claws drawn in. The edges of them still sharp in the mud. Still wet. Going down the bank and into the channel and not coming back.

He stood in the reeds in the grey light and looked across the west channel at the Hearthmere bank. At the long low fields going up from the water, wet and brown. At the tower at Redwatch on its rise across the water, with a light at the top still burning. At a dark line on the edge of the sky to the west that was a forest.

Nothing moving. Nobody.

*She took it off the island,* he thought. *Before they came. In the dark. She knew they were coming and she took it away.*

He thought of Liss Ashdown's face on the ferry, looking up at the cloud where Lisska had gone. *Cast out yesterday. For loving a monster.*

*And it went with her.*

---

He went to the arch.

The light was coming. Grey, then paler. The fort came up out of the dark round him, the broken walls and the ivy and the two gates, the way he'd seen it in the snow. The tower at the north-west corner, squat and round, broken off at the top. The arch in its side, with the dark doorway, and the lion's long smooth hollow worn into the threshold.

Over the arch, the verse.

He couldn't read it. He'd known he couldn't. Six lines of square spare letters with little hooks at the ends of the strokes, cut deep and worn shallow, with the lichen grown into the cuts. He stood under it in the grey light and looked at it and it meant nothing at all.

He took out the paper.

He had to stand on a fallen block to reach. He held the sheet flat against the stone over the first line with one hand, the six hands showing on the side away from him, and took the charcoal in the other, and rubbed. Flat, hard, the way his father had done it at the boundary stone. And the letters came up white out of the black, line by line, where the cuts were, and where the stone was gone the black went smooth and empty.

He did it twice, to be sure. When he'd done, he had two sheets, black, with six lines of white hooked letters on each, and gaps.

He rolled them up and put them inside his shirt against his chest. He'd carry them to Aurveth. He'd carry them to the hatching caves if he had to.

---

He went into the tower.

He hadn't meant to. In the snow he hadn't. He'd stood under the arch and felt Aurveth stop reading, a league and a half off, and something had told him not to. He went in now because the light was coming and he needed to be out of it, and because he wanted to know.

It was dark inside. A round room, the ground floor of the tower, with walls a man's arm-span thick and the floor deep in old leaves and fallen stone and the droppings of owls. A stair went up round the inside of the wall, broken, to a floor above that was half gone, with the grey sky showing through the top of the tower beyond it.

And a stair went down.

In the middle of the floor. A square hole, half choked with fallen stone and old black earth that had washed in over the years, and steps going down into it. Stone steps, worn hollow in the middle. He could see four of them, and then the fallen stone.

He knelt at the edge of it.

Below the fourth step, where the fallen stone had slid away to one side, there was something. Grey. Flat. A slab of dressed stone, set in the floor of whatever was down there, flush. He could just see the edge of it.

He reached down. He moved one of the fallen stones, carefully, and then another.

In the slab, in the middle of it, there was an iron plate. Black. Old. As big as a man's hand spread out. And in the middle of the plate, a hole.

A keyhole.

He knelt there looking at it.

He didn't know what it was. He'd never seen anything like it. A lock, in a slab of stone, in the floor at the bottom of a choked stair, under a tower on an island nobody landed on. He thought of the pit at the north end, and the pale stone at the bottom of it under the water. He thought of Aurveth on the ice at midwinter with her throat to it. *Something vast. Bound. Turning.*

He thought of his mother at Bel's table with her hands flat on the wood.

*When two hands open what one hand shut.*

He put the fallen stones back, carefully, the way they'd been.

---

The boats came at full light.

He heard them before he saw them. From the east. From across the east channel, from the Eelweir. A sound like a great many people talking at once a long way off, and the knock of oars, and men shouting the stroke. Closer. Then the scrape of keels on the gravel of the spit at the south end, one after another after another, dozens of them, and boots on stones, and voices, a lot of voices, coming up from the landing toward the fort.

He went up the stair.

The broken stair round the inside of the tower wall. Up, in the half-dark, on steps that rocked under him, to the floor above that was half gone. Joists, and a few rotten boards, and a great beam that had come down from the roof long ago and lay slantwise across the corner where the floor still held, with a gap under it. He crawled under the beam and lay flat on the old boards in the dust and the owl-droppings, with his face to a crack in the wall where a stone had come out, and looked down.

He could see the arch from there. And the fort. And through the broken top of the eastern gate, a long slice of the slope going down to the spit.

They came up it.

Men. Hundreds of them. In grey, most of them, with the six on their coats, and others in the coats of the companies, the red hand, the black ship, Delling's yellow tower. With spears and crossbows. With carts, little ones, hauled by hand up from the boats. With cages on the carts. Iron cages, big ones, empty. With long ladders. With beasts.

The hounds came first. In packs. Silent. With their handlers walking behind them with an arm up.

They came in through the eastern gate of the fort and spread out across it, and the noise filled the old walls like water filling a cistern.

He lay under the beam and watched them take the island.

---

They were everywhere by mid-morning.

In the fort. In the oaks. Along the shores. A company went down the stream to the west shore, and he saw them stop at the reeds where the skiff had been and stand there, looking at the ground. Pointing. One of them knelt down. Then they all came back up the stream fast, and a man went running toward the arch, toward a knot of men standing by the western gate.

A company went north with chains. He saw them go, through the oaks toward the pit. Men with the long coiled beast-chains over their shoulders and the six on every link, and men with clay pots on poles, smoking, a thick sweet smoke that came back on the wind and made his eyes water even up in the tower. *Honey-smoke.* Hounds in front. Going carefully, spread out.

They came back an hour later. The chains still coiled on their shoulders. The pots still smoking. Nothing between them.

Somebody below the tower was shouting.

He pressed his face to the crack.

By the arch, under the verse, a man stood with his back to the tower. Tall. Thin. Dark hair cut short. Very still. The man who'd run from the west shore was standing in front of him, talking fast, pointing west, across the channel. The tall man didn't look where he was pointing. He stood under the verse with his hands behind his back and listened, and when the man had finished he said something, one word, Calder couldn't hear it, and the man went away again fast.

The tall man stood under the arch a while longer.

He turned his head, and looked up. At the verse. At the six lines of old letters over the doorway, worn and lichened. He looked at them a long while. Reading them, Calder thought. He could read them.

He wasn't the only one. A little behind him stood a small grey man in a long brown gown gone ragged at the hem, like a chapel-man's, with his head tipped back and his lips moving. The men round the arch left a space round him, the way men leave a space round a dog they don't know.

He looked down. At the threshold of the arch. At the long smooth hollow worn in the stone, where something huge had lain longer than anyone could say.

He crouched down and put his hand in it.

Calder lay under the beam with his face to the crack in the wall and didn't breathe.

It was the face from the back room at the Ram's Head. The one who'd sat at the table writing while Rook stood beside him. It was the face from the Tidesteps, too, he thought. It must be. Vale. But it crouched under the arch with its hand flat in the lion's hollow as still as the Ram's Head face had been, and he couldn't make it be the same as the laughing one on the Tidesteps, throwing his arms out.

The tall man stood up.

He said something to the men behind him, quietly. Two of them came forward with crowbars and went into the tower under Calder, into the dark room below, and he heard them start to shift the fallen stone from the stair.

---

He lay under the beam all day.

He didn't move. He couldn't. The men in the room below dug at the stair all morning, carrying the fallen stone out in baskets, and he lay above them on the rotten boards and listened to every basket go out. By noon they'd stopped. He heard the tall man's voice below, quiet. Then nothing. Somebody laid something down on the floor, heavy, with a clink. Then two men sat down by the door of the tower and stayed there.

Guards. On the stair. On the slab with the lock in it.

He lay above them.

The island filled up all afternoon. More boats. More men. Fires lit in the fort. By the western gate a man sat on a crate with a pot of white paint and a stack of company shields beside him, painting marks on the face of each, small and square with hooks at the ends, quick, the way Ada wrote a tally. He never once looked up at the verse to copy them. He knew them already. Tents going up in the oaks. A cage brought up from the spit with something in it, something large and grey and silent, and set down by the eastern gate. The noise of a camp. Men laughing. Somebody singing one of the old songs, badly. *Before the six, there was one.*

He lay under the beam with the rubbing against his chest under his shirt and the chains in his coat and Joss's knife in his belt, and felt, very far away in the north, the three of them on their hill. Not happy. There.

*Not yet,* he thought at them, though they couldn't hear words. *Not yet. Stay.*

He thought of a skiff's keel-mark in the mud at the west shore, with the water just seeping into it. A woman's narrow boot-prints. The lion's prints going down into the channel beside them, the edges still sharp.

*Hours,* he thought. *She missed them by hours.*

*So did I.*

Below him, by the arch, somebody laughed, and somebody else shushed them, and the tall man's quiet voice said something, and the island went on filling with men as the light went.
