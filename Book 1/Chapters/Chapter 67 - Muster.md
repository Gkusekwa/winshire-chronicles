---

The Brackenwood began on a long rise two leagues west of the river, and the rise was called Hollin Knowe.

She'd ridden past the foot of it a hundred times on patrol and never been up. Nobody went up. There was nothing on the top but heather and the old beacon-stones, a ring of grey boulders where the river watch had lit fires in the old wars to warn Hallowmere. Behind them the forest began, all at once, the way the Brackenwood did: one moment open heather, the next a wall of oaks so old and thick that the light went green under them a step inside.

She came up the knowe as the sun came up on the hundred-and-fortieth day with the lion beside her.

They'd come two leagues across the fields in the dark. Wet plough, and hedges, and ditches full of thaw-water. She'd waded the ditches. The lion had stepped over them. They'd gone round the farms, wide, where the dogs were. Once a dog had started to bark in a yard a long way off and then stopped as if somebody had put a hand over its mouth, and she'd felt the ground under her feet go still, and looked at the lion, and it was looking at the farm with its lip back. She'd put her hand on its shoulder. It had come on.

At the top of the knowe she stopped among the beacon-stones and turned round, and looked back the way they'd come.

---

You could see everything.

That was why the old watch had lit their fires there. The whole reach of the Wend lay below her in the dawn, two leagues off, brown and wide and shining. Redwatch on its rise above the near bank, small and red, with its tower. Upriver, the long bend toward the Scarfell, white still on the far tops. Downriver, the grey hill of Greyhold, with a long dark smudge of smoke over it, and past it, smaller, the roofs of Millbrook.

And in the middle of the river, the island.

She couldn't see it well. It was too far. A dark shape in the brown water, long, with the oaks on it bare still, and the stump of the tower. But she could see the boats.

They were all round it. On the east channel, between the island and the Saltreach shore, so many she couldn't count them: little dark specks going back and forth, back and forth, in lines, like water-beetles. On the island's shores, drawn up in rows. And on the Saltreach bank beyond, where Harrow's End was, where the hill went up behind the village, the hill was black.

Not black with trees. Black with tents. Black with men.

Smoke went up from the island in a dozen places. From the fort. From the oaks at the north end, where the pit was.

The lion stood beside her among the beacon-stones and looked at it.

---

It didn't want to be there.

She knew that before the sun was clear of the hills. It paced. Along the edge of the trees, behind the stones, back and forth, the way she'd seen the hounds in the Long Barn pace when something was wrong in the yard. Up to the end of the ring of stones and stop and look east. Back. Stop. Look east. Its tail going. Its mane up and then down and then up.

Twice it went down the slope.

She didn't stop it. She couldn't have. She sat on a beacon-stone and watched it go down through the heather toward the fields and the river, a dozen strides, then fifty, with its head low and its eyes on the island. Stop. Stand there, looking.

And come back.

It came back up the slope both times and lay down in the heather beside the stone where she was sitting, and put its head on its paws, facing east.

She didn't ask it why. She couldn't. She sat on the beacon-stone and looked at it lying there with its eyes on the island and its whole great body pointing at the island like a hound's at a hare, and it didn't go.

*It came with me,* she thought. *Off the island. In the dark. I didn't call it. And now it won't go back.*

She didn't know what to do with that. She put her hand down on its mane. It let her.

---

She watched all of the hundred-and-fortieth day.

She'd been the river watch for eight winters before she ever went to Greyhold. She knew how to watch. She'd written fourteen pages about a roadblock. She sat on the beacon-stone and watched the island and the river and the far bank and counted, the way she'd count anything, and kept the count in her head because she had nothing to write on.

Boats. Too many to count. She stopped trying. Lines of them, all day, from the Eelweir to the island's east shore, and back. Laden going. Empty coming.

Rafts. Big ones. Four boats lashed together with planks over them, poled across slow, with things on them. Cages. She could see them even from the knowe, square and dark against the brown water. Iron cages, big enough for a greyhart. Six rafts. Seven. Some of the cages had something in them. Some didn't.

Smoke. In the fort, all day. In the oaks.

In the afternoon, the ladders.

She saw them go down to the west shore of the island. Long things, pale, new-cut timber, carried by a dozen men each, down through the oaks to the reeds on the side of the island that faced Hearthmere. Faced her. Faced Redwatch. Ten of them. Fifteen. Longer than any ladder she'd ever seen. Long enough to reach the top of a wall.

Boats with them, too. Drawn up in the reeds on the west shore. Facing west. Facing this bank.

She sat on the stone and looked at them.

*They're not going to stay on the island,* she thought.

She'd known that. She'd known it since the bill on the Millbrook jetty, since the paper on the river gate, since Hale's face. *The thing under the hill.* The island was the way across. It wasn't what they'd come for.

---

It hunted that night, or tried to.

It went into the Brackenwood at dusk without a sound, and she sat among the beacon-stones in her father's brown cloak with her back against the coldest stone in the world and ate the last of the bread from her sack, and drank from the spring that came out of the rock under the stones, cold enough to hurt her teeth. She heard it in the wood, sometimes, a long way off. A crash. Something running. Then nothing.

It came back before dawn with nothing.

It lay down in the heather beside her again and put its head on its paws and looked east.

*You're not hungry,* she thought. *You didn't go to hunt. You went to look at the island from somewhere else.*

---

On the hundred-and-forty-first day, at dawn, Greyhold's gate opened.

She saw it from the knowe. She'd been awake since before light; she'd hardly slept. The river was grey and still in the first light, and downriver the grey hill of Greyhold with its smoke, and she saw the river gate open in the wall above the landing, a dark square, and out of it, onto the River Road, the orders came.

She'd seen them ride out before. On patrol, in fours and eights. To Holloway, the night it burned. To Ferriby.

She'd never seen this.

They came out of the gate in a column and turned north up the River Road, toward Redwatch, toward the island, and kept coming. Riders. Two by two. In the grey, with the sun coming up on their helms. Greyharts among them, white, tall as the riders' heads, with their long antlered heads up. Hounds at the horses' heels in packs. Wagons. One with a great square cage on it, drawn by four dray-beasts, and something black and humped inside it that could only be Thorne's boar. And behind it a dray-wain with a load under canvas so heavy that the beasts in the traces leaned into it with their heads down.

She knew what it was. She'd heard it made, from the midden, the week before: four men at one fire, hammering, and links as big as a man's head. She'd seen it, too, across the yard with her barrow: old Abel, the licensed smith-reader, standing at the quench-trough with his eyes shut, saying the words into each link as it went hissing in.

Behind the wain, on mules, two men in long grey coats with satchels and no beasts. The infirmary's licensed surgeons, the two younger ones. The old one, who'd set a groom's wrist with his bare hands at midwinter, swearing, when the words wouldn't come, would be behind the wall with the handful. They were taking the young ones anyway, words or none.

She counted. She couldn't stop herself. Two hundred riders. Two hundred and fifty. More. She lost count somewhere in the greyharts. The column went up the River Road along the bank, far off below her, for half the morning, and the dust of it hung over the road behind.

At the head of it, a single rider on a grey, with no beast beside her.

Rowan. It could only be Rowan.

The column went on up the road toward Redwatch. Toward the river reach opposite the island, where the ladders were. To hold the bank.

The gate of Greyhold shut behind them.

She looked at Greyhold, on its hill, downriver. At the river wall, with its new hoardings, yellow. At the towers with the engines on them. At the gate, shut. She could see a few figures on the wall. A handful. The ones who'd stayed. She couldn't see who.

*When they cross,* Tam had said, in the Kettle's yard, with the axe in his hands. *The knights'll go up the River Road to meet them. All of them. To Redwatch, to hold the bank. And when they've gone…*

*Millbrook'll rise.*

She looked downriver past Greyhold, at the roofs of Millbrook in the morning sun, small and grey and quiet. At the water-meadows below the town, by the river, where there was a camp of tents. She couldn't see it from here. She knew it was there.

---

She thought about going down.

She sat on the beacon-stone all that morning and thought about it. Going down off the knowe, across the fields, to the River Road. To the column. To Rowan on her grey horse. *Captain. It's a trick. They want you up here. When you've gone, Millbrook rises behind you, and Greyhold's empty.*

Rowan would look at her. An outcast. Cast out five days ago, in her shirt, at the river gate, in front of everybody, for striking a knight. Come down out of the Brackenwood. With a lion behind her.

*If they take it, it's a weapon.*

The great new chain on the dray-wain, under its canvas.

She looked at the lion lying in the heather beside her, with its eyes on the island.

She went down anyway.

She got halfway. Off the knowe through the heather and across two wet fields, to a hedge on the last rise above the road, where she could see the pennons and the shine on the helms. The lion came behind her. She didn't send it back. She stood in the gap of the hedge with the words ready in her mouth.

The wind had gone round into the west.

On the road the greyharts smelled it. The nearest one stopped dead and threw its antlered head up, and its rider swore and hauled at it, and then the one behind was doing it, and the next, white heads going up all down that stretch of the column, every one turned toward the hedge. A hound began to bay. A knight pulled out of the line onto the verge and sat his horse there, looking up at the fields, with his crossbow across his knees.

She stepped back out of the gap. She went back up the fields with the lion beside her, and didn't look round until she was among the stones.

She thought about going to Greyhold. To the gate. To Hale. To Pell. To the handful on the wall. *Shut it. Bar it. Don't open it for anyone. Not for any seal, not for any rider.* And she thought of a seal she'd heard of from Ottery, twice, that had cleared the river on the nights the barges needed it clear. In a hand nobody knew. *Somebody inside.* She had no idea who on that wall she'd be telling.

She thought about Millbrook.

Liss. Who'd held up the lantern on the red door while she cast off, and hadn't said a word. Tam, in the Kettle's yard, with the axe in his hands, saying *I'll come home after.* A camp of tents by the water-meadows, below the town. Ada Fenn, who Liss had known thirty years. Bel Fenn, whose husband was dead. Two little ones.

A girl called Wren.

She didn't know which way to go. She sat on the beacon-stone on the top of Hollin Knowe with the whole river below her, and the lion beside her. Every way she looked there was somebody, and she was one woman in her father's cloak with no sword and no grey and no oath, and a beast that wouldn't go back to the island and wouldn't leave her.

She sat there the rest of the day.

---

The column reached Redwatch in the afternoon.

She watched it spread out along the bank below the tower. Along the reeds. Facing the island across the west channel, where the ladders were and the boats. Lines of riders. The greyharts. The cage with the boar in it, set down at the water's edge. The dray-wain with the chain. Fires lit along the bank in the dusk, a long line of them, red points in the grey.

On the island, opposite, the boats lay in the west reeds and stayed there.

Two armies with a river channel between them. Half a league of brown water. Facing each other in the dusk. Neither crossing.

*They're waiting,* she thought. *Both of them. For something.*

She didn't know what. She thought of Hale's black door, warm under her palm.

---

At dusk the island lit up.

She'd been watching the bank at Redwatch, the long line of the orders' fires. When she looked back at the island it had changed.

Fires. Everywhere. All over it. In the fort, a great one, in the middle of the old walls, and a dozen smaller round it. Along the east shore. Along the west, where the boats were. In the oaks at the north end, where the pit was: a ring of them, red, close together, round something. At the spit. Among the trees. On every shore, at every landing, under every tree.

So many that the island didn't look like an island any more. It looked like a coal. A long red coal lying in the black river, with the fires in it like the bright places in a coal when you blow on it, and the smoke going up off it into the dark, lit red underneath.

The Old Ruin. Full of fires and men.

She stood among the beacon-stones and looked at it.

Beside her, the lion stood up.

It walked past her. To the edge of the ring of stones, at the top of the slope, where the heather went down toward the dark fields and the river. It stood there on the very edge of the knowe, against the sky, with its mane lifting in the wind off the river, and looked at the island burning in the dark two leagues off.

Then it made a sound.

She'd heard it roar. On the shore, the first night. Across the river, through the stone of the archive, the night her hand was on the door. She'd heard it growl, and breathe, and once, in the arch on the night the river went out, sigh like a dog. She'd never heard this.

It was low. Very low, and long, and it went up at the end and broke. It went out from the top of Hollin Knowe over the dark fields toward the river and the island and the fires and didn't come back.

She'd heard a sound like it once before. In the infirmary at Greyhold, on the second night, from Ide Darrow, in the dark, asking for Bracken.

A question. That nobody was going to answer.

The lion stood on the edge of the knowe and made it again. And then a third time, quieter. And then it stood there, silent, with its head up, looking at the island.

She went and stood beside it. She put her hand on its shoulder.

Far off, across the river, on the island, at the north end, where the ring of fires was, round the pit, something moved in the dark. A long way down. She felt it. Through the lion's shoulder, under her hand. Through the ground under her feet, two leagues from the river. A hum. Deep. Turning. The way the ground had turned under the archive when her hand was on the door.

The lion went utterly still under her palm.

Then it lay down on the edge of the knowe, facing east, and put its head on its paws, and she sat down in the heather against its shoulder, and they watched the island burn together all night, and neither of them slept.
