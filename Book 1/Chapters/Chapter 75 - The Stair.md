---

He ran.

She'd thought she knew what it would be. She'd seen him run on the island, in the summer, on the shore, the night he woke. She'd felt him come down the slope of the knowe behind her and the ground jump. She'd never been on him.

A horse goes up and down. He didn't. He went flat, and long, and the ground went under them in great slow strides that weren't slow at all, and the fields went past in the dark, and the hedges, and the ditches full of thaw-water that he went over without seeming to notice they were there. She lay flat along his neck in the mane with her burned hands knotted in it and her knees gripping and the wind tearing at her eyes so she couldn't see.

She didn't need to. She could feel where he was going. Through the bond. The way you feel where your own feet are going in the dark.

*Greyhold,* she'd said. *The school. The tall hill by the river. With the walls.*

*I know it,* said Orrun. *It stinks of iron.*

Over them, high, green against the stars, the dragon came. Maren felt her through Orrun. A small bright frightened fire up there in the dark, keeping pace.

---

The river gate was open.

She saw it from the River Road. They came up out of the fields onto the road below Greyhold's hill and there it was, above the landing, where Pell had sat down. The great oak leaves of the gate standing open, both of them, wide, with the light of fires coming out between them.

There were men on the road. Bodies. A horse, dead. In the open gateway, a great black shape lying across the threshold that she knew before she was close enough to see it.

Thorne's boar.

Dead. On its side, in the gateway, with spears in it. And lying against its shoulder, with one arm across its neck the way you'd put your arm across a friend's, a broad square grey-haired woman in a riding coat.

*They went together,* thought Maren.

Orrun went over them.

---

Everything in the yard stopped.

He came in through the river gate at a run with her flat on his neck, into the yard of Greyhold, into the light of fires and the noise of fighting, and the noise stopped.

Men in grey with the six on their coats. Knights in mail. Students. All of them, all over the yard, in the middle of whatever they were doing, turning their heads.

A lion ten feet at the shoulder had come in through the gate. With a woman on its back.

She saw faces. She knew some. A second-year with a marten on his shoulder, the one who'd leaned out over the wall to hear her shout about Callis, with a sword in his hand and his mouth open. A knight of the gate she'd mucked out under since the autumn, Vasey, with no helm and blood in his beard. Restoration men she didn't know, with spears, with rings on their fingers, with their faces gone white.

Then the yard broke open round him.

Not at him. Away from him. Every man in the yard, Restoration and knight alike, went back from him, the way water goes back from a stone thrown into it. Orrun stood in the middle of the open space with his mane up and the firelight on him and made a sound in his chest that she felt in her own.

And over the yard, out of the dark, screaming, with her wings spread wide, came the green dragon.

The Restoration men by the gate broke and ran.

---

The Long Barn was barricaded.

Carts across the great double door, and barrels, and the iron-banded door off Thorne's boar's stall. Behind the barricade, people. Faces. Refugees from the camp. Grooms. Children. Grieve, bald, with a pitchfork, standing on a cart.

In the gap at the end of the barricade, where the carts didn't quite meet, a dray-beast.

Grey. Huge. Standing in the gap with his head low and his eyes empty, not moving. Like a wall. A man in grey with a spear had come at the gap and was standing in front of him, a few paces off, with his spear up, and not coming any closer. Old Grey stood and looked at nothing over his head, and the man didn't dare.

Behind the grey, in the dark of the barn, an old woman in a good shawl with a stick, and a square-faced woman with her arms round two children.

Maren looked at them over the yard from Orrun's back. Ada saw her. Ada lifted her stick an inch off the ground.

There wasn't time.

*There,* said Orrun.

He'd turned his head. North. Across the yard, toward the north tower.

---

There was fighting on the north tower stair.

The stair that went down from the yard to the archive. The narrow stone steps under the north tower that the cat nobody owned had slept on for three nights in the winter, looking down at the archive door. Somebody was holding it. One man, on the steps, with his back to the archive door below him and a sword in his hand, against a crowd of men in grey with the six on their coats at the top. And over his head, going up and down, up and down, stooping at the faces of the men at the top of the stair with her claws out and screaming, a falcon.

Haverly.

And Kesh.

She saw it from across the yard.

Haverly on the stair, alone, holding it. His face white and set. Blood on him. Kesh going up off his shoulder and down at the men above him like a thrown knife and up again. The men at the top with spears, jabbing down, and Haverly turning them, and the falcon stooping.

A crossbow.

At the top of the stair, behind the spears, a man lifted it. Not at Haverly. Up. At the falcon, at the top of her climb, hanging for a moment against the firelight before she stooped.

Maren screamed.

The bolt went up. Kesh folded in the air. She came down. Not in a stoop. Down, all wrong, turning over and over like a dropped glove, into the stairwell, onto the steps at Haverly's feet.

He looked down at her.

He stopped fighting. He looked down at the falcon on the steps at his feet and stopped, with his sword still up, and his face went from white to nothing at all. Maren saw it go. Across the yard. Everything in it going out at once, the way a lamp goes out.

A spear came down the stair and went into him.

---

Orrun cleared the stair.

He went across the yard so fast she nearly came off his neck. At the men at the top of the north tower stair. They saw him come. They had time to see him come. Then he was on them, and the ground under the north tower heaved, and the flagstones at the top of the stair came up out of the yard like the lid of a box, and the men went over and down.

Maren slid off his neck at the top of the stair.

She went down the steps.

Haverly was lying on them. Halfway down, on his back, with his head on the step above and his legs on the step below and the spear gone out of him, and Kesh on the step beside him. The falcon was dead. Haverly wasn't. Not yet. His eyes were open. He was looking up past her at the sky over the yard.

She knelt on the step and got his head into her lap.

"Lucan," she said.

His eyes came to her.

"Ashdown." His voice was very small. "You've got your hair tied back."

"Don't talk."

"Everybody says that." He almost smiled. Pell's words, in his mouth. He must have been there, she thought. In the gateway. He must have heard. "Is she…"

Maren looked at the falcon on the step beside him. At the fierce black eye, open, still.

"Yes," she said.

"I know." He shut his eyes a moment and opened them. "I felt her go. Like a… like a door shutting. On the other side of me." He was quiet. "One life between two. They always told us. I never…" His breath caught. "I never thought it'd be quick."

She held his head.

"They went down," said Haverly. "An hour ago. Him and Callis. Twenty men. Through the archive. Callis had Thorne's key off her at the gate. I saw him take it." His eyes went to the archive door at the bottom of the stair below them. "I held the stair. So nobody else could follow. That's all I could think of. Hold the stair." He looked at her. "I wasn't brave enough to go down after them. I was brave enough for the stair."

"I know."

"Your mother," said Haverly. "She came to the water-gate at dusk, shouting for Hale. Nobody believed her." He swallowed. "Hale did."

His eyes went past her, up the stair, to the top, where Orrun stood in the broken flagstones against the firelight with his mane up, looking down.

"Is that it?" said Haverly. "The lion?"

"Yes. His name's Orrun."

Haverly looked at him.

"He came down," he said. Very quietly. "Didn't he. Out of the sky. Onto your fist."

She didn't know what to say.

"I'm glad," said Haverly.

He put his hand out, without looking, on the step beside him, and found the falcon, and laid his hand on her. And left it there.

That was all.

---

She sat on the stair with his head in her lap for as long as she could bear. It wasn't long. There wasn't time.

Over the top of the stair the green dragon came down. Lisska. She came down into the broken flagstones beside Orrun, and folded her wings, and looked down the stair at Maren with the dead man in her lap and the dead falcon beside him. Then she came down three steps, as far as she could, and spread her wings out over the stairwell, wide, green, like a roof. Over them.

*She says she'll stay,* said Orrun. *Here. Over him. Nobody will come down the stair.*

Maren laid Haverly's head down on the step.

She went down to the archive.

---

The archive door was open.

Inside, it was dark. Not the brass lamp, not the old lamp that burned without oil; both out. A lantern on the floor at the far end, past the stacks, on its side, still burning, throwing a long yellow fan of light across the bare swept floor and the three steps going down.

And at the foot of the three steps, against the black door, a man.

She went down the long room between the stacks, past the long table where she'd sat with the black book under her hands, past the shelves of brown ledgers where she'd hidden from Thorne. Into the yellow light.

Hale was sitting on the bottom step with his back against the black door.

His stick was beside him on the floor. His white beard was red down the front. There was a sword-cut across him from the shoulder, through the coat, deep, and his big hands were pressed flat over it, and they were red to the wrists.

He looked up at her.

"Ashdown," he said.

She knelt in front of him.

"Don't," said Hale, before she could say it. "There isn't time and I know it and so do you." He breathed. It was a long wet sound. "Your mother came. To the water-gate. At dusk. In her boat." He almost smiled. "Shouting for me by name. Nobody shouts for me by name. I came down to the water-gate and she told me through the bars. *Callis. The seal. Tonight. The river gate.*" He shut his eyes. "Rowan didn't believe it. Callis was on the gate with her that minute. Fifteen years. Rowan didn't believe it. I did."

"You came here."

"I came here. To the door. Where else?" He opened his eyes. "I thought I'd stand in front of it. When they came. I'm an old man, I'm a Watch, I've a key and a stick. I thought I'd stand in front of the door." He looked down at his hands. "Callis did this. With his good arm. He'd Thorne's key off her body. He opened the door with it and they went through, the one with the face from the paper and Callis and twenty men, and Callis locked it again behind them. From the other side." He breathed. "So nobody'd follow."

Behind his back, the black door was shut.

She put her hand on it. Over his shoulder.

It was hot.

---

"There's a lock," said Hale. "At the bottom."

She looked at him.

"I never saw it. When I went down. Merrow didn't show me. But it's in the oldest pages, the ones that weren't cut. *The knight's lock.* In a room. Off the ledge. Under the chains." His eyes held hers. "It turns. At the hour. In the same breath as another. And if it turns…"

"I know."

He looked at her.

"You do," he said. "Don't you. How?"

"A dragon told my lion," said Maren. "And my lion told me."

Hale stared at her.

Then he laughed. It hurt him. She saw it hurt. He laughed anyway, a short wet broken sound, with his head back against the black door and his red hands on his chest.

"*My lion,*" he said. "Oh, Ashdown."

He fumbled at his belt. At the ring of keys. His fingers were slippery and they wouldn't work, and she put her hands over his and helped, and together they got it off the ring. The long black key. Iron, as long as a forearm, with a bit as big as a child's fist. Cold and heavy.

He put it in her hands. He closed her fingers round it with his.

"I said I'd not make you that," he said. "On this step. With this key. I said I'd not make you Watch." His hands were very cold on hers. "I'm making you it now. Go through. Go down. Be what you have to."

"Hale…"

"*What is written here is true,*" said Hale. He was looking past her, up the long dark room, toward the door, toward the carved line over it she couldn't see from here. "*What is true is not all written here.*" He smiled. "Write the rest, Ashdown. After. You write fourteen pages about anything."

His hands slid off hers.

---

Orrun was at the top of the three steps.

She hadn't heard him come. He'd come down the north tower stair and through the archive door and down the long room between the stacks, and the stacks were still standing, which she wouldn't have thought possible. He stood at the top of the three steps with his great head low, looking down at her kneeling in front of the old man, with the key in her hands.

*He's gone,* said Orrun.

"Yes."

*He smelled of old paper and of you. He was kind to you.*

"Yes."

Orrun looked at the black door.

She felt him look at it. Through the bond. She felt what he felt, and it went through her so hard she put her hand on the wall.

The door. The heat coming off it. And below it, far below, a long way down, under the archive and the hill and the rock, the thing that had been under her hand on the night of the roar. Turning. Pulling. The chains groaning. She felt them through him. Going taut. Holding.

And something else.

*There,* said Orrun. Very low. *Below. Off the chains. A room.* He was very still. *A man. With his hand on iron. Waiting.*

She knelt at the foot of the three steps with the key in her hands.

*Someone's already at the lock,* she thought.

---

She moved him first. Gently, the way you'd move somebody asleep, off the door and along the bottom step to the wall, with his stick across his knees. Then she put the key in the lock.

It went in a long way. Further than she would have thought. She turned it.

It was stiff. It didn't want to. It had been turned once for each of three hundred and eleven names, and tonight, with a dead woman's copy, for Callis. She put both hands to it, her burned hands, and turned, and felt it go, iron grinding on iron, and the bolt went back with a sound like a bone breaking.

She pushed the door.

It swung inward onto dark. A breath of cold came out of it into her face, and warmth under the cold, and the smell of old stone and iron and something else, very deep. A stair. Going down. Stone steps, worn in the middle like a chapel's, going round and down into the dark.

A man's width.

She looked back at Orrun.

He came down the three steps.

He put his great head through the door, and looked at the stair. Then he drew his head back, and set his shoulder to the stone of the doorframe, the way she'd seen him set his shoulder to the old grey blocks at the bottom of the pit. And leaned.

The ground answered.

She felt it come. Up through the floor of the archive. The hum, rising, deeper, louder, until the shelves behind them rattled and the brown ledgers began to slide off them onto the floor. The stone round the black door groaned. The arch over it cracked. A long crack running up through the old grey stone of the wall, and another, and the doorframe opening, the stone round it moving back like the jaws of something opening.

The black door came off its hinges and fell inward onto the stair.

The opening was wide enough for a lion.

Beyond it, the stair groaned. She heard it. All the way down and round into the dark, the walls of the stair shifting, the rock moving back, the way snow loosens on a roof before it slides. Making way.

*It knows me,* said Orrun. *The stone. It's the same stone as the pit. It knows what I'm for.*

She picked up the lantern from the floor.

She looked once at Hale, sitting at the end of the bottom step against the wall, beside the place where the door had been, with his stick beside him and his hands in his lap. Then she got up on Orrun's back, and held the lantern up, and he went through the open stone and down.
