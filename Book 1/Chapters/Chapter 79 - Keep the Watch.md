---

The rock opened for him.

She'd felt it move for him on the stair under the school, and along the old stone road under the river. That had been the rock stepping back. Making way. This was different.

He walked up into it.

He put his head down, and his shoulder, and walked at the wall of the cavern at the foot of the rubble slope, and the wall wasn't there. It opened in front of him, as water opens round your arms when you swim, and closing again a little way behind. A passage, going up. Round, and rough, and just big enough, and getting longer as he walked, and the rock on every side of it humming with him.

*Up,* said Orrun. *Toward the water. Not into it. Beside it.*

She lay on his neck with her hands in his mane and felt him find the way. Through the stone, blind, knowing it. The way her father had known where the snags were in the river in the dark.

Behind them, the others came.

Lisska first, ahead of Orrun's head now, with the lantern hooked in her front claw, its light swinging on the rough rock. Then the farmer, on foot, at the head of the old bronze dragon, with his hand on her jaw. And the old dragon, walking, dragging the torn wing. With the red one under her other shoulder, holding her up, pressing his whole side against hers at every step so she wouldn't fall.

She heard him talk to them. The farmer. Out loud. Low and steady, the whole way. She couldn't hear the answers. She could feel them, a little, through Orrun, through Lisska: kin to kin. A great slow weight of pain, and under it something patient. A rust-red heat, holding.

*Ma says she was the best in the barn at her letters,* the farmer was saying, to the old dragon, for no reason Maren could understand. *At Varrhold. Sixty years ago.* And a pause, while something answered him. *I didn't know that either.*

---

Behind them and under them, the chains went.

She felt the second one. Through the rock. Through Orrun. A long falling thunder, going down and down, the way the first had gone past them on the rubble slope. A silence, and the cavern below them heaving, and the passage round them shaking so hard that Lisska dropped the lantern and caught it again.

The third.

Each one a long falling thunder under them. Each one followed by a heave from below that came up through the rock into Orrun's feet and through Orrun into her. Bigger every time. Something lifting. Something pulling against what was left.

She lay on Orrun's neck and felt him feel it, and felt what he felt.

He didn't stop walking.

At the fourth the roof came down.

Behind them. She felt it through Orrun before she heard it: a slab of the passage roof the size of a barn door breaking loose over the old dragon's back, where the rock should have closed a little way behind them and hadn't closed yet. The farmer shouted.

And the rock stood up.

Out of the passage wall beside the old dragon, a shoulder of grey stone, and a mane of it, and a back. A lion of the hill's own rock, Orrun's size and Orrun's shape, standing up under the falling slab with its legs braced and its head down. The slab came down on its back and stopped there.

Maren looked back past Lisska's lantern and saw it. Holding the roof, with the dust pouring off it, while the old dragon dragged herself under it with the red one at her shoulder and the farmer at her head, his face turned up to the stone thing over him.

Under her, Orrun shook. All of him, like a horse under a load too heavy for it. She felt it go out of him into the thing behind them, warm and steady.

*Go,* he said, to the ones behind.

They went. The red one last, his tail clear. Then the stone lion let the roof down behind them and went back into the wall it had come out of, and the passage closed over the place where it had stood.

Orrun stood with his head down and his breath going in and out of him like a bellows. She put her hand flat on his neck and kept it there.

Then he walked on.

The fifth.

Then nothing, for a long way. The passage going up. The rock opening and humming and closing behind. The farmer's voice, low and steady. Her own breath.

Then the sixth chain went, the last, and there was no thunder at all.

There was a silence.

The whole of the rock round them, the whole hill, the whole cavern below, the river overhead, all of it, went utterly silent and still, as a room goes still when somebody in it stands up who's been sitting a long time.

*It's free,* said Orrun.

---

They came out into the reeds at first grey.

She smelled it first. Mud. River. Reeds. The cold of morning after a long dark. The passage went up and up and grew lighter and then there was earth in front of Orrun's head instead of rock, black wet river-earth full of roots, and he pushed through it and the reeds were all round them, tall, dry, rattling.

She knew the place.

The reeds below Redwatch, a long way down from the tower. Where she'd run Liss's skiff ashore four mornings ago, before light, with the lion coming up out of the river beside her, streaming. He'd known it. He'd brought them out at the one place on the whole Hearthmere bank where they'd come ashore together.

He stood in the reeds with the earth falling off his back and his mane full of it, and she slid down off his neck into the mud.

Behind them, out of the hole in the riverbank, came Lisska with the lantern. And the farmer, with his hand on the old dragon's jaw. And the old dragon, slowly, last, with the red one under her shoulder, coming up out of the ground into the reeds like something being born.

They stood there. All of them. In the reeds in the grey light.

And looked at the river.

---

The island was gone.

She'd known it would be. She'd felt it fall, from the knight's lock, through Orrun. It didn't help.

Where it had been, in the middle of the Wend, half a league out from the reeds, there was a hole. In the river. A great round darkness a quarter of a mile across, and the Wend was pouring into it. From both channels, east and west, from upriver and from down, the whole brown river was running in over the edges of it in sheets and falling, falling, down into the dark, round and round, in a whirlpool so big she couldn't see the far side of it for the spray. The noise of it filled everything. Steam came up out of it, and smoke, grey, in great slow columns, into the grey morning sky.

No oaks. No fort. No tower. No arch. No verse.

Nothing. The river going down into the dark where they'd been.

She stood in the reeds and looked at it and couldn't breathe.

Beside her, the farmer was looking at it too. He had something in his hand. The bundle she'd given him, the five links in the strip of his shirt. He was holding it so hard his knuckles were white.

---

Everybody had stopped.

She saw it as the light came. Along the Hearthmere bank, up at Redwatch, the knights' line along the reeds where they'd fought all yesterday and all night: men standing still. Horses standing still. Nobody fighting. Everybody facing the river.

On the water, the Restoration's boats. Dozens of them, scattered all over the reach between the bank and the whirlpool, where they'd been crossing, and fighting, and running. Men in them sitting still with their oars shipped. One boat too close to the hole, caught in the edge of the pull, turning slowly, and the men in it not even trying to row.

On the far bank, at Harrow's End, on the hill where the campfires had been, black with men: nobody moving.

The whole river had stopped. Everyone on both banks of it. Standing. Looking at the hole where the island had been.

The fighting had stopped by itself. Nobody had told it to.

---

The water in the hole moved.

Not round. Up.

The whirlpool slowed. She saw it. The great slow wheel of brown water going down into the dark, slowing, slowing, as if something in the bottom of it had put out a hand and stopped it. The river still pouring in over the edges, and nowhere for it to go. The water in the hole rising. Heaving. Lifting, all of it at once, in a great slow dome of brown water a quarter of a mile across, with the spray going up off it in sheets.

Beside her, Orrun made a sound.

The dome of water broke.

---

It came up out of the river.

She'd try to tell it, after. To Liss. To Tam. To a girl with a great deal of hair who'd make her say it twice. She'd never get it right. There weren't words the size of it.

A head first. Out of the water in the middle of the hole, with the river falling off it. A head like Orrun's. She knew it the moment she saw it. The shape of it. The breadth of the brow, the line of the jaw, the set of the eyes. She'd lain against that shape all winter on the island. She'd put her hand on it in the arch and in the pit and on the knowe. She'd seen it drawn on a cut stub in a black book: a forefoot, four toes splayed, the claws drawn in. *…kin.*

The same shape. And the size of a hill.

It came up. And up. A neck. Shoulders. A mane, if it was a mane, like a ridge of broken grey rock all down the back of the neck, with the water pouring through it in falls. A back, plated, plate on plate like stone laid on stone, like the courses of the inner wall at Greyhold, each plate the size of a house. Forelegs, coming up out of the hole and setting down on the river-bed at the edges of it, and the river breaking white round them like round the piers of a bridge.

On the forelegs, round them, the chains.

What was left of them. The broken ends. Each link taller than a man, black with three hundred years, trailing from its legs down into the water, and as it moved, they moved, like threads. Like nothing. On the inside of the links, faint in the daylight, the thin white writing was still burning.

It was grey. Grey as old stone. Grey as the inner wall. Grey as the slab at the bottom of the pit, scored all over with the pale marks of Orrun's claws.

It stood in the river where the island had been, with the Wend breaking round its legs and pouring off its back, as tall as Greyhold's hill.

---

The ground moved when it moved.

She felt it in her feet. On both banks. A heave that went out from it through the river-bed and up through the reeds and the mud and the fields, in a great slow ring, like a ripple from a stone. The reeds round her lay down flat. Up at Redwatch, the tower cracked. She saw it go: a long dark line running up the red stone from the foot to the top, and the top of the tower leaning, and a cloud of dust going up.

Downriver, far off, on its hill, Greyhold.

She looked. She couldn't stop herself.

The outer walls stood. The scaffolds. The new yellow hoardings. And in the middle of it all, the old wall. The inner wall. The grey stones, older than the rest, each block the size of a cart, with the frost in the seams. The wall that everyone in Greyhold faced at noon. The wall that had been built over the way down.

It split.

From top to bottom. She saw it from leagues off. A crack going up through the grey stones from the ground to the top of the wall, black, and the two halves of the wall leaning away from each other, slowly, and stopping.

*Keep the watch below.*

---

It opened its wings.

She hadn't known it had wings. She'd seen the shape of its back come up out of the river and thought it was all plates, stone on stone. Folded along its back, under the plates, under the river falling off it, there were wings.

They came up out of the water, unfolding. Like stone, and like leather, and like neither. Grey. Ribbed like the roof of a barn. Wider than the river. Wider than the river and both its banks. When they were all the way open, the shadow of them lay across the whole reach of the Wend in the grey dawn from the reeds at her feet to the far bank at Harrow's End.

Beside her, the old bronze dragon made a sound. Very low.

The farmer said something. To her. To the dragon. She didn't hear what.

---

Orrun roared.

She felt it come up through him before it came. Through the bond. Through her hand, which was on his shoulder; she didn't remember putting it there. All the way up from the bottom of him, from all the years outside a door.

It was the question.

The sound from the knowe. From the knight's lock, with the dead man on the key. Low, and long, and going up at the end, and breaking. Louder than she'd ever heard it. Louder than the roar on the night of the field. It went out from the reeds across the half-league of river toward the thing standing in the water where the island had been, and every man on both banks heard it, and every beast.

The great grey head turned.

Toward the reeds. Toward the shore. Toward the sound.

She saw its eye.

The size of it. The age. It was pale. Pale like Orrun's. And it looked, for a moment, toward the reeds where the lion stood with a woman's hand on his shoulder, and three dragons, and a man.

She couldn't tell whether it saw him.

It turned its head away.

---

It flew.

Not fast, at first. The great wings came down, once, and the river under them went flat and white for half a league in every direction, and the reeds round her lay down in the mud, and the wind of it hit her in the face like a wall. It came up out of the water. All of it. With the Wend falling off it in a grey curtain, and the broken chains trailing from its legs, swinging.

Up.

The wings came down again.

It went up over the river, and over the Saltreach bank, slowly, enormous, with its shadow going ahead of it over the hill at Harrow's End where the Restoration's army stood. She saw them go flat. Thousands. Down in the mud, on their faces, as the shadow went over. It didn't look at them.

North-east. Over the Fellwood. Over a black crag standing out of the trees. Over the hills toward the Scarfell, white still on the tops. Climbing. Faster now. The dawn coming up behind the Saltreach hills, gold, and the great grey shape going up into it.

She watched it go.

It was a mountain in the sky, then a hill, then something you could cover with your hand held out at arm's length, then a bird, very high, going north-east over the Scarfell into the sunrise.

Then nothing.

---

On the Hearthmere bank, up at Redwatch, the lamps went out.

She'd seen them burning, at the tower, when they came up out of the reeds. The old lamps. The ones that burned without oil. Hung on the cracked red tower and along the knights' line by the water, burning white in the grey dawn, too white, the way the lamps had burned on and off all over the river since the day before. Since midnight they hadn't gone out at all.

They went out, all of them at once. No guttering, no dimming. The way a candle goes out when you close your fingers on it.

She looked downriver. At Greyhold on its hill, leagues off, with its split inner wall. There'd been lamps on the walls there too, she knew. White points in the grey.

There weren't.

She felt it through Orrun. Through the ground. The thing she'd felt under her boots all winter on the island, under the archive, through the black door, warm, humming, coming up from below. She felt it go. Draining. The way a river goes down after a flood, back off the fields into its bed, and then down in its bed, and down, until there's only mud. Going out of the ground under Hearthmere. Going away, north-east, after a speck in the sunrise.

---

It was very quiet.

The river was coming back. She could hear it. The Wend pouring into the hole where the island had been, filling it, the roar of it getting less as the hole filled. The whirlpool gone. Just water now, brown, going round and round in a great slow eddy where the island had been, and settling.

On both banks, nobody moved.

She stood in the reeds at the stream-mouth with her hand on Orrun's shoulder.

Beside her the farmer stood with the bundle in his hand. Behind him the old bronze dragon had lain down in the reeds with her torn wing out and her one gold eye open, looking north-east at the empty sky. The red one lay against her, the way he'd walked against her all the way up through the rock. The green one was standing in the mud beside the farmer with her head against his shoulder and the blue ribbon on her horn, shaking.

Nobody spoke.

The sun came up over the Saltreach hills.

---

"What was it?" said the farmer.

She looked at him.

He was looking north-east. At the place in the sky where it had gone. He didn't look at her. His face in the sunrise was tired, and filthy, and bloody down one side from a blow, and very calm. A barley farmer's face. The kind she'd seen at the Staithe every autumn of her life, selling sacks.

"The Watch had a name for it," she said.

She heard Hale say it, in the archive, with the cup in his hands. *Too big. Too angry. Too…*

"The Last Titan," said Maren.

The farmer was quiet for a while.

"The last," he said.

"That's what the songs say. The last great beast." She looked at the empty sky. "*Before the six, there was one.* The lullaby. Every child on the river knows it."

"*And the last great beast lay down below,*" said the farmer, very low. He'd sung it too, she thought. Somebody had sung it to him. "*Hush now, the last one's sleeping.*"

"It's not sleeping now."

"No."

He looked down at the bundle in his hand, the five links in the strip of his shirt. Then he put it inside his coat, with something else that clinked there, iron on iron.

"My mother has another verse," he said. "A third. That nobody sings." He looked at her for the first time since the sun came up. "*When two hands open what one hand shut, it wakes, and it will not sleep again.*"

She stood in the reeds and heard it.

*Two hands.* A dead man's, on the knight's lock, falling. And another, kneeling on the Ruin's lock, turning a key with the farmer's hand on his wrist, and smiling. Gone up an old stair. Not looking back.

"It will not sleep again," she said.

"No."

He looked at the lion. "There was more on the arch than you could read. The old one had it off a rubbing." His voice went low again, the way it had for the song. "*The last great one lay down to sleep below the stone, and its own kin kept the door.*"

---

Orrun turned his head.

Away from the north-east. Away from the empty sky where his kin had gone. He turned his great grey head and looked at her, beside him in the reeds, with her hand on his shoulder.

She felt what was in him. All of it. Through the door they'd made on the knowe. The grief. The touch in the dark at the bottom of the world, and nobody there. The question, and no answer.

And under the grief, something else. Something she'd felt in him on the knowe, the first night, when the chains broke and the beacon-stones rolled. Something that had come up the passage toward her when she was going out through the door, and stopped her going, and held her.

*It went north,* said Orrun. *It knows where it's going.*

"I know."

*It wants everything down.*

"I know."

He looked at her with his pale eyes.

*Somebody has to keep a watch,* said Orrun.

She stood in the reeds in the sunrise with her hand on the lion's shoulder, and the farmer beside her, and three dragons, and the river coming back into the place where the island had been.

She thought of an arch with six lines over it, gone now, fallen into the dark. *Keep the watch below, and do not ask.* Three hundred voices in a yard at noon, every day, facing a wall. Her own, among them. Two years of her own voice, and the last few weeks of it stopping, every day, on the second half, the way a hand won't close round something too hot.

The old ones' words. Not the knights'. Given to a great grey cat on a tower step, a long time ago, before anyone thought to put chains on anything. *Its own kin kept the door.*

She looked north-east, at the empty sky over the Scarfell, gold with the sunrise.

"Keep the watch," she said.

Not *below.* Not *and do not ask.* Just that. Out loud. To Orrun, and the farmer, and the three dragons, and the river.

The farmer looked at her. Then he looked north-east too, where the speck had gone, and nodded, once, as a man nods when something's been decided that he didn't decide, and finds he agrees with it.

The lion lay down in the reeds beside her, facing north-east, with his head up.

The green dragon lay down beside him.

Maren sat down in the mud against Orrun's shoulder, and the farmer sat down against the green dragon's, and they watched the sky where it had gone until the sun was well up over the hills, and the river had filled the hole where the island had been, and the people on both banks had begun, slowly, to move again.
