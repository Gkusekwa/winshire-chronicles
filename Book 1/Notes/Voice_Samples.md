# Voice samples: the opening of Chapter 1

*The same moment written three ways, to choose the series voice. The choice is made: B + C together (see `Style_Guide.md`). Samples A, B and C are kept only as superseded samples; their facts are out of date (B's "four winters" is now eight; C's "four hundred years" and A's "Holm girl" don't match canon). Greyhold and Lucan Haverly are now established names.*

## A. Grounded and plain *(superseded sample)*

The yard at Greyhold had been swept before dawn, and by the second bell it was mud again. Maren stood in the line of the unbonded with her practice sword point-down in the dirt and watched the beasts come in.

They came in with their students the way dogs come in with farmers, close at the knee, though none of them were dogs. A grey cat the size of a calf. Something long and low with a badger's stripe that Instructor Pell called a marten and nobody believed. The Holm girl's hawk, which hated everyone, sat on her fist and screamed at the swallows.

Maren counted them. Eleven this year. Last year, nine.

"You're doing it again," said Tobin.

"Doing what."

"Counting."

She didn't stop. He grinned and looked off across the river, where the Saltreach bank was a low brown line under smoke. Someone over there was burning stubble. Or someone over there was burning something.

## B. Lean and hard-edged *(superseded sample)*

Twelve beasts in the yard. Eleven students standing with them. One beast hadn't picked yet: the brindled thing by the well, all shoulder, watching the line of the unbonded like a buyer at market.

Maren didn't look at it. Looking was begging.

She was twenty-seven. Oldest in the yard by six years. She'd held the river watch through four winters and she could put a boy twice her weight on his back before he'd finished choosing a guard. None of that was on the list the beasts read from.

The brindled thing yawned. Walked. Stopped in front of Lucan Haverly, who had never had to wait for anything, and sat down on his boots.

The yard cheered. Maren cheered with it. You learned that too.

## C. Mythic and lyrical *(superseded sample)*

In Hearthmere they say a beast chooses the way rain chooses a field: it doesn't ask, it doesn't explain, and afterwards everyone pretends they knew. Maren Ashdown had heard this said at three funerals and one wedding, and she had come to Greyhold anyway, at twenty-seven, to be passed over in front of people.

The choosing was held in the long yard under the east wall, where the flagstones had worn hollow from four hundred years of people standing still. Old knights watched from the wall. New beasts walked the line below. And past all of them the river went by, brown and in no hurry, carrying the Saltreach smoke down to the sea.

## B + C together (chosen direction)

*C is the wide lens: the world, the river, the past, oaths and songs. B is the close lens: bodies, danger, talk. The prose moves between them as the camera moves in and out.*

### Maren (Chapter 1 sketch)

They say the river at Greyhold was here before the kingdoms and will be here after them, and that it has never once taken a side. It runs brown between two countries that don't trust each other and carries the silt of both down to the same sea. In spring the fishermen on both banks curse it in two accents. In winter it freezes from the edges in, and for a week or two a person could walk across into Saltreach, and every year someone tries, and every year the river takes one of them back.

The school sits on the Hearthmere bank where the water bends. It was a fort before it was a school and a quarry before it was a fort, and the yard is still quarry floor under the sand, so anyone who falls in it hard enough learns the difference.

Maren fell in it twice before the second bell.

The first time was her fault. The second time was Lucan Haverly's cat.

"Again," said Instructor Pell.

She got up. Sand in her collar, sand in her teeth. Haverly was back on his mark already, sword low, smiling the way he smiled at tavern girls and the chaplain. Behind him the cat lay along the fence rail, grey and calf-sized and bored, and watched her with its eyes half shut.

It hadn't touched her. It hadn't needed to. It had stood up, that was all, at the moment she committed her weight, and something older than her training had turned her head.

"Eyes on the man," Pell said.

"The man brought a cat."

A few laughs from the fence. Pell didn't laugh. "The man will bring a cat to every fight for the rest of his life. Again."

The third time she watched the man.

He was good. Quick feet, a wrist like a whip. He'd learned from a master in Ashford before he ever came here and it showed in everything but his patience. She gave him the opening he wanted, and he took it, and she was inside his guard before he'd finished being pleased with himself. Pommel to the breastbone. Heel behind his heel. Down.

Sand in his collar now.

The fence went quiet. Then the cat yawned, enormously, and somebody snorted, and it was over.

"Ashdown takes the bout," Pell said, and wrote on his slate. "Haverly takes first lance."

She had known he would say it. She had known since spring. She saluted anyway, sword to brow, and walked back to her mark and stood on it.

"First lance," Tobin said, low, when she reached the line. "For lying down."

"For the cat."

"You put him in the sand."

"And the cat would've put me in the ground." She wiped her mouth on her wrist. "Pell's right."

"Pell's a coward."

"Pell's alive. Most of his year isn't."

Tobin opened his mouth and shut it again. It was the most sense she'd had out of him all summer.

Then the shadows came over the yard.

Nobody shouted. You didn't, at Greyhold. But every head on the fence went up, and the cat flattened itself along its rail, and Maren was counting before she'd decided to.

Five. Six. Seven.

Clan dragons, high and in no order, following the river south the way geese follow it, as if the water were a road somebody had drawn for them. The sun came through their wings the colour of a cut. One banked low enough that she saw the rider, a dark knot strapped in at the shoulders, and then they were over the far bank and gone into the haze above Saltreach, where something had been burning since dawn.

"Seven," Tobin said. "War party."

"Funeral," said a boy behind him. "Their old chief's dead. They'll be fighting over the bones by harvest."

"Better their bones than ours."

That got a laugh. Maren didn't join it. She was watching the smoke.

At the noon bell they said the oath. They said it every day, bonded and unbonded together, facing east toward the river and the old stones of the inner wall, and they said it the way you say anything you learned before you understood it, which is to say without listening.

Maren listened. She always had.

*I will hold the river. I will keep the road. I will stand between the weak and what would harm them. I will keep the watch below, and I will not ask what I keep.*

Below what. Nobody knew. She'd asked Pell once, her first month, and he had looked at her the way you look at a horse someone has just told you is lame. "Below the river," he'd said, and walked off. The river had nothing below it but mud.

She said it anyway. *I will not ask what I keep.* Then the bell stopped, and they went in to eat.

### Calder (Chapter 2 sketch)

His mother had a saying for every weather and none of them were any use. *Red smoke at morning, the Clan's in mourning.* She said that one in the doorway with her shawl pulled tight, watching the pyre smoke from the hold smudge the north, and then she went back inside and sang the old one to Wren, the one about the six and the one.

Calder stayed out with the cow.

The cow had an abscess in her hoof and no opinions about the Clan. He liked her for it. He had her foot up on his knee and the knife ready when the dragons came over, low enough that she bawled and kicked and put him on his back in the muck.

He lay there and looked up. Seven of them, black against the cloud, making for the river.

"Well," he told the cow. "There goes the harvest."
