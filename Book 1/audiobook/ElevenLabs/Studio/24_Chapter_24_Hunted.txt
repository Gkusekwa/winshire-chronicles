Chapter Twenty-four.

Hunted.

There’s a word the Clan uses for a beast that everybody wants. Hearth-lost. Every hold has a claim on it and none of them has a fire for it, and in the end, the old men say, it goes into the woods and doesn’t come out.

Gorm Askell had come the afternoon before. Arn’s steward, the long sour man who’d counted the barley sacks every autumn for ten years, with four riders behind him in grey with the red thread. He’d stopped his horse at the gate and read Arn’s terms off a paper in his flat counting-house voice, and never once looked at the field. The beasts were Clan beasts. Bred in the Clan’s caves, raised by Clan riders, named by the Clan. They were to be brought to Varrhold within three days, and Calder with them, since it seemed they wouldn’t come without him. He’d have a place in the hold as their keeper. And a wage.

Keeper, Kharrak had said, and the red tide came up so fast that Calder had to hold the gatepost. I’ll keep him. I’ll keep him in my teeth.

“And if I don’t?”

“Then Arn Varr will come and fetch them,” Gorm Askell had said, folding his paper. “And you.” He’d looked at the field at last, quickly, and away. “He’d rather not. He says you covered his brother’s face. He remembers that. But he’ll do it.”

Ulric’s red-bearded man came on the fourteenth morning, down the Drover’s Way off the moor, with six behind him and a pack-horse. He got down at the gate, which Gorm Askell hadn’t, and leaned his arms on the top bar, and looked up at the three dragons in the field like a man at a horse fair who’s already decided what he’ll pay.

“Sten Raske,” he said. He hadn’t given his name on the green. “You know what I’m here for.”

“Same as the last one.”

“Not the same at all.” He grinned through the red beard. “Arn wants them back in the caves on chains, with you mucking out after them for a wage. Ulric’s seen what you are. Three bonds. There’s not a hold in the six kingdoms has heard of it. That’s not a keeper, Fenn. That’s a war-leader.”

He flicked the cloth off the pack-horse. Another chest. He didn’t bother to open it this time.

“Silver. For the barley I’ll not be getting from that mill. And for you, the war-leader’s place at Ulric’s table at Skarrow, the one Sigrun Holm sat in, gods rest her, with those three behind it.” He leaned on the gate. “Arn’s got two hundred spears. Ulric’s got four hundred and fifty. Every bonded pair in the Roosts has shut itself in rather than fight kin, so there’s three dragons in this war, and you’ve got all of them. Bring them east and it’s over by midwinter. You’d save more lives than any man in the Marches.”

He’s not lying, said Aurveth, very quietly. That is the trouble with him. Ulric would win, with us. And there would be a great many fewer pyres on the Scarfell this winter.

Then go, said Kharrak. Go east. Burn Arn. Burn them all.

Ulric’s war-leader took Dag off your back, child. Sigrun took him. I took him. I have his blood in my teeth still. And you took Sigrun. Whose side would you like to be on?

Kharrak said nothing at all. Calder felt the red tide go still, and something underneath it, much worse, come up.

Sorry, said Aurveth. Very low. That was cruel. I am more tired than I knew.

“No,” Calder said, aloud.

Sten Raske raised his eyebrows.

“I’m not saying no to you. I’m saying no to all of it. I’m not taking them east and I’m not taking them west. They’re not going on anybody’s chains and they’re not burning anybody’s village for anybody’s chair.” His voice didn’t sound like his. “Tell Ulric I’m sorry about his nephew. Tell him I don’t think he did it. And tell him no.”

The grin had gone.

“You don’t think he did it,” said Sten Raske.

“No.”

“Then who?”

Calder said nothing. He stood at his own gate with his hand on the post and his best coat on, because it was the only one not torn, and the chain heavy in the pocket against his hip.

Sten Raske threw the cloth back over the chest.

“He’ll not take that for an answer, Fenn. Nobody will. Not from a barley farmer with three war dragons in his field.” He swung up onto his horse. “Everybody wants them. And there’s no middle of the river.”

The sea man came that afternoon, up the Drover’s Way from Cotter’s Cross.

That was what Lisska called him afterwards. He was Saltreach, coast Saltreach by his voice, long vowels and short temper: a young man in a good leather coat with a sword at his hip and a hooded hawk on his fist. He had a paper, like Gorm Askell, but this one had a seal on it, a big red seal with a ship and a wave. Calder had seen that ship and wave before, pressed small into the tin tags the charm-sellers hawked at Cotter’s Cross, where it meant warranted, and mostly meant nothing.

“Lieutenant Corrin Ash. Of Captain Idris Vey’s company, out of Wendmouth, under contract to the Tide Council at Corsa.” He held the seal up for Calder to see. “Word gets to the hiring yard fast when it’s dragons, Master Fenn. The Captain holds the Council’s commission for the Marches. The Council thinks the Clan’s war is the Clan’s business. It’ll think three war dragons are everybody’s.” He smiled. He had very good teeth. “The Captain offers a contract. A wage, better than either Varr’s. You’d come south to Wendmouth, you and the beasts, and serve the Council.”

“Serve it how?”

“However it needs. There’s a great deal of coast. And a great many people who’d think twice about a great many things, if they knew the Council had three dragons.”

No, said Lisska, from the roof of the byre, before Calder could open his mouth. Not the sea. Imra said the sea people put hoods on everything. Look at that bird. It can’t see. Why has it got a hood on? I don’t want a hood.

“No,” said Calder.

The smile went, all at once.

“The Captain doesn’t usually ask twice,” said the lieutenant. “I’ll tell him you were asked once.”

Calder didn’t go in after. He sat down on the mounting block by the byre with his leg out stiff in front of him, and watched the hawk go off down the lane on its fist, and the light go off the hill. Three of them since yesterday. The new moon tonight, the elders’ day, and the Ashby widow’s face when she’d said it. He was still sitting there at dusk when he looked up and found a man at the gate.

He hadn’t heard him come. Alone, on foot. Middle years, middle height, in a good dark coat of plain cloth, with a pleasant open face and brown eyes, and a thumb that kept rubbing at his forefinger. He didn’t look at the dragons. He looked at Calder, and smiled, and nodded, as if they’d met before.

“Master Fenn. Forgive me. I didn’t mean to startle you.”

“Who are you?”

“Nobody of any importance.” He said it so simply that it might even have been true. “I speak for some people who’d like very much to help you.”

“Everybody wants to help me.”

“Yes. I’d heard.” The smile went rueful. “Arn wants you as a stable-hand, Ulric wants you as a sword, the Tide Council wants you as a threat. I don’t imagine any of them asked what you wanted.” He leaned on the gate, gently. “The people I speak for would. They’d ask. They’d listen. They believe a man like you ought to have a say in what’s done with what he’s been given. A great many things are going to change, Master Fenn, very soon, all over Winshire. You could be part of that. Not anyone’s keeper. Not anyone’s sword. Part of it.”

He took a purse out of his coat and set it on the top bar of the gate.

“That’s not a payment. It’s a courtesy, for the trouble of listening.” He took his hand away. “I’ll come again.”

Calder didn’t see which way he went. The lane was empty, and the dusk coming down, and the purse sitting on the gate.

I don’t like him, said Lisska. Very small. He smells cold. Why does he smell cold?

Calder got up off the block and went to the gate on his crutch and opened the purse.

New silver, so fresh from the die the coins still had a burr at the edge. He turned one over. A ship on one side, Saltreach coin, ordinary. A wave on the other. And on the edge, struck small into the rim where you’d never see it unless you were looking, as he’d never have seen it on a pommel without the light of a stable door on it:

A ring of six dots.

That’s the smell, said Lisska. That’s the cold. It’s the same as the thing in your coat. The heavy thing you won’t tell me about. Isn’t it?

“Yes.”

You promised you’d tell me.

“I know.”

Is it later now?

He drew the string tight. He didn’t take it into the house. He went down the lane on his crutch to where the beck went under the road in a stone culvert, and dropped the purse into the water, and watched it go down into the dark.

“Yes,” he said. “It’s later now.”

He told all three of them, sitting in the stubble in the dark at the top of the burned field with his back against Aurveth’s foreleg, where he’d sat the night his mother came out with the lantern.

He held the chain up, though it was too dark to see. He told them about Upper Rush and the ashes, and the ring of six on every link. About the man on the bucket at the stable door at Varrhold, with a face you forgot while you were looking at it, and good boots, and a ring of six dots on his knife. About Teo on the last step of the cliff path saying it out loud, the hour and the place, three paces from the bucket. About the empty bucket on the night of the pyre. Drew his wages the night before the ford. Off before light. About the seventeen words he’d carried in his pocket for more than a week and never said.

He told it badly, in bits, going back, starting again. Through the marks he could feel them listening all the way down, taking the cold of the chain and the bucket and the long slow sickness of the ford into themselves as he’d carried it, and he felt it land in them, one after another.

When he’d finished, nobody said anything.

Then Kharrak lifted his head.

Him, he said.

The red tide had gone. What came instead was cold, and very still, and very clear. Not rage. The place rage goes to, after.

Him. The bucket man. The cold man.

“I don’t know it was him.”

You know. I can feel you know. The great head came round in the dark until the breath went over Calder’s face, hot. You knew at the ford, with the boy in your arms. And you didn’t tell the boy. And you didn’t tell Arn. And the boy is dead, and Dag is dead, and Sigrun, and Imra, and the hold is at war with itself. And you knew.

There was nothing to say. It was true.

Kharrak, said Aurveth. He did not know. He feared. That is not the same, and you can feel the difference as well as I can, if you will stop shouting long enough to feel it. The great bronze head came down on Calder’s other side. He was afraid, and he waited, and he was wrong to wait. And the boy might have died anyway. The cold man might have found another hour and another ford. We will never know. And this one has carried it every hour since, alone, with no one to put it down in front of. A long breath went out of her. Well. He has put it down now. In front of us. That is a hard thing to do.

Kharrak didn’t answer. But he didn’t take his head away.

From the bottom of the field, where she’d been lying all through it, Lisska said, very small:

Thank you for telling me.

Calder put his face in his hands.

Is that the coin man too? said Lisska, after a while. The soft man at the gate. Is he the bucket man?

“No. A different one. The same mark.”

So there’s more than one of them.

“Yes.”

How many?

“I don’t know.”

Then we’ll find out, said Kharrak. Low. Cold. All of them.

Nobody told him not to.

They planned it at the kitchen table that night, Calder and Ada, with Wren asleep upstairs and the shutters closed.

It was the new moon. He’d said he’d go, and he’d go: tomorrow night, when they’d had a day to get ready. Not up the lane through the village in front of everybody, and not by daylight, with Gorm Askell’s riders and Sten Raske’s and Corrin Ash’s all watching the roads.

“Up the Drover’s Way,” he said. “Tomorrow, late. Past the ford, up onto the moor, over toward the Scarfell. There’s high ground up there nobody farms. Caves. Kharrak can walk. Aurveth can glide a bit, from height.” He looked at his mother. “And you and Wren won’t be here.”

“Calder.”

“You won’t, Ma. When they come for the dragons and find them gone, they’ll come to the house.”

Ada looked at him across the table.

“Upper Rush,” she said at last. “Bel’s. Joss’d never turn anybody away.”

“Hob’ll drive you. In the cart.” He’d asked Hob that morning. He’d gone down to the black heap of the mill on his crutch and found Hob raking through the ashes for nails, and Hob had looked at him a long time and said all right in a strange flat voice and gone back to raking. “He’ll come up at midnight. You’ll go by the river lane, the back way, along the water. Nobody’ll be watching that. They’ll be watching the Way.”

“And you’ll be on the Way.”

“Yes.”

“With three dragons.”

“Yes.”

Ada got up and went to the dresser and began, slowly, to take things down and wrap them in cloths: the good knife, the salt box, the little brass candlestick that had been her mother’s, the knotted hearth-cord from over the door, which she said had never once kept anything out but she’d not leave it to strangers. Packing. Not crying. He’d never seen her cry. He thought perhaps she’d done all her crying before he was born.

“Hob,” she said, with her back to him.

“I know.”

“He’s not been right since the mill.”

“I know, Ma. There’s nobody else with a cart.”

She went on wrapping things.

Lisska came back at dusk the next day with blood on her muzzle and no deer.

She came in fast and low over the Fellwood, and he felt her before he saw her, a high thin clamour in the green mark like a bell rung too hard. She landed so badly in the yard that she went over on her side, and scrambled up, and came to him on the step with her wings still half open.

Men, she said. Men in the trees. On the Way, above the ford, where you said you’re going. Lots of them. Horses. Hidden in the dark under the trees on both sides, waiting. They didn’t see me. Lots.

He sat down on the step.

And before that. At the mill. The black place. I always go over it, I like the smell of the ash, I don’t know why. She was shaking. He could feel it through the mark. The miller was there. The one who doesn’t like you. Hob. And the soft man. The coin man. Standing in the black. And the soft man gave Hob a bag, and it smelled cold, like the coin, like the chain. And Hob took it.

The yard was very quiet.

Did I do right? said Lisska. To tell you?

“Yes, little one. You did right.”

He sat on the step in the dusk.

He thought of Hob at the fair with a cup of his own ale, laughing at old Garrow’s knees. Hob crying in the bracken with his face in his arm. Hob at the gate with the elders. I lost my mill, Calder. My father’s mill. And you came out of it with three dragons.

All right, Hob had said, raking the ashes.

He knew the road. He knew the hour. He knew Ma and Wren would be in his cart.

Calder got up off the step and went in.

“You’re not going,” he said to Ada. “Neither of you. Not to Bel’s. Not in Hob’s cart.”

She stood in the middle of the kitchen with the brass candlestick in her hands, wrapped in its cloth.

He told her. When he’d finished she put the candlestick down on the table, very carefully, as if it might break.

“Hob,” she said.

“Yes.”

“For silver.”

“For silver. And the mill. And because he’s frightened, Ma, and he thinks it’s my fault, and maybe it is.” He sat down. “It doesn’t matter. They think I’m going up the Way tonight with three dragons, and they think you and Wren will be in a cart on the river lane where anybody can stop it and hold it till I come back for you. So you’re staying here, with the door barred. And when they come and find the dragons gone and me gone, you’ll tell them the truth. That you don’t know where. Because you won’t.”

“Calder.”

“It’s me they want. Without me here, you’re an old woman and a girl in a farmhouse, and they’ll leave you be.” He said it as if he believed it. He did, mostly. “With me here, you’re the thing they hold till I come out.”

“Where will you go?”

“Not up the Way.”

“Where?”

He looked at the shutters. At the dark beyond them, where the field was, and above the field the long black shoulder of the hill, and above that the forest.

“In,” he said.

Wren came down the stairs in her nightgown with her shawl round her before he’d gone up to wake her, and stood on the bottom step and looked at his face, as she had on the night of the ford.

“You’re going,” she said.

“Yes, chick.”

“Without me.”

He knelt on the kitchen floor in front of her, which hurt his leg and his ribs so badly he had to stop halfway, and took her hands.

“It’s not safe. For you, with me. People want them. They’d use you to get to me.”

She was nine. She understood perfectly.

“Will you come back?”

“Yes.”

“Promise?”

“Promise.”

“Lisska says that,” said Wren. “Promise. She says it all the time. She doesn’t know what it means.” Her chin was going. She held it still. “Does she know what it means now?”

“I think she’s learning.”

Wren pulled her hands out of his and went past him to the door, and unbarred it, and went out into the yard in the dark without her boots.

He went after her on his crutch.

Lisska was waiting by the step. Wren went straight to her across the cobbles in her bare feet and put her arms round the long green neck, as far as they’d go, and put her face against the scales.

Lisska went still as a hare in its form.

“You look after him,” said Wren, into the scales. “You hear me? You bring him back.”

I will, said Lisska. Tell her I will. Tell her I promise. Tell her I know what it means.

“She says she will,” said Calder. His voice wasn’t working very well. “She says she promises. She says she knows what it means.”

Wren let go. She touched the blue ribbon at the base of the green horn, once, with one finger. Then she walked back across the yard past Calder without looking at him, into the house, to her grandmother, and the door shut.

They went into the Fellwood at midnight, the four of them.

Not up the Way. East, straight up the slope of the burned field behind the farm, over the top hedge, across the high pasture where his father had grazed the ewes, and in under the first of the great oaks. Kharrak walked, with his torn wing folded tight and dragging a little. Lisska went ahead, low, flitting from tree to tree like a bat. And Aurveth carried him.

He’d thought he’d walk, and hobble on his crutch beside them till his leg gave out. But at the top of the field she stopped, and lowered her great head to the ground in front of him, and stretched her neck out along the stubble like a bridge.

Up, she said. Behind my head, where the neck is narrowest. There’s a ridge there, between the plates. Sigrun sat there sometimes, when she was tired, and wanted to talk to me without the saddle between us. Hold the ridge. Not my horns; I don’t like it. I’ll not go fast.

He’d never been on anything bigger than a dray-mare. He climbed onto her neck in the dark with his crutch and his bad leg and his broken ribs, and found the ridge, and held on, and she lifted her head, slowly, and the ground went away below him.

He was higher than the roof of the house. He could see the whole valley in the starlight: the farm, the house with its shutters closed and a crack of light at the edge, the black field, the village, the black heap of the mill, the river going silver beyond it, the island in the middle of the river, dark.

And away up the Way, where it climbed toward the moor, nothing. Just the dark, and somewhere in it the men who were waiting.

Ready? said Aurveth.

He looked at the crack of light at the edge of the shutters.

“Yes.”

She turned, and went into the forest.

The Fellwood took them like water.

He’d never been further in than the edge, where the charcoal burners worked and the swine-herds took their pigs. Nobody went further. Aurveth went in under the oaks with her head low and her wings folded tight, and the oaks closed over them, and the stars went out.

It was the size of the trees he’d remember. A league in, the oaks were wider than his house, their trunks going up into the dark like the posts of a tithe barn, so far apart that Aurveth could walk between them without folding her wings. The ground was soft and deep with leaves and moss, and silent. Nothing grew down there but ferns as tall as a man, in great pale drifts, and toadstools as big as plates, glowing faintly green. The air smelled of rot and earth and something sweet, like old honey.

They went on all night, east and up. Kharrak behind, breathing hard. Lisska ahead, a flicker in the dark, calling back. This way. Mind the stream. There’s a fallen one, go round. This way.

Once, deep in the night, something big moved out under the trees to their left. A slow heavy tread, the crack of a branch, a long snuffling breath. It stopped. It had smelled them. He felt it stand out there and think about three dragons, like weather making up its mind. Then it went away.

Bear, said Lisska. A very big one. Nearly as big as me. They always go away from Aurveth.

Sensible, said Kharrak.

Calder sat on Aurveth’s neck in the dark and found he was crying again, quietly. For the crack of light at the shutters. For Wren in her bare feet in the yard. For Hob, raking the ashes.

He felt the three of them feel it. Aurveth went a little slower. Kharrak came up close behind, so close Calder could feel the heat of him, and Lisska came back out of the dark and flew beside him for a while, low and silent, with her wingtip almost brushing his knee.

They came to Crow Scar at dawn.

It stood up out of the treetops alone, two leagues east of the farm and far up the slope of the Scarfell: a great black crag, so high its top was in the cloud. The forest went right up round its foot and stopped, and the rock went on, bare and black and streaked white where the crows had nested on its ledges for a thousand years. They went up off the ledges now in hundreds, in a black clamour, as the dragons came out from under the trees.

Three-quarters of the way up its face there was a cave, a great dark mouth with a ledge in front of it like a lip.

There, said Lisska. I found it, hunting. It’s dry. It goes back a long way. Nobody’s been in it for years and years, only bats, and a bear once, but the bear’s dead. There’s bones. She went up to the ledge in a long climbing spiral and turned round on it, very pleased. See? Big enough for all of us. And nobody can get up. Not without wings.

Aurveth looked up at it for a long time.

It will do, she said.

She couldn’t fly that far, not yet. She climbed, foreclaws in the rock, wings half open for balance, with Calder flat on her neck and his arms round the ridge and his eyes shut. Kharrak came behind with his good wing out and his torn one tight, cursing in fragments the whole way. Then they were on the ledge, and in the mouth of the cave, and out of the wind.

He slid off Aurveth’s neck onto cold stone and lay there.

Below them the Fellwood went away in every direction, green and black and endless under the morning mist. West, very far off, he could see the line of the hill, and a thread of smoke that might have been Harrow’s End. Everything else was trees.

Nobody would find them here. You could lose an army in the Fellwood. You could lose three dragons in it, and a man, and never see them again.

Hearth-lost, he thought.

They lay up in Crow Scar for three days. He slept most of it. When he wasn’t sleeping he lay in the mouth of the cave with his leg up on a stone and his ribs aching, and watched the forest, and listened to them.

Aurveth slept too, long and deep, with her torn back to the wall, and he could feel it drawing closed through the mark, slowly, like a seam being sewn. Kharrak didn’t sleep. He lay in the mouth of the cave with his torn wing stretched out in the sun, and flexed it, over and over, and swore when it hurt, and flexed it again. Lisska hunted, and brought back deer, and went out again, further each time.

On the third day she came back at dusk without any deer at all.

There’s a girl, she said.

He was half asleep. He opened his eyes.

On the island. The old stone one in the river. I went right out to the edge of the trees and over the hills to the river, to see, and down the river, and there’s a girl on the island. She settled on the ledge, quick and bright. A grey one. River grey, like the boats. With her arm all tied up in white. Sitting on the bank of a stream by herself at the end of the day.

Calder sat up.

And on the other side of the stream, in the trees, a cat. A huge cat, grey, with all its fur round its head like a cloud, and iron round its neck, and it’s hurt, it’s holding its foot up. Just standing there looking at her. And she’s just sitting there. Not moving. Neither of them. Not doing anything at all.

The cave was very quiet.

It didn’t eat her, said Lisska. Why didn’t it eat her? It’s bigger than a bear. It could eat her in one bite. I would have. I mean, I wouldn’t. But I could have. And it just looked at her, and then it went away into the trees, and she went the other way, up to the stone tower. She cocked her head. Is that the old thing? The one that roared, the night of the fire?

Calder looked out of the mouth of the cave, west, over the dark sea of the Fellwood, toward where the river must be.

Old, something had said, in the field.

“I don’t know,” he said. “Maybe.”

Can I go back and see?

“No.”

Why not?

He sat in the mouth of the cave and thought about a girl in river grey, alone, with her arm bound up in white, and the thing across the water from her.

She must be very brave, said Lisska. Or very stupid.

“Imra said those were mostly the same,” said Calder.

Lisska went very still.

Yes, she said, after a long time, very quietly. She did. You remembered.

He hadn’t known he would.
