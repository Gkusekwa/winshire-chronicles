Chapter Seventy-seven.

Midnight.

The stair went down a long way.

She sat on Orrun’s back in the mane with the lantern held up and her other hand knotted in the coarse grey hair, and the stair went down and round and down, and the rock moved out of their way as they went.

She felt it. Through the bond. Through his body under her. The stone of the stair walls groaning back from his shoulders, the roof lifting over his head, the steps under his feet settling and widening. The way the river moves round a rock. The old grey stone of Greyhold’s hill making room for him, step by step, all the way down, because it knew what he was.

It remembers, said Orrun. This stone. It remembers the old ones. It remembers before.

She didn’t ask him how he knew. She could feel him know it.

The hum came up through him into her, stronger with every turn of the stair. Louder. Deeper. Until it wasn’t a hum any more but a sound, a great slow sound like a bell rung under water, and the lantern in her hand shook with it.

And under the sound, now, the chains.

She’d heard them through the black door. Iron drawing over stone, far down. Drag, and knock. This wasn’t that. This was the chains going taut. All of them. And holding. A groan of iron so deep and so long it made her teeth ache. Holding. Holding.

It’s pulling, said Orrun. It knows. It knows the hour’s come.

The stair came out onto a ledge.

She’d known it would. Hale had told her. Stairs, going down. And then the stairs stop, and there’s a ledge. She’d heard it in the archive in the yellow circle of the brass lamp with Hale’s big hands flat on the table. She’d seen it in her head ever since. She’d never thought she’d see it.

Orrun stepped off the last step onto it and the walls went away.

Dark. Enormous. The lantern light went out from them into nothing and didn’t come back. At the edge of the ledge, close enough to touch, going down past them into the dark from somewhere high above: chain. One link of it taller than a man, as high as Orrun’s chest. Black. Rust gone hard on it like bark.

Beside it another, and another. She counted. Six.

Taut. Humming. A note so low she felt it in her chest more than heard it. On the inside of the nearest link, in the curve where the iron bent round, where you’d never look, there was light. A thin white line of it, broken into small square marks with hooks at the ends. Writing. Struck into the iron and burning now from inside it like the filament in a lamp-glass, the same marks over and over all the way round the curve. In the next link the same. And the next, going down into the dark, a row of small white burnings on every chain, as far as she could see. All six of them leaning out over the dark below, toward the east, toward the river, as if something down there at the far end of them were pulling with all its weight.

Hale had stood here at twenty-three with Merrow holding the lantern out over the edge. That’s what you’re keeping, lad.

She held the lantern out over the edge.

The dark below moved.

There, said Orrun.

He’d turned his head. Along the ledge. To the left. Where the ledge went round the curve of the rock wall, there was light. Lanterns. More than one. And voices. And a passage, square, cut into the rock.

She could feel him feel it. Through the bond. Through the floor. A room off the ledge, with men in it, and in the middle of the men, kneeling, one man with his hand on iron.

Waiting.

How long? she said.

Not long, said Orrun. The candle’s nearly at its mark.

She didn’t ask him how he knew about the candle. She could see it, through him. A fat candle in a glass, on a stone floor, burning down to a row of small notches scored in its side.

“Go,” she said.

He went along the ledge and into the passage at a run, and she lay flat on his neck, and the walls of the passage were carved with the old square hooked letters, row on row, the same as the verse over the arch, and every one of them was alight. White, from inside the stone, the way the lamps on Greyhold’s wall had gone white at noon. They went past her so fast they blurred into lines.

The room was round.

She saw it all at once as they came out of the passage. A low dome of rock. Lanterns on the floor round the walls. Men, in grey, with the six on their coats, in the coats of the companies, standing round the walls with swords and spears. Eighteen, twenty. And the floor.

A great disc of grey stone. Smooth. Carved in rings of the old letters, round and round, smaller and smaller toward the middle, and the letters were lit like the passage, a faint white in the grey, brightening and fading and brightening, like a coal when you blow on it. And in the middle of it, an iron boss as big as two hands.

Kneeling on the boss, with his hand on a long black key, and a candle in a glass burning on the stone beside him, a man. Thorne’s key, the dead woman’s copy, in the boss to the bow.

Tall. Thin. Long pale face, clean-shaven, dark hair cut short. Grey eyes.

The man from the hedge-wain. The man from the smithy roof. The same face.

He looked up.

The men round the walls broke.

She’d seen it in the yard. She saw it again. A lion ten feet at the shoulder came out of the passage into the lamplit room with a woman on its back and its mane up, and the ground in the room heaved under it, and the men round the walls went back from it into the rock as if they could go through it. Some of them ran. Out of the passage behind Orrun, past him, round him, along the ledge in the dark. He let them. Some of them dropped their spears and stood against the wall with their hands up.

One didn’t.

He came across the stone disc at her with a sword in his left hand, his good hand, and his right arm strapped to his chest in a splint, where she’d broken it with Tobin’s blunt practice sword on the island.

Callis.

She slid off Orrun’s neck to meet him.

She didn’t know why. Orrun could have had him. She felt Orrun want to. She felt it go through the bond, hot, and she put her hand on his shoulder as she slid down, and he stopped.

Mine, she thought at him. Not in words. This one’s mine.

Callis came at her across the carved stone. His face was white and wild. He’d lost his helm somewhere. He was shouting a word as he came, past her, at Orrun: the stand-word, the one the old knights said to a frightened horse. She felt it come at them and go by, like a thrown stone past a boat, and saw his face when it did. He swung at her with his left hand, clumsily, and she did what Pell had taught her in the yard at Greyhold against the straw men and against Haverly and against Callis himself, two years ago, on her first day, when he’d put her on her back on the cobbles and laughed.

Close. Take the weapon arm.

She took it. The flat of the river watch sword across his left wrist, hard, where the bone was close under the skin. The sword went out of his hand and rang on the stone. She stepped in and put her shoulder into him, and he went down on the carved stone on his back, with both arms useless, and his head hit the stone.

He lay there.

She stood over him with the sword. She could have. It was easy. He was a man on his back on the floor with both arms broken, who’d opened the river gate tonight for three hundred men and taken a dead woman’s key off her body in the gateway and locked the black door behind him so nobody could follow, and put a sword into an old man sitting on a step.

Subdue, said Pell, in the gateway, with his back to the gate. Don’t kill.

Unless you have to.

She didn’t have to.

She stepped over him.

The man on the boss was smiling at her.

Not the smile from the hedge-wain, warm and wide and meant for seven hundred. Something else. Real. Delighted. Like a man who’s been waiting at a crossroads for a friend in the rain, and the friend’s come.

“Ashdown,” he said.

She stopped.

“Maren Ashdown. The knight with the lion. Of course.” He looked past her at Orrun, standing in the mouth of the passage with his mane up, enormous, and his face lit up even more. “And there it is. There it is. Oh, look at it. Look at it.” His voice cracked. “You came. You came home.”

He didn’t know her face.

She saw it. He was looking at her the way he’d looked at her at the gate of the field under the thorn hedge, going past with seven hundred people round him: friendly, glad, and not knowing her at all. He knew the lion. He knew her name because he knew the lion. He didn’t know her.

The crowd, she’d told herself, on the hill above Fennick’s. Seven hundred faces.

The other one had known her name in the dark at Ferriby. This one had never seen her in his life.

He glanced down at the candle. The flame had come to a notch.

“Four,” said Cassian, and looked back up at her.

“Stop,” she said.

He looked at her.

“Stop. Take your hand off it. Please.” She came across the stone toward him, a step, two. “You don’t know what it is. You don’t know what’s down there. Nobody does. Hale didn’t. The Watch didn’t. Your brother doesn’t. Nobody’s seen it in three hundred years. You don’t know what it’ll be when it comes up.”

“I know what it’ll be,” said Cassian gently. “It’ll be the one realm.” He looked at her with his grey eyes full of something that might have been tears. “Maren, it’s under your feet. It’s been under your feet your whole life. Every lamp you ever read by. Every word they licensed. Every knight who stood at the front of the line in the yard while you stood at the back. All of it bled out of something in chains.”

He looked down at the candle, and back up.

“My father stood at the back of that line for thirty years,” he said. “A knight of the gate. Hearthmere born. Nothing ever came down out of the sky to his fist. He said the words at noon every day of his life with his face to that wall, and when he died they buried him outside it, with the grooms.” Something nearly a smile. “Corvin and I stood at the back with him, when we were small. Two boys with one face. Nobody looked at us twice either.”

His voice rose. “We’re letting it go, Maren. That’s all. Help me let it go.”

“Into a collar.”

“Three,” said Cassian, with his eyes on the flame.

“You’ll put a collar on it. Like the hounds. Like Old Grey. Like the lion, on the island, with the honey-smoke, with Odo’s hammers inside.” She was close now. A pace off. “You’ll let it go out of one chain into another, and you’ll call it the one realm.”

Cassian looked at her.

Something went across his face. Quick. The same thing that had gone across Tobin’s face at the barricade, at the foot of the jetty road, with the fire behind him. Nobody said about the camp.

Then it was gone.

“Two,” he said.

His hand tightened on the key.

She did what she had to.

She’d think about it for the rest of her life. That it was quick. That there wasn’t any decision, at the end. That she’d been deciding for a long time, on the ice and in the pit and on the knowe and in the gateway with Pell sitting down, and when it came there was nothing left to decide.

Unless you have to.

She put the river watch sword into him.

Under the arm, the way the river watch teaches you when there’s a man in a boat with a knife and your life or his, low, up, through the coat. As he said one. As his hand turned on the key.

He looked at her.

Very surprised. The grey eyes wide. His mouth a little open on the word. He looked at her the way the boy Davin had looked at her through the bars of the cell, glad, when he saw her face and said you asked.

His hand didn’t come off the key.

It went round. With his weight on it. With him falling, forward, onto the boss, onto the key, his hand still closed round the bow of it, and the key turning under him as he fell, a quarter-turn, as if it wanted to.

And the whole stone disc turned under her feet.

She felt it go. Grinding. A quarter of a turn. The rings of carved letters sliding past each other, ring past ring, under her boots, and stopping. In a line. From the rim to the boss.

The line lit. All of it at once, rim to boss, white, so bright it came up through the soles of her boots and she saw the bones of her own hand on the sword hilt. A sentence, in a script she couldn’t read. It burned there under the dead man and didn’t fade.

And in the same breath, a long way off, under the river, the same sound.

She felt it through the stone. Through her boots. Through the bond, through Orrun, who felt it the way he felt everything in the ground. Far off. East. Under the river, under the island. A great slow grinding turn. The same. At the same moment as this one.

Two, she thought.

Cassian lay across the iron boss with his hand on the key and his cheek on the cold carved stone, and didn’t move.

The lock had given way under a dead man’s hand.

The seal broke.

Not here.

She’d thought it would be here. She’d braced for it. The floor. The dome. The whole hill of Greyhold coming down on her. It didn’t. The stone disc under her feet held. The dome held. The chamber shook, and dust came down, and the lanterns round the walls went over and two of them went out, but it held.

It broke there.

She felt it. Through Orrun. Through the rock under the river. Far off, east, under the island. Something falling. Something huge. A floor going out from under itself, and a chamber, and a ledge, and above the ledge a tower and an arch and a fort and old broken walls, all of it, cracking and going down into the dark with a sound she couldn’t hear and felt in every bone she had.

The island, she thought. The Old Ruin.

The arch. The verse. The hollow where he lay.

Gone.

Below the ledge, outside the passage, in the dark, the six chains made a sound she’d never heard iron make. A note. High, and rising, and rising, like a rope just before it parts.

And Orrun reached.

She felt him do it. She was standing on the turned stone with the sword in her hand and the dead man at her feet and she felt him do it, through the bond, all the way down.

He’d been reaching all winter. She knew that now. She’d felt it on the knowe, the night the bond was made, in the flood of everything that came through the door: the pit, the stone under his claws, scraping, every night, every night. Kin. The pull she’d felt on the knowe. Reaching down through a mile of rock toward something he could feel and couldn’t touch.

The seal was open.

He could touch it.

He reached, as you’d put your hand out in the dark to a face you’ve known all your life. Down. Through the open seal. Under the river. Into the dark at the bottom of the six chains, where the thing that had been sleeping was awake now, all the way, for the first time in three hundred years.

It was there.

It was open.

He touched it.

She’d never be able to say what it was.

The words wouldn’t go round it. It was too big. It was the size of the dark. It was the size of the river and the hill and the cavern and the night and it filled all of them, and Orrun’s touch on it was a fly on the skin of something the size of the world, and through Orrun she was the fly’s foot.

It was awake.

It was old. Older than the kingdoms. Older than the bridge and the road and the one realm. She felt that. She felt, very far back in it, like a light at the far end of a passage, what it had been: a sleeper. Great, and old, and kin. Lying down below the stone with the old ones gone into the light and its own kin keeping the door. Asleep. At peace. A long time.

And then hands.

And then chains.

Three hundred years of chains. She felt them. Every year. Every link. The iron going in. The bleeding. The lamps going up in Hallowmere off its blood. The knights at noon, every day, three hundred voices, a hand on the chain, keep the watch below, every day, every day, three hundred years of every day.

It had been a sleeper.

It wasn’t now.

There was nothing in it now but the chains, and the hate of the chains, and under the hate, so huge she couldn’t see the edges of it, a want. Like hunger. Like the river going to the sea.

Far off.

She felt it lean, the way the chains leaned, away from here, toward somewhere a long way off that it knew and she didn’t. It had always known. It wanted to go there.

And it wanted the world down.

All of it. The kingdoms on top of it and the crowns and the walls and the lamps and the roads. Down. The way it had been taken down. Every hand that had ever been on the chain, and every hand that hadn’t but had read by the lamps. Everything. Flat, like a valley after a flood.

It didn’t know Orrun.

Or it knew him, and it didn’t care. She couldn’t tell which was worse. He touched it, his kin, the thing he’d kept the door for, the thing he’d scraped at the stone over all winter, every night, alone, in the dark, and it didn’t turn toward him. It didn’t turn toward anything. There was nothing left in it that turned toward anything. It was all one way, like a river in flood, going down.

A destroyer.

That was the word that came. Orrun’s, not hers, and only the shape of a word, from very far back in him, from before the bridge, from what the old ones had told him once before they went into the light.

This one was never that.

They made it one.

Orrun’s grief came through the bond.

She went down on her knees.

On the turned stone. Beside the dead man with his hand on the key. With the sword falling out of her hand and ringing on the stone, and her hands over her face, and her whole body shaking. It wasn’t hers. It came through the door they’d made on the knowe the way the river had come through the ice: all at once, everywhere, cold, too much. She couldn’t hold it. Nobody could have held it.

All those years outside a door.

Keeping a watch.

The thing he’d kept it for was gone. Not dead. Worse than dead. Still there, and not there. The face you’ve known all your life turning toward you in the dark, and nobody behind it.

She knelt on the stone and wept for something that wasn’t hers, and she couldn’t stop, and behind her in the mouth of the passage the lion stood with his head down and made the sound she’d heard on the knowe. The question. Low, and long, and going up at the end, and breaking.

Nothing answered it.

Something came down the stair behind them.

She heard it. Wings, scraping on rock. Claws on steps. Coming down, fast, round and round, the way they’d come, through the stone Orrun had opened. A small bright voice going ahead of it, frightened, that she couldn’t hear and could feel. Through him.

Lisska came out of the passage into the chamber.

Green, filthy, with her wings folded tight and scraped raw from the stair and the blue ribbon still on her horn. She came across the turned stone floor past the men against the walls with their hands up, past Callis on his back, past the dead man on the boss, to Orrun. And lay down beside him. Close. Against his shoulder. The way Maren had sat against it all winter.

She put her head against his.

Maren felt it go between them. Kin to kin. A small bright warmth going into the great grey grief, like a candle held up in a cellar.

Then Lisska lifted her head and looked east.

Through the rock. Under the river. Toward where the island had been. She didn’t have to say anything. Maren could feel it through Orrun as plain as her own heart.

He’s there. He fell. He’s down there. In the dark. With the others.

I want to go.
