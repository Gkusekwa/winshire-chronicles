“The man will bring a bird to every fight he has for the rest of his life,” he said. “So will the man who kills you. Again.”

The third time, she watched the man.

He was good. That was the trouble with Haverly; it would have been easier if he weren’t. He had quick feet and a wrist like a coachman’s whip, and he’d been taught by a master in Ashford before he ever came to Greyhold, and it showed in everything but his patience. He was twenty-one. He had never had to wait for anything in his life, and so he didn’t.

She gave him the opening he wanted, a little low on her left, a little slow on the recovery. He took it. She let his blade go past her ribs close enough to feel the air move, and then she was inside his guard before he’d finished being pleased with himself. Pommel to the breastbone. Heel behind his heel. Down.

Sand in his collar now.

[dryly] The fence went quiet. On her post, Kesh turned her head all the way round and looked at /ˈmɛərən/ out of one flat gold eye. Then somebody at the back snorted, and somebody else said oh, well done in the voice you use for a good throw at skittles, and it was over.

[calm] Pell wrote on his slate. He took his time about it. Haverly got up on his own and brushed himself down and offered his hand across the mark, which was the worst part, because he meant it.

“Ashdown takes the bout,” Pell said. He tucked the chalk behind his ear. “Haverly takes first lance.”

Nobody on the fence looked surprised. Pell didn’t look at /ˈmɛərən/ at all. He looked at the falcon, and then at his slate, and then he called the next pair. [long pause]

She had known he would say it. She had known since spring, since the morning the lists went up for the summer bouts with her name at the bottom, where the late-oath candidates always were, and Haverly’s at the top, where the bonded always were.

No rule said first lance went to the bonded. Nobody had to write one. On the chapel wall there was a board with every first lance of Greyhold painted on it in gold, sixty-one names, and /ˈmɛərən/ had read them all in her first week. Fifty-nine had ridden out with a beast at their side. The other two were from the year of the fever, when there hadn’t been enough bonded students left to choose from.

The gate said there was nothing to explain. The board explained it anyway.
