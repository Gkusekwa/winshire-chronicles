She saluted. Blade to brow, as you were taught. Then she walked back to her mark and stood on it.

First lance meant riding at the front of the knights’ patrol when the school went out on the river road in autumn. It meant your name read first at the muster, and the first posting when the year was done, and a good horse instead of whatever the stables had left. In a war it would mean dying first. There wasn’t a war. There hadn’t been one on this stretch of the river in sixty years, which was why first lance was worth having, and why it always went to someone with a beast at his shoulder.

Tobin was waiting for her at the end of the line. He was nineteen, all elbows, unbonded like her and much louder about it.

[low] “First lance,” he said, low. [low] “For lying down.”

[calm] “For the bird.”

“You put him in the sand.”

“And the bird would’ve put me in the ground.” She wiped her mouth on her wrist. Blood, a little. She’d bitten the inside of her cheek on the second fall. “Pell’s right.”

“Pell’s a coward.”

“Pell’s alive. Most of his year isn’t.”

Tobin opened his mouth and shut it again. It was the most sense she’d had out of him all summer.

Down the line the bonded students were drifting back to their beasts like people to a fire. There were eleven of them this year. Last year there had been nine. /ˈmɛərən/ counted them without meaning to, because she counted most things without meaning to: the brindled hound that belonged to the Darrow girl, the long low creature with a badger’s stripe that Pell insisted was a marten and nobody believed, Haverly’s falcon, a grey cat the size of a calf asleep in the only shade. Each of them with a person beside it who stood a little differently from everyone else. You could see it if you knew how to look. As if they were never quite alone in their own bodies.

Her father had stood like that.

[sadly] She remembered his dog better than she remembered his face, which she was not proud of. It had been a big rough-coated thing with a jaw like a mantrap and a habit of leaning its whole weight on your legs, and it had followed him from the stable to the river and back every day of her childhood. When a poacher’s bolt took it in the throat on the far bank one autumn, her father carried it home across the ferry himself. He sat with it in the barn all night. [pause]
