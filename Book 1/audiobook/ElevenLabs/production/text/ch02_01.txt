Chapter Two.

Across the River. [long pause]

His mother had a saying for every weather, and none of them were any use.

[slowly] Red smoke at morning, the Clan’s in mourning. She said that one in the doorway with her shawl pulled tight, looking north to where the smoke from /ˈvɑːhəʊld/ lay along the hills like a stain on a sleeve. It had been lying there three days. Everyone in Harrow’s End knew what it meant. Haldor /vɑː/ was dead, and the Clan was burning him slowly, the way they burned chiefs, so the smoke could be seen in every valley he had ever taxed.

[calm] “It’s a poor rhyme,” /ˈkɔːldə/ said.

“It’s an old one.” Ada Fenn went back in to the fire. “Old ones don’t have to be good. They have to be true.”

On the /ˈsɔːltriːtʃ/ bank of the Wend they say the river was put there to keep the knights off, and that it has done a fair job of it. The farms on that side are older than the kingdom they belong to, if a league of port-cities with no king can be called a kingdom, which on the river it always is, and most of them have never seen a tax collector, because /ˈsɔːltriːtʃ/ keeps its collectors in the port-cities on the coast, a long way south, where the money is. What the Marches farmers pay, they pay to the Clan. Grain at harvest, wool at shearing, a calf when the hold asks for one. In return the Clan keeps the sellswords out of the valley and the dragons off the sheep, mostly, and nobody has to think too hard about whether that’s a fair arrangement or the other kind.

/ˈkɔːldə/ Fenn had never thought about it at all. He had paid the dues every year since his father died, the same as his father had. It was cheaper than thinking. [long pause]

Wren was at the table with her porridge, eating it the way she did everything, one careful edge at a time.

“Will they burn him all week?”

“Till the new moon,” said Ada. “Then they scatter him on /ˈkəʊldtɑːn/.”

“Why the new moon?”

“Because it isn’t the full one.”

Wren thought about this and decided to let it go, which was new. She was nine. Last year she’d have followed it all the way down. She had her mother’s habit of going quiet when she was storing something, and /ˈkɔːldə/ had stopped trying to guess what she did with it all.

“Davy Garrow says there’ll be a war,” she said.

“Davy Garrow is ten.”
