[calm] The chain was in his coat pocket. He’d thought about leaving the coat at home, and then put it on anyway, and now it sat against his hip like a stone in a shoe. Every time he bent to the spade it swung and knocked him. He knew exactly where it was all the time. It was like having a fourth person standing in the conversation. [long pause]

Joss was the one he was worried about.

Joss had been out in the far paddock since sunrise with the only beast the raiders hadn’t got: a young dray-mare called Sorrel, three years old, the colour of wet sand. She’d been loose in the paddock under the alders all night with nobody to see her, and she’d heard everything, and smelled everything, and now she stood in the far corner with her head up and her whole hide shivering in long ripples, and if anyone came within ten paces she went up on her hind legs and screamed.

Dray-beasts were not horses. People who didn’t know them called them horses because they had four legs and you could put a collar on them, just as people who didn’t know dragons called them big lizards. A grown dray-beast stood eight feet at the shoulder and more, taller than a man could reach, three times the weight of the biggest horse, and could pull a loaded wain out of a ditch that would drown a team of oxen. It had a long, soft, heavy face like a sad old man’s, and small round ears, and a way of looking at you that made you feel it had been thinking about something for a long time and was about to tell you. Some of the old farmers swore their beasts understood them without any bond at all. /ˈkɔːldə/ had never believed it. He’d also never said it out loud, in case one of them heard.
