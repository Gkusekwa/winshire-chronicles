Joss said, from the gate, in a voice /ˈkɔːldə/ had never heard him use: “My greys. Two of them. Mabbin and Old Grey. If you find who has them.”

“Then you’ll have them back, and the men who took them will have a short conversation with my uncle, and a shorter one with me.” /ˈteɪəʊ/ looked at him. “Your word I’ll have them looked for. On every road out of the Marches.”

It was a good answer. It was a chief’s son’s answer. /ˈkɔːldə/ sat on the grass under the mare and thought that it was the best answer anyone could have given, and that it changed nothing, and that the chain in his coat pocket was knocking against his hip again because he’d shifted his weight to see /ˈteɪəʊ/’s face. [long pause]

They stayed an hour. The riders walked the ashes and the yard, and two of them went off down the slope a little way, toward the river, and came back and spoke to /ˈteɪəʊ/ in low voices. /ˈkɔːldə/ watched them go and come back. He knew what they’d been looking at. /ˈteɪəʊ/ listened and nodded and his long face got tighter.

Then something went over the sun.

Not seven this time. One. Small, as dragons went, and quick: a lean dark-green shape that came down the line of the hills with the light flashing off it, and turned over Upper Rush in a long lazy curve, and came round again lower, so low that the wind of it lifted the ash off the burned barns in a grey sheet and everybody in the yard ducked and swore.

Sorrel went up. /ˈkɔːldə/ was on his feet before he’d thought about it, with his hand on her neck and his voice going, low and steady, nonsense about bees and swallows, and she came down again, shuddering, and stood. Over at the barn wall, Pip and Nan had both burst into tears.

[in awe] Wren hadn’t. Wren was standing in the middle of the yard with a cup of water in each hand and her head tipped right back, watching the dragon go round, and her face was the face she’d had at four years old the first time she saw snow. [pause]
