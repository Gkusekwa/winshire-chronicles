He went. The bay danced a little when he swung up, and he gathered it without thinking, and lifted a hand to the yard, and the riders went up the lane in a long jingling line and over the top of the hill and away north. [long pause]

They worked till the light went. By the end, two of the barns were clean, black squares on the earth, and the third had a tarp over its gable end and Sorrel standing under it, eating from Joss’s hand as if nothing had ever happened. /bɛl/ walked /ˈkɔːldə/ and Wren to the top of the lane.

“/ˈteɪəʊ/ /vɑː/,” she said. “Shaking your hand. Asking you to come to the hold. You.”

“He’s like that with everyone.”

“He’s not, you know.” /bɛl/ looked at him sideways. “I saw your face when he did it. You had the nail face. Worse.”

“I’m tired.”

“You’re always tired. That’s not it.” She waited. He didn’t say anything. She sighed, the long, put-upon sigh she’d been sighing at him since she was six years old and he was nine and had broken her doll and blamed the dog. “Go home, Cal. Thank you for coming. Thank you for running.” She hugged Wren. “And you, my love. You were brave with the dragon.”

[tenderly] “I wasn’t brave,” said Wren. “I liked her.”

[calm] They walked home along the river lane in the dusk, the two leagues he’d run the night before. Wren held his hand and talked the whole way about the dragon: its colour, which she said was the colour of the beck in spring; the woman on its back, who had been laughing; its eye, which had looked at her. Only her. She was sure.

He let her talk. The chain knocked against his hip with every step.

Halfway home she went quiet, and then she said, “Da. Why didn’t you give it to him?”

He nearly stopped walking. He didn’t.

“Give what?”

[quietly] “The thing in your pocket. You keep touching it.” She looked up at him. She had Senna’s look, the long, level one, and she was using it. “You were going to give it to him. Your hand went to your pocket. And then it didn’t.”

[calm] He walked a while.

[thoughtful] “Because some things,” he said at last, “once you’ve given them to somebody, you can’t take them back. And you don’t know what they’ll do with them. And then it’s your fault, whatever they do.”

[calm] Wren thought about that. The river went by on their left, wide and dark and smooth. Across the water the lanterns were coming out on Greyhold’s wall, one after another, small and steady.
