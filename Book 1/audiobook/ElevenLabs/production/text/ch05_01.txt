Chapter Five.

Roads and Roadblocks. [long pause]

The River Road is the oldest road in /ˈhɑːθmɪə/ that anyone still uses, and the knights will tell you it was built for them. It wasn’t. It was built for the river: a towpath first, a drovers’ track after that, and only last of all a patrol road, when the orders took the Marches and needed a way to ride the whole bank in a day. It runs from Millbrook in the south to Northwatch in the north, a little back from the water so the floods don’t take it, with a tower every few leagues and a wayside shrine in between, and the knights have ridden it every autumn for three hundred years. The farmers along it still call it the towpath. The knights have stopped correcting them.

First patrol was supposed to be a ceremony. /ˈmɛərən/ had seen it from the river watch, years ago: the year’s students riding out of Greyhold in two lines with the first lance at the front and the banner going, and everyone in Millbrook coming out to watch them go by. It was the first time a student rode the road as a knight, more or less. It meant you were nearly something.

This year it was eight students and one instructor and nobody watching, because two nights ago the far bank had burned, and yesterday every knight in Greyhold who could sit a horse had ridden north on the same road in a column, and come back at midnight with their faces shut.

“Not a ceremony,” Pell said in the yard at dawn, while they were mounting. “A patrol. You’ll ride to Northwatch and back. You’ll look at things. You’ll tell me what you saw. You will not draw a blade unless I draw first, and if I draw first you will still think about it. Haverly, you’re lance. Ashdown, rear. Questions?”

Nobody had questions. Pell didn’t encourage them before breakfast.

/ˈmɛərən/ had been given Turnip.

[dryly] Turnip was what the stables had left. He was an elderly piebald gelding with a hanging lip and one white eye and a habit, the grooms said, of stopping dead in the road to consider his life choices. First lance got the good horse; the late-oath candidates got Turnip, and had for years. Someone had tied a turnip to his bridle as a joke, years ago, and nobody had ever taken it off, so it had dried into a small brown fist that knocked against his cheek when he walked. /ˈmɛərən/ checked his feet, and his girth, and his teeth, which were older than Tobin, and got up.
