She watered Turnip at the trough. It took a long time, because Turnip drank like a man with nothing better to do. When she rode back up the track to the road, the patrol was waiting, and Pell looked at her face and didn’t ask, and she didn’t tell him. She put it away in the place where she put things, and counted it. [long pause]

North of Redwatch the road ran through farmland, wheat and barley on the inland side and water meadows on the river side, with the Brackenwood coming down dark and close on the left like a wave that had stopped. They passed the end of the lane up to Ferriby, which was a dozen houses and a smithy round a green a little way inland, and the children came running down the lane to watch, because whatever else the orders had stopped being, they were still the best thing a village child could see on an ordinary morning. The smith’s boy ran beside Haverly’s horse for a hundred yards shouting questions at the falcon. Kesh ignored him magnificently.

“Why don’t they cross?” a girl called after /ˈmɛərən/ from a gate, when the rest of the line had gone by. She couldn’t have been more than eight. “My nan says the knights saw Upper Rush burn and didn’t cross. Why not?”

[calm] /ˈmɛərən/ reined Turnip in. He stopped gladly. The girl looked up at her with the frank, fearless contempt of a child who has heard her nan say something.

“Because it’s not our side of the river,” /ˈmɛərən/ said.

“So?”

“So if we crossed every time something happened over there, we’d be at war with /ˈsɔːltriːtʃ/ in a week.”

[dryly] The girl thought about this. “My nan says if it was our barns, they’d cross quick enough.”

[calm] /ˈmɛərən/ had no answer to that. Turnip took the silence as permission and walked on. [long pause]

They found the roadblock a league short of Northwatch, at the wayside shrine.
