Chapter Six.

The Uncle and the Sons. [long pause]

When a Clan chief dies, the hold eats for a month: thirty days of open tables in the long hall at /ˈvɑːhəʊld/, paid for by the farmers of the Marches, who send their grain up the Drover’s Way when the hold asks for it. The hold always asks.

Haldor /vɑː/ had been a long time dying and a short time dead, and the hold had asked within the week. [long pause]

/ˈkɔːldə/ put the chain under a stone in the byre before he left.

It was a loose flag in the corner behind Bramble’s stall, where his father had kept the rent money when there had been rent money to keep. He lifted it with the edge of the spade, and wrapped the chain in an old sack, and laid it in the dry earth underneath, and put the flag back, and kicked straw over it. Then he stood there in the half-dark with the cow chewing at him.

[dryly] If it wasn’t in his pocket, he couldn’t take it out. If he couldn’t take it out, he couldn’t give it to anyone. It was the kind of reasoning that wouldn’t have survived five minutes of Wren, so he made sure Wren was asleep when he did it.

[calm] “It’s two days,” Ada said in the doorway, in the dark before dawn. “Two days up and two days back. You’ve never been gone four days in your life.”

“I went to Cotter’s Cross for the wedding.”

“That was three, and you came back on the second because you didn’t like the beds.” His mother pulled her shawl round her. “Why you? Hob’s got a cart. Garrow’s got two sons doing nothing.”

“Hob’s cart’s got a cracked axle. And Garrow’s sons would drink the levy before they got it to the Ram’s Head.” He tightened the last rope. “Somebody has to.”

“Somebody always has to,” said Ada. “It’s never been you before.”

He didn’t have an answer to that, so he didn’t give one. He kissed her, and went up the ladder and looked at Wren asleep in the loft with her fists by her face, and came down, and went out to the lane where Sorrel was standing in the traces of Joss’s big wain, with Joss holding her head and eleven sacks of barley roped down behind her.

“She’s been good all night,” Joss said. “She knew you were coming. I swear she did.”

“She knew you’d been feeding her apples.”

“That too.” Joss rubbed the mare’s long face. He’d stopped crying in the days since the fire. He’d started doing something worse, which was not talking about it at all. “Take care of her, Cal. She’s all I’ve got.”
