[calm] The Drover’s Way left the river at Harrow’s End and climbed. It went up Hollow Beck between steep fields and then out onto the open moor, where the farms gave out and there was nothing but heather and sheep and the long brown backs of the /ˈskɑːfɛl/ going up into cloud. It was a sheep road, a cattle road, a road for drovers and their dogs, and it had never been built so much as worn: a pale scar in the heather wide enough for a cart if you didn’t mind your wheels in the ditch every hundred yards.

[quietly] Sorrel didn’t mind. Sorrel, once she was in a collar and had somewhere to go, turned out to be the steadiest thing /ˈkɔːldə/ had ever walked beside. She leaned into the load as if she’d been waiting all her life to be asked, and she went up the long slopes of the moor at the same pace she went down them, and when the wind came off the tops in the afternoon, cold, with rain in it, she put her head down and walked through it and never once looked back for the barn that wasn’t there anymore.

[calm] He talked to her the whole way. It seemed to help them both. [long pause]

The Ram’s Head stood on its own at the halfway point, a long low stone inn with a turf roof and a yard for carts and a stone trough the size of a boat. It had been there as long as the road had. Every drover in the Marches had slept there, and most of them had fought there, and the landlord was a huge silent man called Brack who settled the fights by standing up.

It was full that night. The whole Marches was going up to /ˈvɑːhəʊld/ to eat Haldor’s bread, or sending grain to pay for it, and the yard was packed with carts and the taproom with men. /ˈkɔːldə/ put Sorrel in the last stall and rubbed her down and gave her the oats Joss had sent, and stood with her a while until she’d stopped flicking her ears at the noise, and then went in and found a corner and a bowl of mutton stew and kept his head down.

It wasn’t hard. Nobody in that room was looking for a quiet farmer from Harrow’s End.
