---

The river came back slowly.

All through the sunrise it poured into the hole where the island had been, from both channels, brown and loud, and then less loud, and then only the sound of a lot of water going round in a slow eddy a quarter-mile across, carrying reeds and spars and the white bellies of fish. By the time the sun was a hand's width over the Saltreach hills the eddy had begun to settle. A tree went round in it, a whole oak off the island with its roots in the air, round and round, as if it were looking for the place it had stood.

Maren sat in the mud against Orrun's shoulder and watched the tree.

She was cold. She noticed it as she'd notice a stranger at a gate: there, and not yet her business. Her clothes were wet to the waist from the passage under the rock. Her hands were burned and split, and one of them was holding a strip of rag at her throat with something long and iron hung on it, and she didn't remember closing her fingers round it. Hale's key. She let go of it.

Under her the lion breathed, slow. Every breath lifted her and put her down.

There was blood in his mane, some of it his. She hadn't asked him where it came from. She found she didn't want to know yet, and that he knew she didn't, and that it was all right. That was new. It wasn't a night old.

---

The farmer was talking.

He sat against the green dragon a few paces off, with his knees up and his arms on them, and he talked, low, steadily, as a man talks to frightened stock. She heard only his half.

"No. Lie still. You can't, you know you can't, you'll open it again." A pause. "I know. I know it does." A longer one. "Because I'm not having you walk on it till somebody's looked. That's why." He put a hand back without looking and laid it flat on the green dragon's neck, and the green dragon stopped shaking for as long as the hand was there. "No, he's not. Kharrak. Kharrak, leave her be, she's not going anywhere. Neither are you." He listened. Something in his face moved. "Well, I don't know that. Nobody knows that."

Behind him the old bronze dragon lay in the reeds with her torn wing out across the mud like a sail somebody had dropped. Her one gold eye was open. The red one stood over her with his broken horn and his legs braced, as if the ground might go out from under her too and he meant to be there to catch it.

"I said I don't know." The farmer rubbed his face with both hands. "Ask her, then. She's sat there long enough."

The green dragon turned her head and looked at Maren.

Maren looked back. She had never been looked at by a dragon before. It was like being looked at by a horse, and not like it at all.

"She wants to know what your lion's called," the farmer said. He didn't seem to find it strange to be saying it. "She says he told her, down there, and she's forgotten, and she says she never forgets anything, so it must have been a lie."

"Orrun," said Maren.

The farmer said it to the dragon. The green dragon put her head down on her forefeet, satisfied.

"She says that's what she said."

Maren nearly laughed. It came up her chest and stopped somewhere under her collarbone, and she let it stay there.

"You're Calder Fenn," she said.

He looked at her properly then. A big man, sitting in the mud. Not purple in the face. She'd been wrong about that, years ago, at the Staithe, imagining him.

"Liss Ashdown's girl," he said. "The lion-woman. Lisska's been on about you since the autumn." He tipped his head at the green dragon. "My Wren asks after you. She's never met you, and she asks."

"She met me at Greyhold's gate," said Maren. "In the crush. She said, *you're her.*"

He was quiet a moment, hearing that.

"She'll want to know you came out." He looked downriver, toward where Greyhold was, out of sight behind the bend. "She'll want it said twice."

---

On both banks the world was beginning to move again.

It started on the Saltreach side. All night there had been an army on the hill above Harrow's End, thousands, with the ring of six on its banners and its boats drawn up along the Eelweir. When the Titan's shadow went over they had gone flat in the mud, all of them, like barley under hail. Now, a man at a time, they were getting up.

Nobody was telling them to. She watched for it, out of eight winters' habit on the river: the horn, the runner, the banner dipping to call the companies in. It didn't come. The men stood up and looked at the hole in the river, and at each other, and at the empty sky in the north-east, and then a pair of them turned and walked away north along the Drover's Way, not fast, like men walking home from a fair. Then a dozen. Then a whole company, with its banner rolled on its pole and carried like a hoe. A boat at the Eelweir slipped its rope and went round once in the eddy and out into the current, and nobody on the bank went after it.

"They're going," said the farmer.

"There's nobody to tell them not to."

He was quiet a moment. "There was one at the tower," he said. "On the Ruin. With the key."

"I know."

"He went up a stair. There was an older stair, at the back. He knew it." The farmer looked at the eddy. "He didn't look back."

"I know," said Maren. "I heard."

She didn't say what else she knew: that there had been another one with a key, under the school, kneeling on the boss of the knight's lock, smiling at her with real gladness as if she were someone he'd been waiting for. She didn't say what she'd done. It would come out of her sometime, to somebody. Not in the reeds, at sunrise, to a man she'd met underground in the night.

Orrun turned his head and touched her shoulder with his nose, once, very lightly. She put her hand back on his mane.

---

The Hearthmere bank moved next.

Redwatch stood half a league back from the water on its rise, a square tower of red sandstone where she'd stood her first watch at seventeen. There was a crack down the face of it now, from the parapet to the second window, wide enough to put an arm in. In the night the lamps on it had burned white, too white, the way every old lamp on the river had burned since the surge began. Now they hung there dark. She could see them from the reeds: the iron cages on their brackets, empty of light, like birds' nests in winter.

Below the tower the knights' line had held the bank all night against the Restoration's ladders. In the dawn it had been a long smudge of men and beasts along the water's edge, standing quite still, watching the Titan go.

The smudge was gathering itself together.

She watched it form. She knew the shapes. Greyharts, white and huge, taller than a man at the shoulder, the crown's war-mounts that the orders rode. Horses among them, fewer than there should have been. Hounds at heel. Two falcons going up off fists into the cold air over the line and turning there, high, watching. A column made itself out of the line and came down off the rise toward the reeds at a walk, and behind it, slow, came a wain with two dray-oxen in the traces, and on the wain something long and grey and coiled that she knew too, because she had watched it quenched.

"What's that?" said the farmer.

"Chain."

He looked at it.

"For him," said Maren. "It was made for him."

The farmer said nothing. Behind him the red dragon lifted his head, and his lip came back off teeth as long as her hand.

---

At the head of the column rode a small woman on a grey horse.

Not a greyhart. Rowan had never ridden a greyhart that Maren had seen; she said they were for people who wanted to be looked at. She rode neat and upright and without waste, with her iron-grey head bare and a dispatch case on her saddle. Thirty-eight behind her. Maren counted them without meaning to. Thirty-eight knights and students who could still sit a beast, out of all the orders that had held this bank since the muster. Wynn among them, on foot, leading his horse, with his two hounds pressed against his legs. The two licensed surgeons on their mules at the back. The wain.

A long bowshot from the reeds, the greyharts stopped.

They didn't balk. They stopped, all of them at once, as if they'd come to the edge of a cliff only they could see. They stood with their great heads up and their nostrils wide, shivering in long ripples from shoulder to haunch, and when their riders put heels to them they didn't move. One of them went back on its haunches and had to be held. Up and down the column the hounds lay down in the wet grass with their bellies flat and their eyes on the reeds, and stayed down.

They smelled the lion. Three days ago, from a hedge below Hollin Knowe, she'd watched a column's greyharts catch his scent on a west wind and stop like this, and she had turned back. She remembered that, and didn't turn back.

The knights got down. They did it in order, by the files, and walked the last of the way leading their beasts, and the beasts came, because their knights asked them. A hound got up when its knight looked at it. A falcon came down out of the sky to the fist that rose for it. All along the line, on ground where not one lamp in a league would light, the bonds held. She saw it and understood it at the same moment, the way she'd understood things through Orrun all night: a bond wasn't the thing that had gone out of the ground. A bond had never come from the ground at all.

It was the only thing in Hearthmere still working.

The column stopped forty paces from the reeds and made a line.

Nobody moved.

---

She didn't remember standing up. She was standing, between the lion and the line, with the river behind her and the mud to her ankles and the river watch sword still in her hand. Nobody had cleaned it since the knight's lock. Orrun had stood up behind her. She felt him. He was the size of a dray-beast and then some, and every knight in the line was looking at him, and he was looking at the wain.

Behind her the farmer had stood up too. So had the red dragon, over the old one. The green dragon was behind the farmer's legs, which was not a place a dragon fitted.

Rowan didn't open the dispatch case.

She didn't need to. Maren knew what was in it. She had stood in the room at the top of the north tower and heard it read, in Rowan's quiet voice, in the same tone Rowan used for the stable rota: *the beast on the island to be secured by the orders, and the farmer with the dragons, if he crosses, to be taken, or, failing that, killed.*

He had crossed. He was standing on the Hearthmere bank, in Hearthmere mud.

Rowan looked at the lion for a long time. She looked at the dragons, the old one lying down, the red one standing, the green one where she was. She looked at the farmer. Then she looked at Maren, and Maren couldn't read anything in her face at all, and thought perhaps there was nothing there to read, that Rowan had spent it all in the night at the gate and on the bank and had none left.

"Ashdown," said Rowan.

"Captain."

The sun climbed. A falcon called, high up. Out in the eddy the oak went round.

Nobody moved. It went on. She could feel the line deciding. It was like standing in the river watch boat with a smuggler's skiff drifting closer in the fog, both crews knowing the first man to reach for a weapon would decide it for everyone, and every man holding his hands very still and very visible on the gunwale. Somebody's greyhart stamped. Somebody's hound whined, very low, and was hushed. The sun was warm on the back of her neck. Her arm, holding the sword, began to shake, and she let it, and didn't put the sword down.

Behind her the farmer said, quietly, to someone she couldn't hear, "No. Not unless they do."

---

Ottery came down from the boathouse.

She heard him before she saw him: the slap of his boots in the wet path and his breath going like a broken bellows, because Ottery hadn't hurried anywhere in thirty years and his body had forgotten how. He came round the end of the knights' line at the water's edge, short and wide and grey, with his old river-watch coat open over his shirt and his pipe in his fist. He didn't look at the knights. He didn't look at the lion. He walked out into the forty paces of open grass between the line and the reeds, where there was a punt lying upside down that the night's flood had thrown up on the bank, and he looked at the punt as if it had been put there for him, and he sat down on it.

He got his pipe going. It took him some time. The tinder was wet.

Thirty-eight knights watched a river-watch sergeant light his pipe.

"Captain," said Ottery, when it was going. He didn't get up. He never got up for anyone below a captain, and he had never, in all the years Maren had known him, got up for a captain either, but he had always at least shifted on his barrel. This morning he didn't shift.

"Sergeant," said Rowan.

"Forty years on this water," said Ottery, to the river. "Never seen the island go. I'd like to see the rest of the morning, if it's all the same to everyone." He took the pipe out of his mouth and looked at it. "Bit tired of seeing people die on my landing. That's all. Bit tired of it."

Nobody answered him.

Rowan lifted her head and looked up the rise, at Redwatch, at the crack down its red face. At the lamps.

Maren followed her look. The iron cages on their brackets, dark. She thought of the yard at Greyhold on the day the chain was quenched: the smith-reader with his licence round his neck, bent over the trough as each great link went hissing in, saying the words into the steam. A licensed word in every link. Paid for by the crown, like the lamps. Like the lamps, drawn from below.

Rowan was looking at the lamps, and she was thinking about the chain. Maren saw her think it. She saw the moment Rowan understood that what was on the wain behind her was forty fathoms of ordinary iron.

"Not today," said Rowan.

She said it to no one. Then she turned in the saddle and said it to the line, in the stable-rota voice. "Not today. Wynn, the wounded to the tower. Corbet, take the wain back up. The rest of you hold the bank till the companies are off the far side." She looked back at Maren once. "Ashdown. Come to me when you can stand."

"Captain."

Rowan turned her horse and rode back up the rise. She didn't look at the lion again. She didn't look at the farmer at all, and Maren thought that was deliberate, and that it was the most Rowan could do: to have looked away at the moment he walked past her.

Out on his punt, Ottery smoked.

---

They brought the wounded down off the bank to a place in the lee of the boathouse, where the ground was dry.

Maren went over. Nobody stopped her. The line had broken up into knights doing things, and the greyharts were being walked back up the rise with their heads still turned over their shoulders toward the reeds, and nobody had time to remember that she'd been confined to the midden wall. She went because there was a boy in the row whose face she knew and couldn't name, and because she couldn't sit in the mud any longer, and because Orrun had lain down again and put his head on his paws and closed his eyes, which meant he would let her.

There were eleven in the row. Students and knights and grooms. The Restoration's ladders had come up the bank all night, and the line had held it, and this was what it cost.

The two licensed surgeons were working down the row on their knees.

She knew the old one, who was at Greyhold behind the barricade, or had been. These were the young ones: Hallard, who had a beard, and Aske, who didn't, and who couldn't have been more than twenty-five. She had watched Aske in the infirmary in the autumn set a groom's wrist with three words and his hands, the bone grating once and then lying straight, and the groom laughing with relief before the pain had even gone. Licensed words. Knitting and stanching. Every surgeon who trained at Hallowmere took the licence and the words with it, and the words had worked for three hundred years, until this winter.

Aske was on his knees by a man in a groom's jerkin with his thigh laid open. It was a spear-cut, long and deep, and somebody had tied it off above the knee in the night, and the binding had slipped, and the grass under him was dark.

She knew him when she got close. Daw Pruett. He had held the greyharts' heads in the Long Barn for as long as she'd been at Greyhold; he'd ridden with the column to hold them again, because greyharts went mad in battle and someone had to. He was looking at the sky. His lips were grey.

Aske had both hands flat on the wound. He was saying the words.

She'd heard them before, over the groom's wrist, over a student's split scalp: the knitting-word, low and quick, then the stanching-word after it, and you felt them in the air like the moment before a storm breaks, a pressure, a pull. She felt nothing. She heard the words, the shape of them, the same as in the infirmary. They went into the air and stopped there. As if he were saying them into a sack.

He said them again. Louder.

The blood came up round his fingers.

"Come on," said Aske. "Come on." He said the words a third time, so hard she saw the spit fly. Nothing came. No pressure, no pull. Just a young man on his knees in the grass shouting old words at a leg.

Daw Pruett stopped looking at the sky. His eyes stayed open, but whatever was looking out of them had stopped.

Aske knelt there. He didn't take his hands away. Then he did, and looked at them, red to the wrist, and sat back on his heels, and swore, low and steady, all the words a surgeon learns that aren't licensed.

"He should have lived," Aske said. To nobody. To his hands. "That's nothing. That's a thigh. I've closed worse than that on a kitchen table with a cook holding the lamp." He wiped his hands on the grass, hard, again and again, as if the grass could take something off them. "It's not me. It's not the words. It's the ground." He laughed, which was worse than the swearing. "It's dead ground. The whole bank. I can feel it. It's like pressing on a drum with the skin gone." He looked up and saw Maren standing over him and didn't seem to know her. "It's dead ground," he said again. "All of it. Every league of it."

*Dead ground.*

She'd never heard it said. She found she knew exactly what it meant.

She'd felt it go. On the shore at sunrise, with her hand on Orrun's shoulder, she'd felt the thing that had been under Hearthmere all her life, warm and humming, the thing under the island and under the archive and behind the black door, go draining away north-east after a speck in the sky, as a flood goes back off the fields and leaves only mud. She'd felt it through Orrun. And now she stood on the bank where she'd stood her first watch, and put her weight on her feet, and listened with whatever it was in her that had learned in the night to listen through him.

Nothing. Not quiet: quiet had something in it. This had nothing. Under her boots the ground lay there like a cart with the horse gone out of the traces.

Back in the reeds, without opening his eyes, Orrun spoke.

*It took it with it.*

"All of it?"

*What was ever its.* A long pause, like a stone settling. *Which was all of it.*

Aske was staring at her. She realised she'd spoken aloud to nobody, the way the farmer did, and that from outside it must look the same.

"Ashdown," Aske said slowly. "You're Ashdown. With the beast."

"Yes."

He looked at Daw Pruett. He looked at his own hands. "Can it do anything?" he said. "Your beast. Can it do anything here? Can anything?"

She thought of the rock opening ahead of Orrun in the dark, the long passage rising, the slab held on the back of a second lion made out of the hill. She thought of the bond, which was holding, which had never come from the ground. She didn't know how to say any of that to a man with a dead groom at his knees.

"Not for him," she said.

Aske nodded, as if he'd expected that, and closed Daw Pruett's eyes with two red fingers, and went on to the next man in the row.

---

By noon the Saltreach bank was nearly empty.

The army that had stood on the hill above Harrow's End all night was gone, north up the Drover's Way and south toward Cotter's Cross in long untidy strings, with their banners furled or dropped, nobody leading them. A few hundred still sat on the hill in the sun as if they'd forgotten how to leave. Smoke went up from Harrow's End, thin and grey: not burning now, only cooking fires, or the ends of the night's. The eddy where the island had been had gone smooth and brown, and the oak had gone out of it at last and down the river toward Greyhold, and the gulls had found the fish.

The farmer was standing at the water's edge when she came back.

He'd got the old dragon up. She stood on three legs with the torn wing folded as far as it would go, which wasn't far, and the red one's shoulder under her chest on the wounded side, holding her up like a man holding a drunk friend up outside a tavern, all his weight leant in. The green one walked round and round them both, anxious, getting in the way.

"She can't fly," the farmer said. He said it to Maren, this time. "She won't be able to fly. Not for a long while. She's saying she can, and she can't."

"Where will you go?"

He looked across the river at Harrow's End and its thin smoke. Then downriver.

"Wren's at the ferry," he said. "Your mother's. Ada. Bel and the little ones. They're on this side." He looked at the old dragon, standing on three legs. "I can't get her across that. Not till somebody's looked at the wing." He looked back at Maren. "So we walk down your road, I suppose. If they'll let us."

They both looked up the rise, at the line of knights holding the bank, and at Rowan's small upright figure by the tower's cracked foot.

"She let you stand there," Maren said. "She'll let you walk past."

"Will she."

"She'll look the other way while you do it. It's the most she can do."

He thought about that. Then he nodded, the same nod he'd given her in the sunrise.

"Down your road, then," he said. To the dragons. "No, slowly. We're going slowly. There's no hurry now." He listened. "Because it's gone, that's why. There's nothing left to hurry for." He put his hand on the old dragon's neck. "Lean on him. Lean. He doesn't mind. He'll tell you he minds, and he doesn't."

They went up off the shore onto the River Road, the farmer at the bronze dragon's head and the red one under her shoulder and the green one going ahead and coming back and going ahead. Maren walked behind them with her hand on Orrun's shoulder. The line on the bank watched them come. She saw hands go to hilts and stay there, and greyharts throw their heads up and be held. She saw Wynn's hounds lie down again as the lion passed, and Wynn put his hand on Ash's head and keep it there.

At the foot of the tower Rowan stood with her back to the road, talking to Corbet about the wain, and didn't turn round.

The noon sun stood over the river. Down at Greyhold, she knew, the bell would be wanting to ring. She listened for it as they walked, out of two years' habit, as she'd listen for her own name in a crowd.

Under her boots the ground said nothing at all.
