---

The bronze dragon walked on three legs, and every fourth step she stopped.

Calder learned the rhythm of it in the first quarter-league out of the reeds. Three steps, and her weight going forward onto the good foreleg and the red one's shoulder taking the rest, and then a stop, with her head down and her one gold eye half shut and her breath going in and out like wind in a split door. Then three more. The torn wing she held out from her side as far as it would go, which wasn't far, because folding it hurt and letting it hang hurt more. It dragged in the ruts of the River Road and left a long smear in the dust behind her, as if someone had walked the road with a broom.

*I can fly,* said Aurveth. *If I chose to. It is a question of choosing.*

"You can't."

*I have flown with worse.*

"You haven't. Lisska asked. You said you'd never been hurt like this in three hundred years."

*Lisska repeats things.*

*I remember everything,* said Lisska, from somewhere ahead of them on the road. She'd gone on and come back four times already. *She did say it. On the ice. I remember.*

*Lean,* said Kharrak, to the old one, in the voice he used for her and nobody else, low and rough. *Lean. More.*

She leaned. Kharrak's legs bowed under it and straightened. He didn't complain. He'd walked her up out of the rock like that, the whole long passage in the dark, and he'd walk her to the end of the world like it if somebody asked him, and probably if nobody did.

Calder walked at her head with his hand on her jaw.

---

At noon the woman with the lion started looking downriver.

She'd walked behind them since the reeds without saying much, her hand on the lion's shoulder, the river watch sword through her belt now instead of in her fist. Every so often the lion turned his great grey head and looked at the dragons and the dragons looked back, and something went between them that Calder only half caught, as you half catch two neighbours talking over a wall about a thing you weren't there for. Kin, Lisska called it. *Far kin. Very far. He talks slow.*

Now the woman kept turning her head toward Greyhold, still hidden by the long bend below the road. Again. Then again.

"What is it?"

"The bell," she said. "It's noon."

He listened. The river. A curlew somewhere out over the water meadows. Kharrak's breathing.

"It rings at noon," she said. "Every day. Three hundred years. They say the oath when it rings, in the yard, facing the inner wall." She was still listening. "It rang the day the gate fell. It rang and nobody said anything. That was the first time anyone didn't."

They walked. The sun stood on top of the sky.

Nothing rang.

She stopped listening after a while. He saw her stop: her shoulders came down a little, as a horse's do when you take the harness off. She didn't say anything else about it, and he didn't ask.

---

There were people on the road.

Not many. The River Road ran along the Hearthmere bank from Redwatch down to Greyhold and past it to Millbrook, and in the afternoon the people of Millbrook began to come up it, the ones who'd run in the night, coming back to see what was left. A man with a handcart and nothing in the handcart. Two women with a goat. A boy on his own, walking fast, looking for someone. They saw what was coming down the road toward them and got off it into the ditch, and stood there, and watched it go by: three dragons and a lion and two people, one of them walking at a dragon's head talking to it as if it were a cow with a bad foot.

Nobody ran. Calder thought that was the strangest thing of a strange day. Nobody even ran.

A woman in a knight's grey stood by the side of the road at the place where the track came down from the water meadows. She was old enough to be someone's grandmother. There was a hound lying across the road at her feet, a big rough-coated grey hound with its head on its paws, and it didn't get up when the dragons came, and it didn't get up when the lion came, and she didn't make it.

"He won't come off the road," she said, to nobody. "His knight went down it last night. He won't come off it."

The woman with the lion stopped. She put her hand on the old woman's shoulder for a moment. Then she walked round the hound, and the lion walked round it, wide, and so did the dragons. The hound watched them go by and didn't lift its head.

*It's waiting,* said Lisska, very small.

"I know."

*It doesn't know he's not coming.*

"It knows," said Calder. "It's waiting anyway."

---

*They're letting us go,* said Kharrak, an hour on. *All of them.*

"Yes."

*The knights. With the chain. They let us go.*

"Yes."

*Why?*

Calder looked back up the road toward Redwatch, out of sight now behind them. He thought about the small grey-haired woman on the grey horse with her back to the road, talking to someone about a cart.

"Because they're tired," he said. "Because the lamps went out, and the chain was full of the same words the lamps were, and she knew it. And because nobody wants to be first." He thought about it. "And because the old sergeant sat down."

Kharrak didn't understand the last part. Calder didn't either, quite. But he'd seen it work, so he didn't argue with it.

---

The bend came round and there was Greyhold.

He'd seen it from the far side all his life. Everybody on the Saltreach bank had: the grey hill and the grey walls on top of it, the river wall along the water, the towers. Lights on it at night like a string of stars set down on the ground. Never a fire. Never a lamp guttering. Clean white lights, every one, all night, all year, so that you could tell the hour on the Saltreach bank by when they came on.

There were no lights on it now. It wasn't dark yet, and he could see that the walls were dark anyway, like a house at noon when everyone in it has died. There was a crack in the inner wall that he could see from a league off, top to bottom, like a split in a log. Smoke went up from inside, thin. Somebody was burning something.

Under the river wall, at the foot of a stone jetty that ran out into the Wend, there was a long low house with a turf roof. A net-loft. A smoke-house. A flat-bottomed ferry drawn up on a slip, and a thick rope running from a post on the jetty out over the water, across to the far bank, to a little huddle of huts and drying racks that he'd sold barley at every autumn of his life.

There was a small figure on the end of the jetty.

He knew it from a league off too.

---

She didn't run.

She stood at the end of the jetty with her arms at her sides and watched them come down off the River Road onto the landing: the old bronze dragon on three legs, the red one under her, the green one, the lion, the woman. She stood very still, the way she did when she was storing something. Then the green dragon broke from his side and went down the landing at a run, wings half up, and stopped dead at the foot of the jetty so hard she skidded, and put her head down, and Wren walked along the jetty and put both arms round her neck.

The blue ribbon on the green dragon's left horn was dirty, and wet, and still there.

Calder walked down the landing. He found he couldn't go faster than he was going.

Wren let go of Lisska and looked at him.

"You left the dragons," she said. "Fisk's boy said. He said you'd gone to the Ruin on your own and left the dragons."

"I did."

"He said you said they'd be seen."

"They would've been."

She looked at him. A long, level look. Senna's.

"Are we all here?" she said.

He thought about who was here and who wasn't. Joss, on the hill above Upper Rush. Teo, in the black water of Coldtarn. Sigrun and Dag and Imra. Hallam. A groom he'd never met, on the bank at Redwatch this morning, with a surgeon swearing over him.

"We're all here," he said.

"Say it again."

"We're all here, Wren."

She nodded, as if that settled it, and came along the jetty and put her face into his coat.

---

Liss Ashdown came out of the house with her sleeves rolled up and a cloth in her hands, and looked at her daughter.

Calder had only seen her across a ferry before, the long years of autumns selling barley at the Staithe: a weathered woman at the far end of a rope, poling, saying the price of the crossing and nothing else. Close to, she was smaller than he'd thought. Her hands were the size of a man's.

The woman with the lion stood at the foot of the landing with her hand on his shoulder and didn't come any closer.

Liss looked at her. Then at the lion, a long look, all the way up and all the way back down. Then at her daughter again.

"You'll want something hot, Maren," said Liss.

That was all. She went back in. Maren stood there a moment longer, and then she sat down on the bottom step of the jetty, where she was, and stayed there.

---

They came down from the Long Barn after dark.

He heard them before he saw them: the click of big hooves on the stone of the water-gate stair, and Nan crying in the tired, steady way of a child who has forgotten what she started crying about. Then lanterns: oil, and candle-stubs in tin, swinging. Bel came first with Nan on her hip and a lantern in her other hand, and Ada behind her with Pip by the wrist, and behind them the greys.

Mabbin, the old one, grey as a winter field. Sorrel, her daughter, younger and darker and jumpier. And Old Grey.

He came down the stair last, slowly, his great feet finding each step. Nobody was leading him. His rope hung from his headstall and trailed on the stone. His eyes had been empty since the Ewe Beck, like windows in a house nobody lived in. He came down the landing past Calder and past the dragons, who didn't trouble him, and past the lion, which should have, and didn't, and he walked along the foot of the jetty to where Wren was sitting on the edge with her legs over the water, and stopped behind her, and stood there.

That was all he did. He stood behind her in the dark.

Bel watched it from the foot of the stair. Her face did something in the lantern light and then stopped doing it.

"He does that," said Bel. "Since the gate. Wherever she is." She hitched Nan up on her hip. "Doesn't know his own name. Knows hers."

Calder went to take Nan from her. Bel turned her shoulder, not much, and he stopped.

"Joss would have liked to see the island go," she said. "He always said there was something wrong with it. Since we were children. Said nobody could tell him what." She looked at the river, at the dark place out on the water where the eddy was. "Now nobody has to."

She went past him into the house.

Ada stopped beside him. She didn't say anything for a while. She looked at the dragons lying on the shingle below the landing, the old one with her wing out, the red one curled round her, the green one on her own with her head toward the jetty where Wren was.

"Three," said Ada.

"Three."

"I sang about it for fifty years," said his mother. "I never once thought I'd have to feed them."

---

Liss's kitchen was long and low and smelled of smoke and eel and wet wool. There was an oil lamp on the table, an old one with a brass foot, and she lit it with a spill from the fire. He watched her do it. Every house on the Hearthmere bank had lamps that lit themselves; you saw them across the river every night. Liss had never stopped keeping one you lit with a spill.

"My mother's," said Liss, seeing him look. "She never trusted the other sort, and I never had the heart to throw it out." She set it down and wiped her hands. "Turns out she was right."

Ada went to the fire, where the kettle was. Out of a lifetime's habit she put two fingers on its side and said the hearth-word over it, low, the one that hurried a kettle. Calder had heard her say it every morning he could remember. Nothing happened. The kettle sat on the fire like any kettle. Ada looked at it, and at her fingers, and took them away.

"Well," she said. "It'll boil when it boils."

---

Later, when the children were asleep in the net-loft and Bel had gone up with them and not come down, he emptied his coat onto Liss's table.

He hadn't meant to. He'd come in from the shingle where the dragons were, and Maren was at the table, alone, with a bowl in front of her she hadn't touched, and the lion lying full length on the jetty outside the open door with his head toward it. Liss at the fire. Ada in the corner, mending something that didn't need mending. He'd sat down across from her, and his coat was heavy, and he'd started taking things out of it so he could take it off. Then he'd kept going.

A knife. Plain, short, a round pommel worn smooth by a thumb, and on the pommel, cut small, a ring of six dots.

An iron ring on a string, small enough to go over a thumb, stamped with the same ring of six. Kharrak had brought it to him off a dead man's neck in the gorse at the ford on Hollow Beck, the second time, the day Hallam died.

A length of beast-chain. Heavy. Cut. Off the island, off the lion's leg, in the summer.

And a strip of his own shirt, wrapped round something, which he unwrapped. Five links of a smaller chain, cut through at one end, rust-brown. He'd pulled them out of the ash at Upper Rush the first night of all of it, and put them in his pocket, and told no one for a hundred days.

He turned the island chain over and found the link he was looking for, and showed her. Inside the curve, where the iron would lie against the skin, two hammers, crossed.

He turned the five links over. Two hammers, crossed.

She looked at them a long time. She didn't touch them.

"Odo Marl," she said. "In Millbrook. My brother's master."

"I know."

"He threw his punches in the river. Both of them. The night they burned his forge."

"I didn't know that." He thought about a man throwing his own mark into the Wend. "Didn't help Joss."

She was quiet.

---

"I heard them," said Calder. "Under the arch."

She looked up.

He hadn't known he was going to say it until he was saying it. He'd had it in him three days now, since the night under the beam in the Ruin's tower with his face to the crack in the stones. He'd carried it up the stair and down the spit and into the dark and up out of it. It had been in him under the river when he met her, and he'd kept it there, because there wasn't room, and because he'd wanted a sky over them before he said it.

There was a sky over them now. He could see a corner of it through the door, past the lion's head, with stars in it.

"The two of them," he said. "Under the arch. Same face. One that stood still and one that laughed. They said each other's names."

"Corvin," said Maren. "Cassian."

He looked at her.

"I heard one of them," she said. "At the end."

So he told her.

He told it the way it had come to him, in order, lying on the beam. The still one saying the knights had come up to Redwatch with the chain, and Greyhold had a handful on its wall, and Millbrook was ready. The laughing one saying he'd rather have the farmer than burn him. The still one saying *we'll have the dragons either way. A man's a halter.* Rook coming up from the spit to report like a drover reporting a load. The second ford: the law that held the bonded long enough, and the old man who came down anyway, *nobody thought he would*, and the still one saying it had done what it was for, the Clan wouldn't stand together again this side of the summer.

Then the first ford.

He told her that part exactly. He'd found that he could say it exactly, word for word, as you can recite a thing you've been made to learn by someone who beat you. The laughing one asking, quietly, as if he'd never asked before: *the boy. The first one. Varr's son.* And Rook: *went in the water like a stone. He never drew. He thought I'd come to hold his horse.*

Ada had stopped mending.

He told her the mill. One of Delling's crossbowmen, hired before the rest of the company, paid to put the woman on the green dragon out of the fight if the other two came down. So all three would break in one night. So the dragons would be loose for anyone with a halter.

Out on the shingle, he felt Lisska go still.

He told her the end: tomorrow Millbrook, and the gate, and the night after the knight's lock, under the school, *at the hour, not before, not after.* And the laughing one saying *two hands*, and neither of them saying what the locks opened.

Then he stopped, because there wasn't any more, and he found that his hands were flat on Liss's table on either side of the knife, holding on.

---

For a while Maren said nothing.

The lamp burned. The kettle, which had boiled at last, sat on the hob. Out on the jetty the lion breathed, slow, and the planks under him creaked with it.

"I believe you," she said.

"I know you do."

"It doesn't help."

"I know that too."

She looked at the knife. "Rook."

"Dead. On the spit. Kharrak." He said it flatly, because there was no other way to say it. "He went for me with that, so Kharrak would do it. He wanted it. He said nobody believes the barley man, and they'd not believe him either." He turned the knife a little on the table, so the six dots faced her. "He said his book went into Delling's chest. Every bag of silver and what it was for. He said Delling sells anything." He looked at his hands. "And he said I can't read."

"Can you?"

"My name. Wren's." He thought. "*Two.* I can write *two.*"

She looked at him, and for a moment he thought she might laugh, the way she'd nearly laughed in the reeds in the morning. She didn't.

"So it's your word," she said. "That you heard two men under an arch. One of them dead and the other gone up a stair. And a dead man's word, out of your mouth, about a dead man's son."

"Yes."

"Nobody will believe you."

"Somebody might," said Calder. "Somebody might, if I say it enough. In the right yard." He didn't know if he believed that. He said it anyway. "Teo's brother. His uncle. They have to hear it. Both of them. Even if they don't believe it, they have to hear it, out loud, the whole thing. That's all I've got."

---

She was quiet so long that he thought she'd finished.

Then she said, not looking at him, looking at the five links in the strip of his shirt: "The one at the school is dead."

He waited.

"The one on the knight's lock," she said. "Under Greyhold. With his key in it." Her burned hands lay on the table in their rags, very still. "Cassian."

She didn't say who'd done it. She didn't need to. He'd seen her hands and her sleeves in the light of Kharrak's fire under the river, the blood to the elbow that wasn't hers, and he hadn't asked, and he didn't ask now.

He found that what he wanted to say was *good*, and that he couldn't say it, because of the way she was sitting.

"The other one turned his," he said instead. "I had my hand on his wrist. I couldn't stop it." He looked at his own hand. "He smiled. When it turned. He looked up the stair and smiled at somebody who wasn't there."

"At his brother."

"Yes," said Calder. "I think so. Yes."

In the corner, Ada put her mending down on her knee and looked at the fire.

---

Neither of them said anything about tomorrow.

He noticed it afterwards, lying on the floor by Liss's hearth with his coat over him and Wren's foot in his back, because she'd come down from the loft in the night and burrowed in, as she used to when she was small. He thought about it a long time in the dark. They'd sat at the same table half the night, and she'd heard everything he had, and he'd heard the one thing she was going to give, and neither of them had said *what now* or *where* or *will you*. As if they'd agreed not to, without agreeing.

He didn't know where she was going. He knew where he was going. Varrhold, with a dragon who couldn't fly, to stand in a yard and say it. Then Skarrow, to the uncle, and say it again. As many times as it took.

He didn't think her road went that way. He didn't think she thought so either.

At the first grey he got up, carefully, so as not to wake Wren, and went out.

The dragons were on the shingle below the landing, the three of them curled together, the old one in the middle with her wing out over the stones. Kharrak lifted his head when Calder came out and put it down again. Lisska didn't wake.

On the end of the jetty the lion lay with his head toward the river. Maren was sitting against his shoulder with her knees up and the river watch sword across them. She was awake. She didn't turn round.

The river went by, grey, smooth, carrying the night's rubbish down to Millbrook. Up on the hill, Greyhold's walls stood dark against the dark sky, with no lights on them anywhere.

He sat down on the shingle against the old dragon's side, and she put her head round and laid it by his knee, and the two of them, on the shingle and on the jetty, a stone's throw apart, watched the light come.
