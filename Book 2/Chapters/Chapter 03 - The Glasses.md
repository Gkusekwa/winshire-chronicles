At the second bell of the night, glass two hundred and one stood at nine and a quarter, and Idony Vael wrote it down.

He wrote it in the ledger with the ink he'd made himself, which was blacker than the College's and cost a third as much, in the column headed in his own hand eleven years ago *Hearthmere: the river hill*. Nine and a quarter. He looked at it. He looked at the glass, a finger of fine clear crystal in its brass cradle on the third shelf, with a bead of quicksilver standing in its throat under a word cut in the old script. He looked back at the ledger.

Nine and a quarter. On a glass that, for eleven years, at every bell of every day, had read between nought and one.

"Well," he said to it. "That's new."

The glass didn't answer. Glasses didn't. That was one of the things he liked about them.

---

The Gallery of Glasses ran the whole length of the College of Tides' east wing, a hundred and forty paces from the door to the window at the far end, and every pace of it on both sides was shelves. Oak shelves, black with age, from floor to roof, and on every shelf the brass cradles, and in every cradle a glass. Three hundred and twelve. Each one was cut with the name of a place in the hooked script of the old milestones: a river ford, a hill, a city, a spring nobody remembered the name of in any other tongue. The quicksilver in it rose and fell with the Deep under that place. You could stand at the door at night with a lamp and look down the Gallery and see the whole of Winshire breathing, if you knew how to look.

Nobody else did, much. The College of Tides was the smallest of Vael's colleges, and the poorest, and the oldest, and its readers spent their lives at these shelves writing numbers into ledgers that nobody above the Third ring ever opened. Idony had been at them since he was twenty. He'd come up from the lake-ferries with a letter from a village priest and a talent for the old script that nobody could account for, and the College had looked at his hands, which were a ferryman's hands, and given him a Second ring and the night bells and the Hearthmere ledgers, because nobody wanted the Hearthmere ledgers.

Nobody wanted them because they were boring. For three hundred years every glass cut for Hearthmere had read low. Not dead. Low. A kingdom of lamps that burned without oil and wells that ran sweet and licensed words that worked every time, and under it, by every glass the College owned, almost nothing. Old readers had noted it once and stopped noting it. The Hearthmere glasses were faulty; Hearthmere was an anomaly; it was in the commentaries.

Idony had looked at it for eleven years and come to a different view, which he had written up, twice, and been told, twice, to stop writing up.

It was a hole. A draw. As if something under that one kingdom were pumping the Deep out of the ground as fast as it came in.

---

At the third bell glass two hundred and one stood at eleven.

So did two hundred and two, *the bridge-island*, and two hundred and three, *the far bank*, which had never in eleven years read above two. Further down the shelf the Marches glasses were all climbing, slow, steady, like water rising in a lock. Further down still, *Hallowmere*, *the Mere*, *the King's Road*, all of Hearthmere's glasses, climbing.

He went down the Gallery with his lamp.

Everything was up, not only Hearthmere. Isvaren's own glasses on the north wall, *Vael*, *Glasswater*, *the Pass*, were up by a third. Kessmoor's, on the high shelves, up. The Wyldmarch, up. Sarovel, Saltreach, up and up. Some of the oldest glasses, the ones that had sat on the top shelves since before the Council of Colleges had a council, were higher than he'd ever seen them, higher than anyone had written in any ledger he'd read, and he'd read all of them.

He stood in the middle of the Gallery with the lamp in his hand and listened to it. You couldn't hear the Deep. Everybody knew that. But on the shelves round him three hundred brass cradles were ticking very faintly as they warmed, and the glasses in them were making a sound like a wet finger drawn round the rim of a cup, very high, at the edge of hearing, and he had never heard it before, and he found that the back of his neck was cold.

"Something is happening," he said aloud. "Something is happening everywhere at once, and it's happening most of all in the place that never happens."

He looked down at the ledger in the crook of his arm and laughed. He couldn't help it. He was frightened, and he had been right for eleven years, and he couldn't yet tell which of those was louder.

---

He went out on the gallery's end balcony to look at the city.

Vael lay below him on its hill above the black water of Glasswater, the slate towers of the colleges stepping down to the lake shore with their covered bridges between them and the snow still on the roofs, and every college's lamps lit in its own colour: the Blue College blue, the College of Stars a pale silver, the Physicians green, the Scribes' College amber. On an ordinary night the city looked like a hand of cards laid down on a black table.

Tonight every lamp in it was burning white.

Every tower, every bridge, every lantern on the quays far below, every lamp on every ferry on the lake, the same hard white, like the snow had caught fire. Up in the observatory towers of the College of Stars, where the lantern owls nested, the owls were out on the ledges in the cold with their eyes blazing like coals, turning their heads, all of them, south. Not one was asleep. Somewhere below, on the Scribes' stair, a student had chalked a sticking-charm on his door to keep it shut against the wind, and Idony could hear him, faintly, swearing, because it wouldn't open.

He stood on the balcony in his shirt in the frost and looked south.

There was nothing to see. Seventy-odd leagues of night between him and Hearthmere, and the Iron Fells across it like a wall. There was nothing to see, and every glass in the Gallery behind him was pointing at it like a dog.

---

He made himself go back in and take the bells.

That was the discipline. Whatever was happening, it was happening at every bell, and if he didn't write it down at every bell then afterwards there'd be stories instead of numbers, and stories were what you got instead of knowledge. So he went down the shelves with his lamp and his ledger at the fourth bell and the fifth, glass by glass, three hundred and twelve, and wrote. His hands were steady. He was a little proud of that.

At the fifth bell glass two hundred and one stood at nineteen. There was no twenty on its scale. The quicksilver had climbed into the throat of the glass above the last mark and stood there, trembling, against the cut word.

At the sixth bell he heard the first one crack.

Somewhere on the Kessmoor shelves, a small dry tick, like ice settling on a pond. He went to look and couldn't find it. Then another, on the Saltreach wall. Then two together.

At a quarter before midnight the whole Gallery was singing.

He couldn't have said it any other way afterwards, and he tried, for the Council, and they wrote down "a high persistent sound" and he let them. Three hundred and twelve glasses, each one at the top of its throat, each one ringing its own note at the edge of hearing, so that the Gallery was full of a chord nobody had ever written. The lamp in his hand burned white. The brass cradles were warm to the touch. He stood in the middle of the floor and didn't write anything down, because there wasn't anything to write that he'd have believed in the morning.

He thought: *something is opening.*

He didn't know where the thought had come from.

At midnight every glass in the Gallery broke at once.

---

It was like the end of a feast. Like three hundred cups dropped on stone together, one sound made of hundreds. Then a rain of very small glass, falling, pattering on the shelves, on the floor, in his hair. Then silence, and in the silence, very softly, the sound of quicksilver running.

He looked down. It was running in the floor. The old builders had cut grooves in the Gallery's flagstones for exactly this, three hundred years ago, against the day a glass broke, so that the quicksilver would run to the drains and not into the readers' boots. No glass had broken in the Gallery in living memory. Now the grooves were silver from end to end, a hundred and forty paces of them, shining in the lamplight, all running toward the window at the far end.

South. The floor sloped south.

He stood in it to his ankles in his stockinged feet and the quicksilver went round them, cold and heavy and bright, and ran on.

"Well," said Idony Vael, to the empty shelves. "That's the ledger closed, then."

---

He didn't sleep.

He went up to his room under the roof, where the ceiling sloped so steeply that he could only stand in the middle of it, and pulled the crate out from under his bed.

It was against College rules. It was against several College rules. The College made its glasses in the College's own glasshouse, of the finest Isvaren crystal, and cut their words with the College's own tools under a Fourth-ring Scribe's eye, and no reader was to make glasses of his own or cut words of his own or keep instruments in a sleeping-room. Idony had done all three. Over three winters, with a glass-blower's apprentice on the lake shore who owed his sister money, he'd made eleven glasses of thick green bottle-glass, ugly, uneven, the kind of glass you made pickle jars out of, and cut their words himself with a cobbler's awl and a magnifying lens, because he'd wanted glasses for Hearthmere that he could read at his own bells, without asking.

He'd cut eleven names. *The river hill.* *The bridge-island.* *The far bank.* *The water meadows.* And then a line of places north and east of the Marches that he'd chosen because they made a straight line on the old charts and he'd wanted to see whether the Deep under the hole moved along it: *the forest of the fell*, *the hill of the hold*, *the high moor*, *the black water*, *the edge*, and two more on the far side of the Scarfell whose names nobody had spoken in a thousand years.

He lifted the lid.

Three of them were cracked. Long cracks, from the cradle up the throat, the kind you got in thick cheap glass when it was asked to do something it wasn't made for. Not one had broken. The quicksilver was still in all eleven, standing high in their throats, quivering, the green glass gone warm in his hands as if he'd been holding them.

He set them out on the floor in their line, south-west to north-east, the way he'd chosen them, and sat on the floorboards with his ledger on his knee and his back against the bed, and waited for the light.

---

It came grey and then gold over the Iron Fells.

Out of his little window he saw the first stormkites come down off the High Crown, early, in pairs, the way they did before weather: great grey fork-tailed shapes sliding down the cold air off the snowfields over the city, not beating their wings, falling and catching and falling, wide as a barn roof and light as paper. The colleges' lamps had gone back to their colours some time after midnight and were going out now, one by one, as the porters went round. Down on Glasswater a ferry was putting out with its lantern owl in its wicker cage on the prow, and he thought of Nessa, who'd be up, who was always up, and wondered whether her owl's eyes had blazed all night too, and whether she'd been frightened, and knew that she would say not.

Then the sun came over the Fells, and he looked at the glasses.

The first four went down.

He watched it happen. *The river hill* first. The quicksilver in its throat, standing at the top all night, dropped. Not to low. Not to the nought-and-a-half it had read for eleven years. Past nought. To the bottom of the glass. Flat. The bead of silver lay in the bottom of the cradle like a coin in a dry well.

*The bridge-island*, the same. *The far bank.* *The water meadows.* One after another, a breath apart, all four, down to nothing.

He didn't understand it. He sat and looked at it.

This was no draw. A draw pulled; you could see it pulling, a glass held low and straining. This wasn't held. There was nothing in these glasses at all. As if the ground under them had been a well, for three hundred years, and someone had been drawing on it day and night, and now the rope was cut, and the well wasn't there.

Then the fifth glass dipped.

*The forest of the fell.* It went down, sharp, not to the bottom, a quarter of the way, and came back up. As if something had passed over it. As if something had passed over the place it named and taken a drink as it went by.

He grabbed the ledger.

*The hill of the hold*: down, and up. *The high moor*, a little later. *The black water.* *The edge.* One after another, south-west to north-east, along the line he'd chosen because it was straight on an old chart. Each glass dipping and coming back as something went over the place it named. He counted between them. He had a good count; he'd learned it on the ferries, counting oar-strokes in the fog. He wrote the counts down. He did the arithmetic on the floorboards with the stub of a pencil because he didn't trust his hand with the pen.

Leagues between the places. Counts between the dips.

Something was moving north-east across the old realm at the speed of a bird.

Not a bird. A bird didn't drink from the Deep as it flew. A bird didn't dip a glass seventy leagues away. Something the speed of a bird, and the size of nothing he had a word for, going north-east, away from the place where for eleven years there'd been a hole, which wasn't there any more.

---

He made the chart.

It took him the morning. He drew it the way he'd drawn the Hearthmere chart every month for eleven years, on the same grid, so the two could be laid one over the other. The old chart had a hole on it, a long dark well of ink-strokes over the river hill where the lines went down and down. The new chart had nothing there. Flat. A dead place, a little round dead place where the hole had been, with the lines lying across it like threads across an empty frame.

And a line of dips going north-east from it, each one a little smaller than the last.

There were two other marks. He nearly didn't put them in, because they were small and he couldn't account for them, and things he couldn't account for were what got your ledgers taken away. He put them in anyway.

One was on the Hearthmere bank glasses, *the water meadows* and *the river hill*, before they went flat: a small draw, there for a count and gone, there again a little further along, gone. Short steps. Like something walking. Like a beast walking along a riverbank, taking a little of the Deep with every footfall and giving it back. He watched it on the last of the readings and it went upriver, then stopped, then went down, then stopped. He wrote beside it, in the margin, *the walker*, and then crossed it out, because it was fanciful, and then wrote it again.

The other was under *the river hill*, at the very bottom of the glass, under the flat. When he put the lens on it there was a point in the bottom of the throat where the bead of quicksilver didn't sit still and didn't move. It wasn't drawing and it wasn't rising. It was doing neither, very precisely, as a man is doing neither when he has filled his lungs and is holding them.

He wrote, *a held breath.* He didn't cross that one out.

---

The Long Library was half a league long and built into the hill above the city, and the Old Reader lived at the far end of it.

You went in at the lake end through a door like a barn's and walked up the hall between the stacks with the hill getting closer over you, the roof coming down in vaults of grey stone that had been there before anyone had thought of colleges. The stacks went up into the dark on either side. Lamps hung in the aisles every fifty paces, burning their ordinary amber again this morning. Readers sat at the long tables under them with their heads down, and some of them looked up as he went by with the chart under his arm and his hair full of glass, and looked down again.

At the far end, under the last vault, where the hall went into the hill and the air was cold and smelled of stone and old paper, there was a perch of black oak as thick as a man's leg, and on it sat the Old Reader.

It was black going grey, the size of a large dog, with feathers that lay over its shoulders like a scholar's gown and a beak like a cobbler's awl. One eye was milky. The other was black and wet and missed nothing. It hadn't flown in anyone's memory. It sat on its perch at the end of the Long Library and was brought scraps by the junior readers and corrected their citations, and nobody knew how old it was, and if you asked it, it asked you what you meant by old.

It was the only friend Idony had in Vael. He'd never said so to anyone, because it was the kind of thing that got a Second ring talked about.

"Ferryman," said the Old Reader, when he was still twenty paces off. Its voice was dry and very exact, like a quill on good paper. "You have glass in your hair."

"The Gallery broke."

"I heard it. The whole hill heard it." The black eye considered him. "All of it?"

"Every glass. At midnight. At once."

The Old Reader was quiet. Its pauses were long; you learned to wait.

"That has not happened," it said, "in the time I have sat here."

"How long have you sat here?"

"Long enough to find the question tiresome. Show me what you have under your arm."

---

He unrolled the charts on the reading-desk at the foot of the perch, the old one and the new one, side by side, and weighted their corners with the bird's dish and two books and his own hand. The Old Reader leaned down from its perch, slowly, the way it did everything, and turned its head to put the good eye close.

He told it. The surge all night, every glass in Winshire climbing, every lamp in Vael burning white. Midnight. His green glasses under the bed, which it already knew about and had never told anyone. Sunrise. The hole gone. Not filled: gone. And the line of dips going north-east at the speed of a bird.

The Old Reader listened. It didn't interrupt, which was rare. When he got to the speed of the thing going north-east, its feathers lifted all down its back, very slightly, and settled again.

"Where was the hole?" it said.

"You know where. I've shown you every month for eleven years."

"Say it."

"Under Hearthmere," said Idony. "Under the river hill on the border. Where Greyhold is. The knights' school."

The Old Reader went silent.

He was used to its silences. He waited. He waited the length of a silence, and then the length of another, and then he became aware that the reader at the nearest table, fifty paces back down the hall, had looked up, and that the bird hadn't moved at all, not the good eye, not a feather, and that its talons on the black oak had closed so hard the wood creaked.

"Old one?"

The silence went on. It was not the kind he knew. He had sat with this bird through a thousand pauses and every one had had a shape, a dryness, the feeling of a sentence being chosen. This one had nothing in it. It was like the flat glass. It was like looking into a well that wasn't there.

Then the Old Reader said, very quietly, in a voice he had never heard it use, a voice like an old man's in the dark:

"Then the door is open."

---

"What door?"

The bird didn't answer.

"Old one. What door? What's under Greyhold?"

"You should go home, ferryman," said the Old Reader. It had found its own voice again, or most of it. "Go home and sleep. You have glass in your hair."

"What door is open?"

The good eye looked at him a long time.

"Leave me the green glasses," it said. "Tonight. Bring them here and leave them with me, and come back in the morning."

He thought about it. He thought about it longer than he was proud of, standing at the foot of the perch with the chart in his hands, because it had asked him for something, and it had never in eleven years asked him for anything.

"No," he said. "I'm sorry. No."

The Old Reader looked at him. Then it did something it had never done: it laughed. A dry, short sound, like a page torn.

"Good," it said. "That's the right answer. Remember you gave it." It settled its feathers. "Go home, Reader Vael."

It only called him that when it was displeased with him, or when it wanted him to hear something as if a stranger had said it. He went home, down the half league of the Long Library with the chart under his arm, and felt the black eye on his back the whole way to the door.

---

He copied the charts that night.

He didn't know why, exactly. He told himself afterwards it was the bird's laugh. He sat up under the sloping roof with a lamp and a sheet of the oiled silk the ferry-folk used for sail-patches, which Nessa had given him once for something and he'd never used, and he traced both charts onto it with a fine pen, the old hole and the new flat and the line going north-east and the walker and the held breath, every mark, every number in the margin. It took till the second bell. Then he folded the silk small and unpicked the lining of his winter coat along the hem and sewed it in, badly, with the big stitches his mother had despaired of.

He felt ridiculous. He felt like a boy hiding a letter. He went to bed and slept the whole of the next day through and woke at dusk with his face in the ledger.

---

Magister Hollis Dane came to the Gallery on the morning of the second day.

Idony was on his knees at the far end with a brush and a pan, sweeping glass. The College had sent no one else. The glasshouse would take a year to blow three hundred and twelve new glasses and the College couldn't afford them in ten, and the senior readers had come and looked at the silver in the floor-grooves and gone away again, and nobody had told him to stop, so he swept.

He heard them before he saw them. Boots on the Gallery floor, and claws.

Dane was a tall man of fifty with a short grey beard and the blue facings of the Blue College on his coat and a Fifth ring on his right hand, notched five times, that he wore turned outward so you saw it when he gestured. Behind him came two Wardens in their pale grey cloaks with their staves, and at the Wardens' heels two snow lynx, white, silent, the size of wolves, with pale eyes like frost on a window. The lynx stopped where the silver ran in the floor and wouldn't step over it, and sat down, and looked at Idony.

"Reader Vael," said Dane.

"Magister."

"You keep the Hearthmere ledgers."

"I did. There's not much to keep this morning."

Dane didn't smile. He looked down the empty shelves and the silver grooves and the glass. He had the look of a man doing an inventory of a fire.

"The Council of Colleges has asked the Blue College to review the readings of the night of the breaking," Dane said. "All of them. With particular attention to the south." He held out a hand without looking at it. One of the Wardens put a paper in it, folded, sealed with blue wax. "You'll give the Wardens your ledgers. All the Hearthmere volumes, and the nightbook for the last month. And any other instruments in your keeping."

"There aren't any other instruments in my keeping."

"Reader," said Dane, mildly. "There is a crate under your bed."

Idony looked at him.

"The College is a small place," said Dane. "And you're not as quiet as you think." He turned the seal over in his fingers. "It's for the Council's review. Nobody's accusing you of anything. You've done excellent work, I'm told. Eleven years of it." He said it the way a man says a horse has a fine coat before he buys it. "The Council will want to understand it properly. You'll be called when they do."

---

They took everything.

The ledgers, all forty-one volumes of them, eleven years of his handwriting in his own black ink. The nightbook. Both charts, the old and the new, from the table by his window. The crate, with the eleven green glasses in it, carried down his narrow stair by a Warden who held it at arm's length like a box of eggs. One of the lynx came up the stair after it and stood in the doorway of his room and looked at him, and he looked back at it, and thought that it was a beautiful animal, and that it had no idea what any of this was, and envied it.

Dane stood at the foot of the stair and watched them load it.

"You'll be called," he said again. Then, as if it had just occurred to him: "The Hearthmere draw. You wrote it up twice, I'm told."

"Twice."

"And were asked not to write it up again."

"Twice."

Dane nodded slowly. "The Council," he said, "may find that it was not a subject for study." He put on his gloves. "Good morning, Reader Vael."

They went. The lynx went last, looking back.

---

Idony stood in the middle of his room under the sloping roof.

There was a clean square on the floor by the bed where the crate had been. There was a clean oblong on the table where the ledgers had been. There was glass in the cracks of the floorboards that he hadn't swept, and it caught the light.

His winter coat hung on its peg on the back of the door.

He went to it and put his hand on the hem, and felt the stiff fold of the oiled silk under the lining, with his bad stitches round it.

He stood like that for a while.

Then he took the coat down off its peg, though it was a mild morning and the snow was going off the roofs, and put it on, and buttoned it to the neck. He went down the stair and out across the College court, past the Gallery door with its smell of quicksilver, and down the long stair to the lake, where Nessa's ferry was coming in to the quay with its lantern owl blinking in its cage on the prow, ordinary amber in the daylight, and the water flat and black and very still.

He'd been right for eleven years. He thought he ought to feel something about that.

What he felt, standing on the quay in his winter coat in the sun, was that somewhere seventy-odd leagues south, something had been holding its breath for three hundred years, and was still holding it, and nobody in the world but him and a bird had noticed.
