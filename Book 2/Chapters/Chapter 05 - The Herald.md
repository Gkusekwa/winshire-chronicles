The herald came on the fourth morning, and he had to shout.

Calder heard the horn first: a thin brass bray from the top of the landing, cracked, as if whoever was blowing it had expected it to be louder and was embarrassed. Then a man's voice, high and strained. He was down on the shingle with the dragons, washing the wound in the old one's chest with river water and a rag, the way Liss had shown him, and he stood up with the rag dripping and looked.

There was a thin man at the top of the jetty steps in a tabard of Hallowmere blue with a gold crown on it, holding a brass horn in one hand and a paper in the other. Two guards behind him in the same blue, with halberds. A small crowd gathering on the landing: the refugees from the stores by the water-gate, Liss's neighbours from the net-huts, some knights from the hill who'd come down to see. Rowan's knights, in grey, standing a little apart.

The herald blew his horn again. It brayed and cracked.

"It used to have a word in it," said Liss, beside him. She'd come out of the house drying her hands. "For loudness. All the heralds' horns did. You could hear the crier on the King's Road at Ashford from the Mere." She watched the thin man redden. "Now he's got to do it himself."

---

The herald read it from the steps, at the top of his voice, which wasn't much of a voice.

There were two parts. The first was for the people from the Saltreach bank who'd crossed in the war: the Garrows and the Pennicks and the rest from Harrow's End who'd come over on Liss's ferry in the thaw, and the ones from Upper Rush, and the ones from further up who'd come down the Wend on rafts when the companies burned their farms. *By order of the crown's council*, all subjects of the League of Saltreach who had crossed the river during the late disorders were to cross back within ten days, the crown having no bread to spare for the subjects of a league that had sent its silver against it.

Somebody in the crowd said, loudly, "Whose silver?"

The herald didn't hear, or pretended not to.

Calder looked at Tessa Garrow. She was standing at the edge of the landing with Davy against her skirts, and her face had gone very still.

"Back to what?" she said. Not loudly. To nobody. "Back to *what*?"

The second part was for him.

*Calder Fenn of Harrow's End in the Marches, called the farmer with the dragons,* the herald read, and the crowd on the landing went quiet in a different way and turned round, all of them, and looked down at the shingle where he was standing with a wet rag in his hand. *For crossing armed into the crown's land in the late war, and bringing beasts of war across the border, and for the deaths of the crown's subjects at Redwatch and the island:* outlawed. In Hearthmere and all its lands. Any subject of the crown might take him. A hundred crowns, alive.

Behind Calder, the red dragon lifted his head off the shingle.

*Deaths,* said Kharrak. *What deaths.*

"Lie still."

*We killed nobody on their side. Nobody. We burned their boats. We held the gate. We…*

"I know. Lie still."

The herald had finished. He rolled up the paper. He looked down from the top of the jetty steps at the man on the shingle with the rag, and at the three dragons lying behind him, and at the lion lying full length on the end of the jetty, and the herald's face did a long slow thing that Calder would remember: the face of a man who has read out a reward of a hundred crowns for somebody, and only now, at the end, has seen what the somebody keeps for company.

He looked at Rowan's knights, standing a little apart in their grey. They looked back at him. None of them moved.

The herald went back up the landing to his horse, quite fast, with his guards behind him.

"A hundred crowns," said Ada, that night, at Liss's table.

"Alive."

"Your father," said Ada, "would have been very proud."

He looked at her. She was mending again, the same shirt she'd been mending for four days. She didn't look up.

"I'm not saying he'd have been proud of you," she said. "I'm saying he'd have been proud of the sum."

Across the table Wren laughed, suddenly, the first time since the jetty, and put her hand over her mouth, and Ada's needle stopped for a moment, and went on.

---

Fisk's boy came over in the morning.

Calder saw the punt coming from the far bank, a little flat eel-punt with one boy standing in the stern poling, the way Fisk's boys had poled across the Wend all their lives, which nobody on the Saltreach side was supposed to do and everybody did. It came in under the landing and the boy threw a rope round a post on the jetty and came up the steps two at a time.

It was Abe, Fisk's youngest. Fourteen, all elbows. He'd rowed over in the night of the gate to tell Wren her father had gone to the Ruin; Wren had told Calder. He stopped at the top of the steps when he saw the lion, and stood there with his mouth open, and the lion opened one pale eye and looked at him and shut it again.

"It's all right," said Calder. "Come down."

Abe came down the shingle the long way round, wide of the jetty end.

"My da says," Abe said, and stopped to breathe, and started again. "My da says to tell you. He says you'd want it told."

"Tell it."

So he told it, standing on the shingle with the dragons behind Calder and his eyes going to them every few words and coming back.

Harrow's End was stripped. Not burned, this time, or not much: stripped. Everything that would carry. The companies that had stood on the hill all night and walked off it in the morning without anyone telling them to, the sellswords and the Wendmouth men and Delling's lot and the rest, nobody had paid them, and nobody was going to now, so they were paying themselves. They'd come down off the hill into the village and gone through it house by house and taken the pots and the blankets and the seed-corn and the hens, and gone off up the Drover's Way with it, north and south, to sell wherever there was anyone left to buy. The Way was full of them. Bands of ten and twenty, going up and down, taking what they could from every steading on the Way, and fighting each other for it when they met.

"They took the Garrows' door," said Abe. "The door. Off its hinges. Da says what's anyone want a door for." He wiped his nose. "And the shrine bowl. At the crossroads. The coppers. The bowl and all."

Calder thought of the crossroads shrine at Harrow's End, where he'd put a copper in the bowl every market day of his life, like everyone did, for luck, and never once thought about who it was for.

"And the Clan?"

Abe shook his head. "Still at it. Up the glens. Da says Arn's riders burned a steading at Skell Head, and Ulric's burned two at the Lowe, and nobody can tell him what for." He shrugged, all elbows. "Da says they've forgot. He says it's been going so long they've forgot what for, and now they do it because the other lot did."

The old bronze dragon made a sound, low, behind Calder. He felt it through the bond. Not words. Something like a sigh, something like a held grief let out.

"And your da?"

"In his door." Abe almost smiled. "He won't leave it. Mam wants him to come over. He says somebody's got to stay. To tell it after."

"Tell him I said thank you."

"He says don't come back," said Abe. "Not yet. He says there's men on the Way asking for you by name."

---

Ada went over to the Staithe after noon with a pail.

It was Liss's idea, or Ada's; Calder never found out which. They'd been at the table together in the morning, the two old women, talking in low voices while he was out with the dragons, and when he came in Ada had her shawl on and a tin pail on her arm and Liss was untying the ferry.

"Milk," said Ada. "There's a woman at the Staithe keeps goats. We've the children to think of."

"Liss has milk."

"Liss's milk," said Ada, "is turning."

He looked at Liss. Liss shrugged. "It always kept," she said. "Three days, four, in the cold-room. My mother's words over the churn. Till this winter. Then it kept a day. Since the night it flew, it turns by evening."

He watched them go across on the guide-rope, the flat ferry going out into the brown spring water with his mother in the bow holding her pail and Liss hauling on the rope, hand over hand, the way she'd hauled it thirty years. Halfway across the current took the ferry and swung it, and the rope went taut and sang, and held.

They came back an hour before dusk. Ada came up the jetty with the pail full and a cloth over it, and set it on Liss's table, and took the cloth off, and put two fingers on the side of the pail.

She said the keeping-word over it. Low. The word he'd heard her say over every pail and churn and bowl of whey since before he could walk.

Nothing. He felt nothing. He'd never felt anything when she did it; that was the point of words, you didn't feel them, they just worked.

Ada looked at the pail a long time.

"Over there," she said, "it took."

He waited.

"At the Staithe. I said it over the pail at the goat-woman's door, the way I always do, out of habit. And it took." She took her fingers off the pail. "Not like it used to. Faint. Like a fire in damp wood. You had to wait for it." She looked at him. "But it took. Over there. And here," she touched the pail, "nothing."

Liss was standing at the fire with her back to them. She didn't turn round.

"It's the ground," said Calder slowly. "On this side. The surgeon said. On the bank at Redwatch. He said it's dead ground."

"I heard him say it," said Liss, to the fire.

"And the far side's not."

"The far side's not," said Ada. "Not yet."

He thought about that. The river, a quarter-league of brown water, and on one side of it a kingdom of dead lamps and sour wells and words that went into the air and stopped, and on the other side a pail of goat's milk that would keep, faintly, if you waited for it.

He thought about the setting-songs. The ledges at Varrhold had setters: old riders past flying, who set dragons' bones and sang over them. He'd seen one at it in the autumn, an old man with his hands on a young dragon's foreleg, singing, and the young dragon's eyes half-closed. Clan words. Saltreach ground.

He went out to the shingle.

---

The old one was lying where she'd lain for five days, with the torn wing out and her chest to the river. When he came close he smelled it before he saw it. Sweet. Wrong. He'd smelled it on a ewe in a bad spring when a cut went bad under the fleece and nobody found it till the flies did.

He knelt and looked at the wound. The horn had gone in under the joint of the foreleg, on the night of the locks, when the ironback got its horn into her on the fort's roof and rode her down through the floor. It was a hand's width across. He'd washed it every day with river water, the way Liss had shown him. The edges of it had gone dark and swollen, and the smell came up out of it.

*It is nothing,* said Aurveth.

"It's not nothing. It's going bad."

*I have had worse.*

"You said that about the wing."

She was quiet. Her one gold eye was half shut.

*Yes,* she said. *I said that about the wing.*

He sat back on his heels on the wet stones.

"There's setters at Varrhold," he said. "On the ledges. Old riders. They set bones. They sing over them." He put his hand flat on her jaw. "On the right ground, they'd do something. Here they'd do nothing. Here nothing does anything."

*Varrhold is eighteen leagues from the Staithe. Fourteen of them on the Way.*

"I know."

*I cannot fly eighteen leagues. I cannot fly one.*

"Then we'll walk it."

She opened the eye and looked at him.

*And Arn,* she said. *In his yard. With his brother's death in his mouth and your name in it.*

"Yes."

*He'll not believe you.*

"Probably not." He took his hand away. "But he'll hear it. Out loud. In his own yard, in front of the ledges. And then I'll go to Skarrow and say it to Ulric, out loud, in his. Both of them. All of it. The ford, and the mill, and the arch." He looked at the river. "Somebody has to say it to them. Rook said nobody believes the barley man. Well. Maybe not. But they'll have heard him."

Behind him the red one lifted his head.

*We'll go with you,* said Kharrak. *Into the yard.*

"I know you will."

*If Arn says liar…*

"Then Arn says liar, and you lie still." He turned round. "Kharrak. You lie still. Whatever he says."

The red dragon looked at him for a long time. Then he put his head down on his forefeet, and the red tide that Calder could always feel in him, low and hot, went down a little.

*For you,* said Kharrak. *Not for him.*

"That'll do."

Out on the shingle by the water Lisska had been pretending to be asleep. She opened one pale green eye.

*Is Wren coming?*

He didn't answer.

*Calder. Is Wren coming?*

---

He told Wren at the end of the jetty, at dusk, with her legs over the water and Old Grey standing behind her.

He'd told her once before. In the autumn, when he left her with Ada and went to Crow Scar with three dragons nobody could stop him having and half the Marches looking for him with crossbows. He'd told her then that wherever he was, people came looking, and people who came looking for him found whoever was near him, and he wasn't going to have her near him till they'd stopped looking. He'd told her that, and she'd made him say it twice, and then she'd gone into the house and not come out till he'd gone.

He told her again. The same reason. The old reason. A hundred crowns, alive, and a herald who'd read it out on the landing in front of everybody, and men on the Drover's Way asking for him by name.

Wren listened. She didn't look at him. She looked at the river.

"You'll be on the Way," she said.

"Yes."

"With the old one walking."

"Yes."

"So they'll find you easy. Because she can't fly."

He hadn't thought of that. He thought of it now.

"Yes," he said.

Wren was quiet. Behind her Old Grey shifted his great feet on the planks of the jetty, and the jetty creaked.

"Say it again," said Wren. "Why."

"Because wherever I am, people come looking. And I'll not have them find you."

She nodded. She still didn't look at him.

"I'm not going to argue," she said. "Grandma says I'm not to argue with you this time. She says you've got enough." She swung her feet over the water. "So I'm not."

"Thank you."

"I'm going to be very cross," said Wren, "after you've gone."

"I know."

"Very cross. For ages."

"That's all right."

She looked at him then. Senna's look. Long and level and storing it.

"Lisska's to come back," she said. "Every few days. And tell me."

"She will."

"Every few days."

"Every few days," said Calder. "Every few days."

Behind her, in the dusk, Old Grey put his great head down, slowly, and rested his chin on the top of Wren's head, as a horse rests its chin on a gate. He'd never done it before. Wren went very still under it. Then she put her hand up, without looking round, and laid it flat on the grey muzzle, and left it there.

He found Maren on the end of the jetty after dark.

She was sitting against the lion with a whetstone and a sword across her knees. Not the river watch sword. A longer one, with a grip wound in dark leather and a pommel like a walnut, and along the fuller a line of small cut letters with hooks at the ends of the strokes. She was drawing the stone down the edge, slow, steady, the way he'd draw a stone down a scythe-blade on a summer evening. The letters along the fuller caught the light of Liss's window behind them and were only cuts in steel, dark.

"Whose is it?"

"Haverly's," she said. "From the stair."

He didn't know who Haverly was. He'd heard the name. A knight, with a falcon. He didn't ask.

"Lisska says those letters burned," he said. "On the stair. White. She saw it from the yard. She says they cut spear-shafts like reeds."

"They did." She drew the stone down. "On the stair. In the surge."

"They don't now."

"No." She held the blade up and looked along it. The letters lay along the fuller like writing on a grave. "Nothing does, here."

"Over the river it might."

She looked at him.

"Ada said the milk-word over a pail at the Staithe," he said. "It took. Faint. Here, nothing. So I'm going over. To Varrhold. The setters on the ledges might do something for her wing, over there. And there's two yards I have to stand in."

"I know."

"Wren stays. With your mother. And mine."

"I know that too."

He stood there on the planks. She went on with the stone. Somewhere out on the dark water a fish jumped.

"I'll write," he said. "To you. When I can."

She stopped the stone.

"You can write *two*," she said.

"I'll learn the rest."

He thought for a moment she might laugh, the way she'd nearly laughed in the reeds and nearly laughed at Liss's table, and that this time she might let it out. She didn't. She looked at him with something in her face he couldn't read, and then went back to the stone, and drew it down the edge once more, slow, all the way to the point.

---

They went at dawn on the sixth day.

The whole landing was out. Liss had the ferry off its slip and in the water, nosed up against the jetty with the guide-rope taut. Ada was on the jetty with Pip by the hand and Nan on her hip. Bel was in the door of the house and didn't come any further. Tam was there, the brother, with Odo propped in a chair at the top of the jetty steps under a blanket, scowling at the dragons as if they owed him money. Wren was on the end of the jetty with her arms folded, being very cross already. Old Grey stood behind her.

Maren was at the foot of the landing, with the lion.

He went to her last.

He didn't know what to say. He'd known her six days. He'd met her in the dark under the river with three dragons round him and a lion in front of him and a chain falling past them into the deep, and they'd watched the thing go into the sunrise together, and sat at a table half a night while he told her about a dead man's son, and she'd told him one thing back. That was all. Six days. Less than he'd known half the people on the Drover's Way who wanted him dead.

She looked at him. She had Haverly's sword at her hip now, and the black book he'd seen once inside her coat, and her hands were still in their rags.

He looked north-east, past her, up the Saltreach bank, where the Drover's Way went up into the hills toward Varrhold and the Scarfell. Then he looked at her again.

"Your road's that way," he said, and nodded north-east. Not up the Way. Past it. Over the hills, where the thing had gone into the sunrise.

She looked where he was looking. Then back at him.

"So's yours," she said.

He nodded. It was the right answer. It was what a neighbour said, at a gate, at the end of a harvest, to the man from the next farm, when they both had a long way to walk home and the same hill to go over, and different doors at the end.

He went down to the water.

---

The old dragon swam the Wend.

He'd been afraid of it all night. A dragon twenty feet at the shoulder with a horn-wound in her chest and a wing she couldn't fold, in the spring river, in the current that took Liss's ferry and swung it every crossing. He'd lain by Liss's fire thinking of it.

She went into the water like an old cow going into a pond: slow, careful, testing each step on the stones, then the shelf going out from under her and the river taking her weight. Kharrak went in beside her on the downstream side, under her chest, and took the current on his shoulder so it pushed him into her and not her away from him. Lisska flew over them, low, crying. Calder went in Liss's ferry alongside on the guide-rope, with Liss hauling, close enough to put his hand out and lay it on the old one's neck where it came up out of the water, and he did, and kept it there.

She held the torn wing up out of the river. All the way. He could see it shaking with the effort, the long bones of it trembling against the grey sky. She didn't let it touch the water.

It took the best part of an hour. Halfway the current caught them all, dragons and ferry, and swung them downstream till the rope sang, and Liss swore and hauled, and Kharrak put his head down and pushed, and the old one's great feet found nothing and then found something and then found the shelf on the far side, and she came up out of the Wend onto the stones at Tolly's Staithe with the river running off her in sheets, and stood there on four legs on Saltreach ground, shaking.

He got out of the ferry. He stood on the stones beside her.

Under his boots, faint, like a pulse you have to press for, he thought he felt something. It might have been his own heart. It might have been nothing.

He looked back across the river.

The jetty, small now, a quarter-league off across the brown water. A row of people on it. A small one on the end with her arms folded, and something grey behind her. And at the foot of the landing, a woman sitting down on the stones against the side of a lion, with something long and bright across her knees, drawing a stone down it. Not watching them go. Or watching, and not lifting her head.

He watched her for a while. She didn't look up.

Then he turned round, and put his hand on the old dragon's jaw, and said, "Come on, then," and they started up the Staithe toward the Drover's Way, slowly, three steps and a stop, three steps and a stop, with the red one under her shoulder and the green one going ahead and coming back.
