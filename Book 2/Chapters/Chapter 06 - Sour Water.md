Millbrook's well had been sweet for three hundred years, and on the sixth day after the flight it turned.

Maren smelled it from the bottom of the jetty road. She'd come down from the ferry house with Tam and a handcart to fetch what was left of Odo's tools out of the burned forge, because Odo had lain in Liss's kitchen for two days telling everyone in it that if the tongs and the swages and the good anvil weren't fetched before the companies got to them he'd go down for them himself on his hands, and nobody doubted him. The road went up from the river past the inn and the mill to the square, and the well stood in the middle of the square under its carved stone hood, the way it had stood all her life, with the bucket on its chain and the trough beside it for the beasts.

There was a queue at it. There'd always been a queue at it. Millbrook's well water was famous on the river; Saltreach boatmen came up the Wend for it; it was said to cure the gripe and keep milk and make bread rise. Hearthmere's sweet wells, the ballad-men called them, as if every town had one. Most did.

Today the queue wasn't at the well. It was going past it, round it, wide, as people walk round a dead dog in a lane, and down the far side of the square to the river steps with buckets.

The smell came off the well like a breath out of a cellar. Iron, and eggs, and something under them she didn't have a word for.

Tam stopped the handcart.

"It was all right on the night," he said. "I drew from it. The night of the forge. For the fire." He looked at it. "It was all right."

A woman came past them with two buckets on a yoke, going down to the river. She saw Maren look at the well and said, without stopping, "Brown since yesterday noon. Brown and stinking. My Hal drank from it before anybody knew and he's been on the pot all night." She went on down the steps.

Maren went to the well and looked into it.

The water lay a long way down, dark, and as she watched a bubble came up through it and broke, and the smell came up with it. On the stone of the hood above her head, where the bucket-chain ran through its iron eye, there was a word cut in the old script. She'd seen it every time she'd come to Millbrook all her life and never once looked at it. The licence. Every sweet well in Hearthmere had one, cut into its hood by a Hallowmere stone-reader the year the well was sworn.

It was only cuts in stone.

---

The Widow Rudd was sitting at her door.

She'd been fined at Ashford in the spring of the year before for saying unlicensed words over a neighbour's sick pig. Everyone on the river knew that, and everyone knew she'd been at it ever since, quietly, words for warts and words for colic and words for a cow that wouldn't let down. She sat on a stool now in the open door of her house on the corner of the square with a board propped against her knee. Somebody had chalked on the board, in big uneven letters: WORDS. A COPPER.

She had a little queue. Three women and a man with a goat.

Maren watched. The first woman put a copper in the Widow Rudd's lap and held out a cup of well-water, brown, and the Widow Rudd put two fingers on the cup's rim and said something low and fast over it, and the woman took it back and looked into it.

It was still brown.

The woman looked at it a long time. Then she said, "Thank you, mistress," and went away with it.

The second woman did the same. A copper. A cup. The words. Brown.

"She knows," said Tam, low, beside her. "She knows they don't take. She must."

"Yes."

"Then why's she selling them?"

The man with the goat had reached the front. He didn't have a cup. He put his copper down and put the goat's head in the Widow's lap, and the Widow put her old hand on the goat's head between its horns and said the words, slow, as if she were saying them to the goat and not over it, and the goat stood there with its eyes half shut, and the man stood with his hat in his hands.

"Why are they buying them?" said Maren.

Tam didn't answer.

The man took his goat and went. The Widow Rudd looked up then, across the square, and saw Maren standing by the sour well, and the lion behind her at the bottom of the jetty road where she'd told him to wait. The old woman's face didn't change. She put the coppers in her apron pocket and nodded to Maren, once, one tradeswoman to another across a market, and turned to the next in her queue.

---

They loaded the cart from the forge.

It was a black ruin now, the roof gone and the walls standing round a heap of fallen beams like ribs round a burned carcass, and inside it the great anvil sat where it had always sat on its oak stump, scorched black, too heavy for anybody to have stolen. Tam stood in the doorway a long time before he went in. Maren didn't go in with him. She stood in the street with her back to the door and let him be in there alone.

He came out after a while with the tongs and the swages in a sack and his face set, and they got the anvil onto the cart between them with a plank and a lot of swearing, and Orrun came up the street and stood beside the cart while they did it, and the people of Millbrook stood in their doors and watched.

Nobody came near. Nobody threw anything. They watched the lion stand in their street beside a cart with Odo Marl's anvil on it, and the girl from the ferry with the river watch sword, and they watched them go back down the jetty road, and nobody said a word.

"They used to wave at me," said Tam, on the road back. "In the street. When I was at the forge. Everybody waved."

She didn't say anything.

"I put my ring in the river," he said. "That night. With Odo's punches. Did you know that?"

"No."

"Well, I did." He pushed the cart. "Doesn't matter. They saw me in the street with it on, all winter. They'll not wave." He was quiet for a bit. "I don't blame them. I wouldn't."

---

The council's men came to the ferry house at noon on the seventh day, and she wasn't there.

She was up on the hill at Greyhold, at the graves. She'd gone up in the morning without knowing why, and stood at the foot of the slope where she'd stood on the first day, and after a while she'd gone up it, slowly, past the rows, and found Pell, and stood there. Nobody had told her she couldn't. Orrun lay at the bottom of the hill. She stood at Pell's grave a long time and didn't say anything to it, because Pell had never had any patience for people who talked to things that couldn't answer.

When she came down to the River Road there was a knot of blue at the ferry house.

She saw it from half a league off. Blue tabards, Hallowmere blue, a dozen of them, and halberds catching the light, and horses tied along Liss's fence. She started to run.

By the time she reached the landing it was done.

---

Liss told her. Liss was standing in her own doorway with her big hands hanging at her sides, and her face had the look Maren had only seen on it once before, the morning they brought her father home.

"A writ," said Liss. "With the council's seal. A captain. Pryne, he said, Sir Aldous Pryne, as if I should know it. Twelve men." She looked at her hands. "*The kin of the outlaw Fenn,* it said. To be held at Greyhold in the crown's keeping until the outlaw surrenders the beasts of war to the crown."

"Where are they?"

"They walked." Liss's voice cracked. "They walked up to Greyhold between his men. Ada told them to. Your lion was on the jetty. He got up when they came, and the men went white, every one of them, and stopped, and the captain had his hand on his sword. And Ada came out of my door and said, *We'll not have a lion fighting a writ on our account,* and she took the little ones by the hand and walked past him. Past him, Maren. Close enough to touch. And Bel after her with the baby, and Wren." Liss shut her eyes. "And the lion lay down. He let them by. He lay down on the planks and let them walk past him between those men, and watched them go up the road."

Maren looked at the end of the jetty.

Orrun was lying there. He'd come back down from the hill before her; she hadn't felt him go. He lay with his head on his paws, facing up the River Road toward Greyhold.

*The old woman asked me,* he said.

"I know."

*She asked me not to. With her hand.* He was quiet. *I did what she asked.*

"I know," said Maren. "It was right."

She didn't know if it was right. She knew that Ada Fenn had walked her grandchildren past a lion rather than have blood spilled on Liss's landing, and that the lion had understood her without a bond, and that Maren would not have been able to say no to her either.

"Where's Tam?"

"Gone after them," said Liss. "To see where they're put."

"And Old Grey?"

Liss almost laughed. It was a terrible sound.

"Followed Wren," she said. "All the way up. Walked behind them with his rope dragging. They tried to turn him and he wouldn't turn. Twelve men with halberds couldn't turn him."

---

They were in the old granary in the inner ward.

Tam came back at dusk and told it. The granary was the long stone barn against the inner wall, on the north side, that the school used for oats; it had one door, on the yard side, with a bar across it, and a guard on the door, and the council's men had put the Fenns in it with a bucket and two blankets and a loaf. The inner wall was its back wall. The same wall that had split from top to bottom the morning the thing flew, the old grey stones of it, a crack you could see from a league off.

Old Grey was standing outside Greyhold's gate. He'd followed Wren up the hill and in through the gate, and the gate-men had got him back out of it with whips and shouting and six of them on his rope, and shut the gate on him, and he'd stood outside it ever since. He didn't push it. He didn't try to get in. He stood there with his great head toward the gate, and his rope hanging, and when the guards came out to drive him off he didn't move, and after a while they stopped coming out.

"He's still there," said Tam. "In the dark. He'll be there all night."

"Rowan?"

Tam shook his head. "Didn't see her. They say she was in the yard when the council's lot came in with them. They say she read the writ and gave it back and went up the north tower and didn't come down."

Maren went up the hill that night.

---

She found Rowan at the gate.

Not inside it. Outside, in the dark, standing in the road with a lantern, beside Old Grey. The great dray-beast stood with his head toward the shut gate and his rope in the dust, and Rowan stood by his shoulder, small and neat and upright, looking at the gate too, as if the two of them were waiting for it to open.

She didn't turn round when Maren came up. She'd have heard the lion. She didn't turn round.

"Captain."

"Ashdown." Rowan looked at the gate. "The council's men are in the guardhouse. Twelve of them, and Pryne. They've taken the north tower's spare room. They brought their own wine." Her voice was the same as it always was, the stable rota. "They are the council's men, with the council's writ. My knights are not at their disposal, and I have told Pryne so, and he has written to Hallowmere about it."

Maren waited.

"I had the inner wall surveyed this morning," Rowan said. "Before they came. The split. The council wants to know whether the wall will stand, and whether it's worth the cost of mending, and whether anything could have got in or out through it in the night of the gate." She held the lantern up and looked at the gate's iron studs, as if she were counting them. "It's not a hand's width at its widest. Nobody could get through it. I've written that in my report." She paused. "Nobody at all."

Maren stood in the road and understood her.

Rowan turned round at last. In the lantern light her face was tired and very still, and she looked at Maren for a long moment, and then past her at the lion, standing in the dark at the edge of the light, and then back.

"The granary," Rowan said, "backs on that wall. As you know. You've hauled oats out of it."

"Yes, Captain."

"Good night, Ashdown." She put her hand on Old Grey's shoulder, once, briefly, as you'd touch a horse in a stall you were passing. "He'll stand here all night, I think. They'll not shift him. Odd beast."

She walked back along the wall toward the postern with the lantern, small and upright, and didn't look back.

---

Odo told her where.

They held a council in Liss's kitchen with the door shut and the shutters closed and Liss's old lamp turned low on the table: Maren and Liss and Tam, and Odo in his chair by the fire with the blanket over his splinted legs and his big burned hands on the arms of it. Odo listened to all of it without saying anything. Then he said, "Back wall of the granary."

"It's the inner wall."

"It's the inner wall from the yard side. From the river side it's the inner wall with a skin on it." Odo shifted in his chair and swore at his legs. "Twenty years ago. Before your time at the forge, boy. The granary roof came in, one wet winter, and pulled the back wall half down with it. The old wall. Grey stuff. Older than the school. The council wouldn't pay a mason to put the old stone back, so they paid a smith to put iron in it and a gang to face it up with rubble. I did the iron." He looked at Maren. "Three ties, top, middle and bottom, through to the yard side, bolted. The rubble's two feet thick and full of lime and it never cured right. The old stone behind it was cracked then. If it's split top to bottom now, the rubble's all that's holding it, and the rubble was never holding much."

"Rowan says the split's not a hand's width."

"Rowan," said Odo, "is a liar, God love her. I've seen it from the river. You could put your arm in it." He looked at the lion's shape in the dark beyond the open door. "Could he put his shoulder in it?"

Maren thought of the black door at the end of the archive, and the stone round it opening like a mouth as Orrun put his shoulder to the frame. She thought of the passage under the rock, rising, the rock opening ahead of him the way water opens round a swimmer.

"Yes," she said.

---

Tam went first, at the third hour of the night, to the gate, with the handcart.

He'd loaded it with Odo's tools from the forge, the tongs and the swages and a bag of horseshoe nails, and he pushed it up the River Road and up the hill to the gate and banged on it with his fist and shouted for the gate-men, and when they came he told them he was delivering the tools to the school's forge by order of the stable master, and he had to deliver them tonight because the stable master wanted them at first light, and was this Greyhold or wasn't it.

The gate-men told him to come back in the morning.

Tam told them the stable master would have their hides.

The gate-men told him where he could put the stable master.

Tam began to argue.

Maren heard it from the river path below the north wall, a long way off, carried on the still night: her brother's voice, rising and falling, indignant, loud, going on and on, the voice he'd used on her all their childhood when he wanted something and wasn't getting it. She heard the gate-men's voices answering, rougher, and a council man's voice joining in, and Tam's going louder. She heard Old Grey stamp once, outside the gate, as if he'd joined the argument.

Behind her, at the bottom of the water-gate stair, in the dark, Odo sat in his chair under his blanket where Tam and Liss had carried him, with his legs out in front of him and his burned hands on the chair-arms, watching the wall.

"There," said Odo, low. "Up a bit. Where it bellies. That's my middle tie. Go left of it a yard."

---

The inner wall rose above her in the dark, grey stone, enormous, older than anything else on the hill. She could see the split from here, black in the grey, running up it from the river path to the parapet, wider in the middle where it bellied out. A pale skin of rubble and lime over the bottom half of it, cracked through.

Orrun went up to it.

She went with him with her hand on his shoulder. She felt him through the bond the way she'd felt him on the archive stair and in the passage under the rock: going still inside, gathering, listening down into the stone until he and the stone were listening to each other.

The ground under them was dead. She could feel that too, through him: nothing in it, like a cart with the horse gone. But this wasn't the ground. This was the lion and the stone. A bond had never come from the ground, and neither did this, whatever this was. It came from him.

He put his shoulder to the wall a yard left of Odo's middle tie.

The rubble went first. It cracked and slumped and came away in a long slide of lime-dust and broken stone, quietly, like snow off a roof. Then the old stone behind it.

It moved.

Not broke. Moved. The split in it widened, slowly, the two sides of it going apart like the two halves of a heavy door, grinding, the sound so low she felt it in her teeth more than heard it. A breath of air came out through the gap, warm, smelling of oats and dust and people.

It opened as wide as a lion's shoulders, and stopped.

There was a sound inside. A child's breath, sharp. Then a voice, low, old, perfectly steady.

"Well," said Ada Fenn, in the dark. "That's a lion, Bel. Get the children's shoes on."

---

Maren went in.

The granary was black and smelled of old oats. She couldn't see anything. She heard them: the rustle of blankets, a child whimpering, someone getting up. A hand found her arm in the dark, small and very strong.

"You're her," said Wren.

"Yes."

"Is it all right?"

"It's all right."

"Say it twice."

"It's all right," said Maren. "It's all right, Wren."

She brought them out one at a time. Ada first, because Ada said she'd go first, and went through the gap in the old stone sideways with her shawl held close and her hand on the stone, as if she were stepping out of a cart. Then Pip, small and silent, handed out to Odo's shadow at the bottom of the wall. Then Bel with Nan asleep on her shoulder, who stopped in the gap with the baby and looked at the lion standing a yard off in the dark, and said nothing, and went past him. Then Wren.

Wren stopped in the gap too. She put her hand out, without asking, and put it flat on Orrun's shoulder, and left it there for the space of a breath. Then she went past him, down to the river path, and Maren came out last.

Orrun stepped back from the wall. The gap stayed open. He looked at it for a moment, and then he put his head down and the old stone ground together again, slowly, not all the way, a hand's width short, and stopped.

*Not a hand's width,* said Orrun. She felt something in him she'd never felt before and didn't have a name for. It was a long time before she understood that it was amusement.

---

Up at the gate, Tam was still arguing.

At the foot of the water-gate stair, Odo was laughing. Silently. In his chair, under his blanket, with his burned hands over his face and his shoulders shaking.

And at the gate, as Wren went down the river path past the corner of the north wall in the dark, Old Grey lifted his head.

Maren heard him. A long, low sound, out of the great chest, the first sound she'd ever heard him make. Then the clop of his feet on the road, turning away from the gate. Coming round. Along the outside of the walls in the dark, slowly, heavily, his rope dragging, toward the water-gate, toward the river path, toward where the small voice had gone.

---

Liss took them over without a lantern.

She'd done it before. She'd done it in the war, in the thaw, with the Garrows and the Pennicks and half of Harrow's End, on nights with no moon, by feel, on the guide-rope. She did it now. The flat ferry went out from the jetty into the black water with Ada and Bel and the children huddled in the middle of it under a sail-cloth, and Liss in the bow hauling hand over hand on the rope, and no sound but the rope creaking in its blocks and the river going by.

Behind the ferry on long lead-ropes swam the greys. Mabbin, and Sorrel, snorting, their heads up and their eyes white in the dark. And Old Grey, last, swimming the way he walked, slow and enormous and steady, with his head toward the ferry and the small shape under the sail-cloth that was Wren.

Maren swam with Orrun.

She hadn't known whether he could. She found that he could. He did most things by doing them: he walked into the river and the river took him and he swam, his great head up and his mane floating out round it on the black water like weed round a rock, and she held on to the mane and swam beside him, and the current pulled at both of them and couldn't move him.

The cold of the Wend in spring went into her like knives. She'd swum it before, on the river watch, in summer, for a bet. Never in spring. Never at night. She held on to the lion's mane and kicked and thought about nothing except the next kick, and the next.

Halfway, she felt him through the bond. Not words. Something like a hand under her, holding her up.

---

They came ashore at Tolly's Staithe an hour before dawn, among the drying racks, on the stones where the farmer had come ashore with the old dragon three days before.

She lay on the stones and shook.

Liss was tying up the ferry. Ada was getting the children out from under the sail-cloth, Pip and then Nan, who'd slept through the whole of it. Bel was standing on the shingle with her arms round herself, looking back across the water at Greyhold's hill, dark, with no lights on it.

Wren was sitting on the stones beside Maren with Old Grey dripping behind her.

"Where's Papa?"

Maren sat up. Her teeth were going so hard she couldn't speak for a moment.

"Up the Way," she said at last. "Three days up. With the old one. She can't fly."

"So he's slow."

"Yes."

Wren thought about it.

"There's men on the Way," she said. "Abe said. Asking for him by name."

"Yes."

"Will they ask for us?"

Maren looked at her. Nine years old, wet to the waist, with the cold coming off her, and a great grey beast behind her that wouldn't leave her, and a long level look in her eyes that was storing it.

She thought of it then, all of it, the way she'd have thought it through on the river watch with a smuggler's skiff in the fog. The companies on the Drover's Way, unpaid, taking what would sell. A hundred crowns alive for the farmer, and his family worth something to whoever brought them to the crown's men in pieces. An old woman, a widow, three children, three greys, and the whole Drover's Way between here and Varrhold. Lisska couldn't carry five people. The farmer was three days up the Way with a dragon on three legs.

Only a lion was escort enough.

And the thing had gone north-east, into the sunrise. Whatever road she was going to take after this, it started north-east too.

"Not if I'm with you," she said.

Wren looked at her for a long moment.

"Say it twice."

"Not if I'm with you, Wren."

Wren nodded, as if that settled it, and leaned back against Old Grey's wet foreleg, and shut her eyes.

As far as the ledges, Maren thought. As far as the Scarfell's edge, where his own people could take them. And no farther.

---

The green dragon found them at dawn on the tenth day.

They were in the edge of the Fellwood above the Staithe, in a hollow under the oaks off the Drover's Way, where Maren had brought them in the grey before sunrise to rest out of sight of the road. Liss had gone back over the river at first light with the ferry. Ada had a fire going, small, out of dry stuff, a fire for a land where people with crossbows looked for smoke. The children were asleep. The greys were grazing under the trees with Old Grey standing over Wren like a roof.

Maren heard the wings first. Then the cry: thin and high, the cry she'd heard in the cavern under the river, and on the shore in the sunrise. A green shape went over the treetops, low, fast, turned, came back, and dropped into the hollow in a flurry of leaves and wingbeats that sent the greys plunging on their ropes.

It was Lisska. She stood in the hollow with her wings half up, shaking, looking round, at Ada, at the children, at the greys, at Wren under Old Grey's chin, waking, sitting up, staring. Then at Orrun.

The two of them looked at each other across the hollow.

Maren felt it go between them through the bond. Not words, at first. A rush, like a stream going over a weir, all at once and tumbling, and Orrun taking it in the way he took everything, slowly, turning each piece over. Kin-speech. Far kin. She'd felt it in the cavern and on the road. She'd felt it only when the green one was near, within sight; further than that, nothing.

*She went to the ferry house,* said Orrun at last, to Maren. *He sent her back. To look in on the child. Every few days, he said. She found the ferry house empty of them, and the mother on the jetty pointing over the water.* He was quiet. *She has been looking for us since the night.*

"Is he all right?"

Orrun turned the question over and gave it to the green dragon. The green dragon answered it, fast, tumbling.

*The old one has fallen,* said Orrun. *Twice. On the Way. Once on a hill above the burned village, and once on the road by a stream. She got up both times.* Another rush from Lisska. *He is at the turn for Ewe Hollow. Three days, for what a horse does in one.* A pause. *There are men on the road behind him.*

Wren was standing up under Old Grey, looking at the green dragon.

"Is she telling you about Papa?" she said to Maren.

"Yes."

"What's she saying?"

Maren looked at the green dragon, standing in the hollow with her wings half up and the dirty blue ribbon on her horn.

"She says your grandmother's to tell her you're all here," said Maren. "She says she'll take it back up the Way. To him."

Wren looked at Lisska.

Then she walked across the hollow, past Orrun, past the fire, to the green dragon, and put her hand up on the green neck, and said, very clearly, as if to someone a long way off:

"We're all here. We're all here, Lisska. Tell him. Twice."
