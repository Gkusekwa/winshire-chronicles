---

On the Saltreach bank, the ground had a pulse.

He felt it the first morning, walking up from the Staithe with his hand on the old one's jaw. Not in his boots. Under them. So faint he thought at first it was his own heart, and stood still on the track to be sure, and the old dragon stood still beside him because he had, and the red one under her shoulder stood still because she had. It was there. Something under the ground, faint, slow, like a river felt through the planks of a bridge without hearing it.

On the other side, for six days, there'd been nothing. He hadn't known there was nothing until now, when there was something.

*You feel it,* said Aurveth.

"A bit."

*It is thin.* She put her great head down toward the track as she walked, three steps and a stop, as if she were listening to it too. *Thinner than it was. Everything here is thinner than it was. But it is there.*

"Will it be enough? For the setters?"

She didn't answer. She took three steps and stopped.

---

The track from the Staithe went north along the bank, a cart-road between hedges, rutted and empty, and for the first day they met no one on it. He'd thought there'd be people. Refugees going back, farmers coming to look at what was left. There was no one. The hedges went by. Lisska flew ahead and came back and flew ahead. Behind them, every time he looked back, Greyhold's hill stood across the river, dark, getting smaller.

He didn't look back after the first afternoon.

They lay up that night in a field below the track with the dragons round him in a ring, the old one in the middle, the way they'd lain on the shingle. He slept against her side. She didn't sleep. He woke in the night and her one gold eye was open, looking north, at the dark line of the hills where the Scarfell began, where Varrhold was.

---

The deserters were at the Ewe Hollow turn.

He saw the sheep first, in the morning: a long dirty river of them coming down the track from the hills on the right, where the turn went off east to Ewe Hollow in its fold, a few hundred, bleating, with dogs running at the edges. Then the men behind them. Sixteen. He counted them like a flock. Men in pieces of three different companies' colours, a yellow tower on blue, a red hand, Delling's grey and black, with packs and crossbows and the look men get after a week of taking what isn't theirs. They were driving Ewe Hollow's sheep down to sell at Cotter's Cross.

They saw him at the same moment.

The sheep went on past, round the dragons, wide, bleating. The men stopped.

He watched them see it. A big man on foot with his hand on the jaw of a bronze dragon that stood on three legs with one wing dragging. A red dragon under her. A green one dropping out of the sky to land behind them on the track, wings up, hissing. He watched them count dragons, and count their own crossbows, and count again. He watched the moment they understood that the old one couldn't fly.

"That's him," said one of them. A thin man with a red hand on his sleeve. "That's the farmer. Hundred crowns. Alive."

"Alive with that?"

"She can't fly. Look at her. She's done."

*Calder,* said Kharrak.

"I know."

*Let me.*

"No."

---

The thin man with the red hand put his crossbow up and loosed at the old one.

It was so quick Calder didn't see it go. He heard it: a flat clack and a whirr, and then a sound like a hammer on a door, and the old dragon's head went back. The bolt was standing in her neck. In the plates behind the jaw, a hand's width from his own hand. Stuck there, quivering, with its fletching shaking.

It hadn't gone through. Her plates were old and thick as roof-slates. But it had gone in.

He felt it go into her. Through the bond. A small hard pain in his own neck, like a wasp. And then, under it, behind him, the red tide.

He'd felt Kharrak's red tide before. On the night of the field, on the night of the fort, on the spit with Rook. It came up from somewhere at the bottom of the red dragon and filled him to the top, fast, hot, a flood filling a lane, and when it was up there was nothing in Kharrak but the tide and the thing it was going to burn. It was coming up now. He felt Kharrak's legs go stiff under the old one's shoulder. He felt the breath go into the great chest, deep, the breath that came before the fire.

He let go of the old one's jaw and stepped out in front of the red dragon.

Into the line of it. Between Kharrak's mouth and the sixteen men. He stood there with his back to the men and his face to the dragon, close enough to feel the heat coming off the red scales, and put both his hands up flat in front of him the way you put your hands up to a bull, and held on.

Not with his hands. Through the bond. He held on to the red tide the way he'd held on to Lisska on the night of the honey-smoke when she was coming loose: he took hold of it from inside, all of it, and held it, and it burned him. It went through him like a fever. He felt his own face go hot and his eyes water.

"No," he said. Out loud. "Kharrak. No."

*She's hurt. They hurt her. They'll do it again.*

"I know."

*Let me.*

"No. Look at me. Look at me, not them."

The red dragon looked at him. The great gold eyes, a hand's width from his face. The breath held in the chest, the fire behind it.

"We said," said Calder. "In the yard. Whatever they say, you lie still. This isn't the yard. It's the same."

The red tide stood at the top of the red dragon and shook.

Behind him, he heard a crossbow being wound.

---

"Leave it."

A voice from the back of the sixteen. A coast voice, Wendmouth, flat and tired.

"Leave it, Pasco. Put it up."

"It's a hundred crowns."

"It's a hundred crowns you'll not live to spend." Feet on the track, coming through the sheep. "Put it up, I said."

Calder didn't turn round. He couldn't. He had the red tide in both hands and if he took his eyes off Kharrak's it would get away from him. But he knew the voice.

"I know that man," said the voice. Closer now. "I was in his barn at Upper Rush, in the winter, with that red bastard's claws round my middle. He had my legs. He could've had them off." The voice stopped a few paces behind him. "He let me walk. Gave me his word and kept it, with his dragon standing over me wanting to do the other thing. Same as now."

"So?"

"So he'll let you walk," said Coll Brask. "He'll let all of you walk, now, today, with your skins on. If you put that up." A pause. "He'll not let you shoot the old one twice. Look at him. Look at what he's holding. You think that's a man stood in front of a dragon? That's a man stood in front of a fire with his arms out. Put it up, Pasco, or I'll put it up for you."

There was a silence, long, full of sheep.

Then the clack of a crossbow being let down, uncocked, and the scrape of it going back on a shoulder.

---

The red tide went down.

Not all at once. A little, and then a little more, like a flood going back off a field: slowly, leaving wrack. Calder held on until he felt it go below the top of the red dragon, and then below the chest, and then the great breath went out of Kharrak in a long hot sigh that smelled of smoke and lifted Calder's hair, and the red dragon put his head down and turned it away.

*For you,* said Kharrak. Thick. *Not for them.*

"I know."

He turned round. His legs nearly went. He put his hand back on the old one's jaw to hold himself up, and she held still for him, with the bolt standing in her neck.

The sixteen were going. South, down the track, away from him. Not fast, and looking back. They'd left the sheep. The sheep stood about on the track and in the ditches, bleating, and the dogs ran round them not knowing what to do.

One man had stayed.

Coll Brask. He was the same as in the winter: a lean, weathered coast-man with a broken nose and a sellsword's coat gone shiny at the elbows, Delling's grey and black, and a face that had decided long ago that the world owed it nothing and was mostly right. He stood on the track a few paces off with his crossbow on his shoulder, looking at the dragons.

"She all right?"

Calder looked at the bolt. He took hold of it, close to the plates, and the old one held still, and he pulled it out. It came hard. There was blood on the head of it, not much. Dark.

*It is nothing,* said Aurveth.

"She says it's nothing."

"They always say that," said Brask. "Horses. Men. Dragons, I suppose." He looked at the bolt in Calder's hand. "That's a warranted bolt, that. See the tin on the fletching? Pasco bought a charm-tag off the board at the Ram's Head before the war. Flies straight, they said. Over here it does, a bit." He spat. "Over there it does nothing. Everything over there does nothing, now. You heard?"

"I was there."

"Course you were." Brask looked at him. "Hundred crowns. Did you know?"

"I heard it read."

"There's more than us on the Way. More than sixteen. They'll not all have been in your barn."

"I know."

---

Brask didn't leave.

He stood on the track with the sheep going round his legs and seemed to be making up his mind about something, like a man at a ford he doesn't know.

"Nobody paid us," he said at last. "You know that? Five thousand on that hill, and nobody paid one of us. The paymaster's dead. Your red one did for him, they say, on the spit. And the one who paid him went up a stair under the island and never came down. And the one who paid him," he jerked his head south, toward the coast, toward Wendmouth and the money, "nobody knows who that was. So we're paying ourselves." He looked at the sheep. "With sheep. Ewe Hollow's sheep. Five silver a head at Cotter's Cross, if you can find a buyer, which you can't, because every company on the Way's driving sheep down to Cotter's Cross to sell."

Calder said nothing. The old one breathed beside him.

"Delling's got the chest," said Brask.

Calder looked up.

"The paymaster's chest. Off the Ram's Head. The morning after the thing flew, while everybody else was sat on the hill with their mouths open, Delling went up to the inn with twenty of us and took the paymaster's chest out of the back room, where it had been all winter under guard, and put it on a cart, and went south." Brask shrugged. "There's not much silver in it. Not after a winter. But there's the book."

"Rook's book."

"Rook's book. Every bag that went out, and what for, and who to. He wrote it all down. He was that kind." Brask looked at him, flat. "Delling's at Cotter's Cross with it. He's in the back room of the Fleece with twenty men and the chest under his bed, waiting for a buyer."

"A buyer for a book?"

"Delling," said Coll Brask, "sells anything."

He let that sit. Then he hitched his crossbow up on his shoulder.

"That's the last of what I owe you," he said. "My legs. That's the last of it. I'll not come with you, and don't ask." He started off south down the track after the others. Then he stopped and looked back. "Get her off the Way, farmer. Get her up on her ledges before the next lot find you. Not everybody on this road was in your barn."

He went off down the track through the sheep.

---

The old one fell in the afternoon.

They were on the long hill above Harrow's End, where the bank track climbed out of the river-flats to come over the shoulder of the land and down into the village. He'd known this hill his whole life. He'd driven carts up it to the Staithe every autumn with barley. He knew the place where the ruts went deep and the place where the spring came across the track and made it slick, and he was thinking about the spring, and about Delling in the back room of the Fleece with a chest under his bed, and he wasn't watching her feet.

She went down all at once. The front legs went, both of them, and she came down onto her chest on the track with a sound like a barn roof coming in, and the torn wing went out flat on the ground beside her, and Kharrak, under her shoulder, went down with her, sideways, into the hedge.

Calder was on his knees at her head before he'd thought.

Her eye was shut. Her breath was going in and out of her in long rough heaves. Through the bond he felt her, the old slow fire of her, and under it, very faint, something he'd never felt in her before: not pain. A tiredness. Old. Three hundred years of tiredness, all at once, as if she'd put down something she'd been carrying and found she couldn't pick it up again.

"Up," he said. "Come on. Up."

She didn't move.

"Aurveth."

The eye opened. Gold. Looking past him, down the hill.

He looked where she was looking.

---

Harrow's End lay below them in the afternoon light.

He'd seen it from up here a thousand times. Thirty-one houses and a mill on Hollow Beck and a shrine at the crossroads, the barley fields running down to the river, the Eelweir at the water's edge with its huts and its racks. Smoke from the chimneys. Hens. Somebody shouting at a dog.

There was no smoke.

There were houses. Most of them were standing; the companies hadn't burned it this time, Abe had said, only stripped it. He could see from up here what stripped meant. Doors gone. Half the houses stood with black square holes in their fronts where the doors had been, like faces with their eyes put out. Shutters gone. Thatch pulled off one roof and lying in the lane where somebody had thought there might be something under it. The mill's sluice-gate was open and the wheel was still, and there was a pale ring on the ground in the mill-yard where the millstones had stood, and no millstones.

No hens. No dog. Nobody shouting.

The fields were green. That was the worst of it. The barley was up, the winter barley he'd sown in the autumn before any of it began, a hand high, green and even, all the way down to the river, with nobody to cut it.

*That is your village,* said the old dragon.

"Yes."

*I have flown over it many times. With Sigrun. At the harvest, collecting the dues.* She was quiet. *I never once looked at it.*

"Nobody does. It's barley."

She looked at it a long time. Then she put her good foreleg under her, and then the other, and with Kharrak getting his shoulder back under her chest out of the hedge, she stood up.

---

Fisk was in his door.

The Eelweir was the last place in Harrow's End you came to, right down at the water, a cluster of tarred huts on the bank among the eel-traps and the drying racks. Fisk's hut was the end one. He was sitting in the doorway on a stool in the dusk with his pipe, the way Calder had seen him sit every evening of his life, a small, brown, wrinkled old man in a fisherman's smock, looking at the river.

He looked up when the dragons came down the lane between the stripped houses. He looked at them for a long time. Then he took his pipe out of his mouth.

"Calder Fenn," said Fisk.

"Fisk."

"Abe said you'd come this way." Fisk looked at the old one, at the wing, at the bolt-hole in her neck. "Didn't say you'd come walking."

"She can't fly."

"No. I see that." Fisk put his pipe back. "Well. Sit down. There's eel."

There was eel. There was nothing else in Harrow's End to eat, Fisk said, but there was always eel; the companies hadn't wanted it. They didn't know how to cook it. Calder sat on the bank in the dusk with the dragons lying along the shingle below the huts and ate smoked eel off a board on his knee, and it was the best thing he'd eaten in a month.

"They took the doors," said Fisk. "Did Abe say? The doors. Off their hinges. Half the village." He shook his head. "What's anyone want a door for, I said to Abe. He said to sell. I said who buys a door. He said somebody who's lost theirs." Fisk sucked his pipe. "Which, he says, is everybody. So it's a good trade."

"Abe said you won't go over."

"Abe's right."

"Your wife wants you over."

"My wife," said Fisk, "has wanted me over that river for forty years. I've never been yet." He looked at the water, going by in the dusk. "Somebody's got to stay. Somebody's got to have stood here and seen it. To tell it after." He took his pipe out and pointed it at Calder. "You'll want somebody to tell it after, Calder Fenn. Mark me. When it's done. Whatever it is. You'll want somebody who stood here."

---

In the morning he went up to the crossroads.

He didn't mean to. The Drover's Way went north from the crossroads in the middle of Harrow's End, up the valley of Hollow Beck toward the hills, and he had to go through it to get on the Way. He led the old one up the lane past the stripped houses with Kharrak under her and Lisska overhead, and they came out into the little square where the lanes met, and there was the shrine.

He'd passed it every market day of his life. Everybody had. A stone pillar at the crossroads, as high as a man, grey, rough, older than the village. On one side of it there'd been a ledge with a bronze bowl on it, green with age, and every market day you put a copper in the bowl as you went by, for luck, for the road, for the weather. Everybody did. Nobody knew who it was for. The gods. The old ones. Whoever had put a stone at a crossroads before there was a crossroads.

The bowl was gone. The ledge was bare.

He stopped. The old one stopped beside him.

And he saw, for the first time, the carving.

It was on the face of the pillar above the ledge, where the bowl had always stood in front of it. Worn nearly smooth. You had to look sideways at it in the morning light to see it at all. A figure, cut into the stone, not much bigger than a child: a body standing straight, arms at its sides. And a head. And on the head, three faces. One looking forward. One looking left, along the lane to the river. One looking right, up the Drover's Way to the hills. Three faces, looking three ways at once, the way a crossroads does.

He'd stood in front of it a thousand times with a copper in his fingers and never seen it. The bowl had been in front of it. He'd been looking at the bowl.

*Three,* said Lisska, from overhead, delighted. *Calder. It's got three. Like you.*

"It's got three faces. I've got three dragons."

*It's the same.*

"It's not the same."

*It looks the same.*

He stood there a while. The old one was looking at it too, with her one gold eye. Through the bond he felt something in her he couldn't read: not a memory, nothing as solid as a memory. The shape of a memory. Like walking into a room you'd been in as a child and couldn't remember.

He felt in his pocket. He had three coppers left, and the six-knife, and the iron ring.

He took out a copper and put it on the bare ledge, where the bowl had been, in front of the three faces. Out of habit. Because he'd done it every market day of his life, and his father before him, and it was a road he was going on.

"For the road," he said, which was what you said.

Then he took the old one's jaw in his hand, and they went up the Drover's Way.

---

The Way went up the valley of Hollow Beck between the hills, a broad drove-road beaten down by a thousand years of beasts going to market, with the beck on the right and the barley giving out to rough pasture and then to bracken and gorse. He knew every league of it. He'd walked it with grain carts every autumn to Varrhold with the dues.

It wasn't the Way he knew.

There were things on it. A cart on its side in the ditch with its wheels gone. A dead horse, three days dead, that the crows had been at. A man's boot, on its own, in the middle of the road. A long trail of feathers where somebody had plucked hens as they walked. Cold fires at the roadside every quarter-league, black rings in the grass with bones in them. And twice, far off, up the valley ahead, the glint of metal and a knot of men on the road, who saw what was coming toward them and got off the Way into the hills before he reached them, and watched him go by from the bracken.

Nobody shot at him again. He thought about Brask. *Not everybody on this road was in your barn.*

He walked with his hand on the old one's jaw and his eyes on her feet.

---

She fell the second time on the ninth day, by the Skell Water, below the Ram's Head.

It was where the beck came down off the hills on the left and went under the Way through a stone culvert, and the road dipped to it and rose again, and the dip was soft. She put her bad side down into it and the leg went, and she went down onto her shoulder in the wet, and lay there with the water going round her.

It took them an hour to get her up. Kharrak got his shoulder under her and pushed, and Calder pulled on her jaw, and Lisska ran round them crying and getting in the way, and in the end it was none of them; it was the old one herself, who lay in the beck with her eye shut until Calder had stopped pulling, and then said, quite calmly, *Stand back,* and got up on her own, slowly, one leg at a time, the way an old woman gets up from her knees after prayers.

She stood in the road with the water running off her and her whole body shaking.

*Twice,* said Lisska, very small. *That's twice. She fell twice.*

"I know."

*Wren said every few days. You said. You said it twice.*

He looked at the green dragon. She was standing in the road with her wings half up and the dirty blue ribbon on her horn, and she was frightened, he could feel it, frightened for the old one and frightened of the road and frightened of the men in the bracken, and she wanted Wren. She'd wanted Wren every hour since the ferry house. He'd felt it the whole way.

"Go on, then," he said.

*Now?*

"Now. Go and see her. Tell her we're all right." He looked at the old one, shaking in the road. "Tell her we're nearly there."

*We're not nearly there.*

"Tell her anyway."

The green dragon looked at him. Then at the old one. Then she went up, fast, with a crack of wings that sent the crows off the dead horse up the road, and turned south, and was a green speck over the valley, and gone.

---

The Ram's Head had been sacked.

He'd known it would be. It was the drovers' inn halfway between Harrow's End and Varrhold, where the Way crossed the road to the river, and all winter it had been the Restoration's camp, a town of tents round it and the companies' lines up the hill behind it. He'd come down to it in the winter in a carter's coat to look, and seen the paymaster's room and the charm-board and the long lines of bound beasts. Now the tents were gone, and the beasts, and the men. The inn stood on its crossroads with its door kicked in and its windows out. Barrels lay stove in the yard. The sign, a ram's head painted on a board, hung by one chain from its iron bracket and swung in the wind, creaking, the ram upside down.

The charm-board was in the mud of the yard. A board as long as a door, painted with the Tide Council's anchor and the words WARRANTED CHARMS, that had stood by the inn door all winter hung with little tin tags on nails: against bolts, against fever, against bad luck at dice, a silver each. Somebody had kicked it over. The tags were gone, all but one, still on its nail, bent.

He looked at it a long time, and didn't pick it up.

They lay up that night behind the inn, out of the wind, in what had been the paymaster's yard.

---

Lisska came back at dusk on the tenth day.

He heard her a long way off. She came up the valley low and fast, crying, and he felt her before he saw her, as he always felt her within a few leagues: a bright frightened rush in the back of his head that got louder as she came. She came down in the yard behind the Ram's Head in a flurry of wings and stood there with her sides heaving.

*They're gone,* she said. *Calder. They're gone. They're not there.*

He was on his feet.

*The ferry house. I went to the ferry house. And they weren't there. Not Wren, not Ada, not Bel, not the little ones, not the greys. Only the mother, on the jetty. And she pointed. Over the river. She pointed over the river and said something and pointed again.*

"Over the river."

*So I went over. And I looked. All night. All along the bank. In the woods.* The green dragon was shaking. *And I found them. Calder, I found them. In the Fellwood, above the Staithe, in a hollow. Ada and Bel and the little ones and Wren and the greys and Old Grey.* She stopped. *And the lion.*

He stood very still.

*The lion-woman,* said Lisska. *With the lion. She brought them over. In the night. All of them. She's bringing them up the Way.* Her voice went very small. *Wren said to tell you. We're all here. She said it twice. She made me.*

He sat down on the cold ground of the paymaster's yard.

He thought: they were at the ferry house. They were safe at the ferry house. With Liss, with the river between them and the Way. That was the whole point. That was why he'd left them. And now they were on this side, on the Way, on this road with the dead horse and the boot and the cold fires and the men watching from the bracken, and sixteen with crossbows at the Ewe Hollow turn who'd shoot an old dragon for a hundred crowns, and Delling with his chest at Cotter's Cross who sold anything. His mother. His sister. Three children. Wren.

On the Drover's Way.

He didn't know why. Lisska didn't know why. Lisska hadn't been able to ask; she couldn't speak to anyone over there but the lion, and the lion talked slow, and she'd only understood that they were coming. He didn't know what had happened at the ferry house. He didn't know why the woman with the lion, who had a road going the other way, who had said *so's yours* at the landing and not looked up when he went, had taken his family over the river in the night and was walking them up the one road in the Marches he'd have given anything to keep them off.

*Is it bad?* said Lisska. *Calder. Is it bad that they're coming?*

He looked south down the Way, into the dusk, where it went down the valley toward Harrow's End and the river, empty, with the cold fires along it.

"I don't know," he said.

It frightened him more than the crossbow had. More than the red tide. More than the old one going down in the road. He sat in the paymaster's yard with his back against the stove barrels and his hands between his knees, and the old dragon lying behind him in the dark, and he was more frightened than he'd been since the night under the beam in the Ruin's tower.

Then he thought of the lion.

He thought of it coming up out of the gallery under the river into the light of Kharrak's fire, the size of a dray-beast and grey as old stone, and stopping ten paces off. He thought of the stone moving for it. He thought of sixteen men at the Ewe Hollow turn seeing that come up the track at them out of the morning.

"Maybe not," he said, to Lisska, to the dark. "Maybe not bad."

*Say it twice,* said Lisska.

He nearly laughed.

"Maybe not bad," said Calder Fenn. "Maybe not."
