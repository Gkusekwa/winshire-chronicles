---

Maren took them off the Drover's Way on the first morning, and kept them off it.

She'd looked at it from the edge of the Fellwood above the Staithe in the grey before sunrise, lying in the bracken with Orrun beside her, while Ada and Bel got the children up in the hollow behind. The Way ran north up the valley of Hollow Beck below her, broad and pale, a drove-road worn down by a thousand years of beasts going to market. On a morning in spring it should have been empty. There were three cold fires on it in the first half-league she could see, and smoke from a fourth further up, and a knot of men moving north on it with something on a cart, and another knot coming south.

"No," she said.

*No,* Orrun agreed.

So they went through the Fellwood.

She knew the southern edge of it. On the river watch they'd come ashore below the Staithe sometimes, against orders, to look for poachers' landings in the trees, and once she'd followed a trail of blood four leagues north into the oaks after a wounded boar until the light went and she had to come back by the stars. There were old tracks through it. Charcoal-burners' tracks, from before anyone burned charcoal in the Fellwood any more, half grown over, running north between the trees parallel to the Way, two leagues east of it. Wide enough for a cart, once. Wide enough for three greys and a lion.

---

They went slowly. They had to.

Mabbin, the old grey, carried the children by turns, two at a time, Pip and Nan clinging to her mane, or Nan alone in front of Bel. Sorrel, the young one, carried the packs, and shied at everything, and had to be led. Ada walked. Ada walked at the head of the line with a stick she'd cut from a hazel on the first morning and wouldn't be helped, and when Bel tried to make her ride she looked at Bel the way Maren's mother looked at a river that was rising, and Bel stopped trying. Bel carried Nan when Nan wasn't on the grey, and didn't talk.

And Wren walked beside Old Grey.

Or Old Grey walked beside Wren. Maren could never tell which. Where Wren went, the great grey dray-beast went, a pace behind her and a pace to the side, with his rope hanging from his headstall and trailing in the leaves, and his empty eyes on nothing, and his great feet coming down beside her small ones. When Wren stopped, he stopped. When she ran ahead to look at something, he came after her at his slow enormous walk and stood over her while she looked. When she sat down, he stood.

"Does he know he's doing it?" Maren said to Ada, on the second day.

Ada looked at the beast and the girl for a long time, walking ahead of them down the green track under the oaks.

"I don't know what he knows," said Ada. "I don't think he knows his own name. I think he knows hers." She walked on. "I think that's all he's got, just now. One name. He's holding on to it."

---

On the second day they came to the place where it had flown.

Maren felt it before she saw it. Through Orrun. They'd been going north along the charcoal track under the oaks all morning, and the ground under them had been what it had been since the Staithe: thin, faint, like the pulse in the wrist of someone asleep. She'd got used to it. It was better than the other bank. Then, toward noon, it went thinner. Not dead. Thinner, as if something had drunk from it and not finished.

Orrun stopped.

The track came out of the trees.

Ahead of them there was no forest. There was a long open country going away north-east as far as she could see, grey and brown, under the sky. A league across. Perhaps more. And in it, every tree was lying down.

All of them. Every oak and ash and birch in that league-wide strip of the Fellwood lay flat on the ground, roots torn up and standing in the air like the hands of drowned men, crowns pointing the same way. North-east. Every one of them pointing north-east, laid down in rows as if a giant had walked through the forest with a scythe, or the wind of something going over had pressed them flat the way hail presses barley. The torn roots were still raw. The leaves on the fallen crowns were still green. They'd come down twelve days ago, and there was nobody to clear them, and there never would be.

Nothing moved in it. No birds. She listened, and the whole league-wide strip of fallen forest lay there in the noon sun in a silence like the inside of a shut box.

Behind her she heard Ada stop. Then Bel. Then nothing, for a long time.

"It flew over here," said Maren.

"Low," said Ada.

"Low enough to touch."

*It drank,* said Orrun.

She looked at him. He had his head down. Not to the ground. To the strip of fallen forest, as if he were smelling it, or listening to it, the way he'd listened on the ledge above the black water.

*As it went over. It was drinking. From the ground under the trees.* He was quiet. *It was thirsty. Three hundred years in the chains, and it came up out of the river thirsty, and it drank as it went. And the trees were standing on what it drank.*

She thought of the surgeon on the bank at Redwatch, with his red hands. *It's dead ground.* And this wasn't dead. This was drunk from. Like a field after a flood had come over it and gone back.

"Can we cross it?"

*Yes.*

"Will it hurt us?"

He lifted his head and looked at her with his pale eyes.

*No,* said Orrun. *It is gone. It is a long way north-east now, and sitting down.*

She didn't ask him how he knew that. She thought she knew. She'd felt it herself, very faintly, through him, for days: a pull. Away to the north-east, somewhere beyond the edge of the world, like the pull of a current on a boat you couldn't see. His kin. Sitting down somewhere. Drinking.

---

It took them half a day to cross a league.

They climbed. There was no going round; the strip went away to the north-east and south-west as far as she could see, and the only way over it was over it. They climbed over the fallen trunks one by one, the children handed up and handed down, Ada with her hazel stick, Bel with Nan on her hip and her mouth shut tight. The greys picked their way, slow, snorting, stepping high. Sorrel balked at every trunk and had to be coaxed. Mabbin went over like the old grey she was, patient, as if she'd been climbing over fallen forests all her life.

Old Grey went over them as if they weren't there. Where Wren went, he went.

And Orrun went first. Ahead of all of them, picking the way. Where the trunks lay too thick, too high, too tangled, he went up to them and put his shoulder to them and the ground under them moved, slowly, the earth shifting and settling, and a gap opened between two trunks, or a root-plate tipped back and lay down, and he walked through, and they came after.

He walked the whole league with his head down.

She felt it in him through the bond. Not grief, now. Something under grief. He was walking the road his kin had taken, through the forest it had flattened, over the ground it had drunk from going by. He was the only one of his kind for a thousand leagues, and here was the shape of the other one, laid out on the land like a footprint.

Once, in the middle of the strip, he stopped by a great oak that lay with its roots up, and put his nose to the raw earth in the root-hole, and stood there a long time.

*It smells of the river,* he said. *Of the cavern. Of the chains.* A pause. *Of me.*

She put her hand on his shoulder and didn't say anything.

---

They came out of the far side at dusk into standing trees again, and made camp in the first hollow they found, and that night, from the north-east, the dragons cried.

She heard it first as nothing: a thinness in the air before it was a sound. Then Orrun lifted his head off his paws. Then she heard it. A long way off. Very faint, on the night wind that was coming down off the hills: high, thin, many-voiced, like geese going over in the dark, but not geese. Rising and falling. Going on.

Ada was sitting by the fire, which she'd lit with a word.

She'd done it out of habit, the first night on this bank, as she did every night of her life: put two fingers to the kindling and said the hearth-word over it. On the other bank, in Liss's kitchen, it had done nothing. Here, the kindling had smoked, after a while. Faintly. Then caught. Ada had looked at it and said nothing and fed it twigs. She did it again tonight. It caught a little sooner.

Now she lifted her head too, and listened.

"What is it?" said Maren.

Ada listened a while longer. The thin high sound came and went on the wind.

"The Roosts," she said. "That's dragons. That's every dragon on the ledges at Varrhold, all crying at once." She was quiet. "They did that at the old chief's burning. And at the ford. When the old woman's man went into the water. All of them, together, all night."

Maren went still.

"No," said Ada. "Not that. Listen to it."

Maren listened. She didn't know what she was listening for.

"It goes up at the end," said Ada. "Hear it? The other times it went down. This goes up." She sat back from the fire. "She's reached the hold. The old one. He's got her there." She looked at the fire a long time. "That's them saying so."

Across the fire, Wren was sitting up in her blanket, with Old Grey standing over her in the dark, listening too.

"Papa's got her there," Wren said.

"Yes," said Ada.

"Say it twice, Grandma."

"He's got her there, Wren," said Ada Fenn. "He's got her there."

---

On the third day they met the bear.

It was in a clearing in the Fellwood where a beck came down over stones, and they came out of the trees into the clearing at noon and it was there, at the water, drinking. A Fellwood bear. Maren had heard about them all her life and never seen one. Taller than a house's eaves when it stood, the stories said. Brown-black, shaggy, with a hump of muscle over the shoulders like a hayrick and a head the size of a cauldron.

It stood.

It was taller than a house's eaves. She'd thought the stories were stories.

The greys stopped dead. Sorrel went back on her haunches and nearly threw the packs. Bel grabbed Nan. Pip, on Mabbin's back, began to cry, a thin high frightened sound. Ada stood where she was with her hazel stick in her hand, quite still.

The bear looked at them. Its small eyes went from the greys to the people to the children. It opened its mouth and its breath came out in a cloud in the cool air.

Then it saw Orrun.

He hadn't done anything. He'd come out of the trees behind Maren and stopped, and stood there, grey as old stone, with his head up, looking at the bear. That was all. He didn't roar. He didn't move.

The bear went down onto all fours. Slowly. It looked at the lion for a long moment with its small eyes, the way a man looks at someone across a crowded room whom he's heard of and didn't expect to see. Then it turned round, unhurried, and walked away across the beck and into the trees on the far side without a sound, and was gone.

Pip was still crying.

Wren hadn't moved at all. She was standing by Old Grey's foreleg with her hand on it.

"It knew him," said Wren. "Didn't it. It knew who he was."

Maren looked at Orrun.

*It knew what I was,* he said. *Not who.* He walked forward into the clearing to the water and drank, where the bear had drunk. *There is a difference.*

---

The deserters had been following them for a day.

She'd known since the morning of the third day. On the river watch you learned to know when you were being followed, as you learned weather: a feeling at the back of the neck, a bird going up off the track behind you that you hadn't put up. She'd gone back alone in the afternoon, a quarter-league, and lain in the bracken above the track, and seen them. Five men. Coming along the charcoal track behind them in the trees, not fast, keeping their distance. Bits of two companies' colours. Crossbows.

They'd seen the children on the Way, she thought. Or heard. A widow and an old woman and three children and three greys on the Saltreach bank, going north, with a hundred crowns on the father's head and the crown's men at Greyhold wanting them back.

They weren't following a lion. They'd have seen the lion and gone home. They were following a reward, and keeping well back from the lion, and waiting.

She'd told Orrun.

*I know,* he'd said. *I have smelled them since yesterday.*

"They want the children."

*Yes.*

"If they come at us," she'd said, "I'll talk to them first. I'll talk to them, and if they won't go, we'll put them down. Not kill them. Put them down, take their bows, send them back. Pell taught it to us every day for two years. Subdue. Don't kill." She'd looked at him. "Unless you have to."

He'd looked at her for a long time with his pale eyes and said nothing.

---

They came at the beck-crossing on the fourth day.

A narrow place, where the charcoal track went down into a cut in the woods and crossed a beck on stepping-stones and went up the far side, with the trees close on both banks. She'd have chosen it herself. She saw the place and felt the back of her neck go cold and put up her hand, and the line behind her stopped.

"Stay here," she said to Ada. "All of you. By the greys. Keep the children down."

She went forward alone, down into the cut, to the edge of the beck. Orrun came behind her. She'd told him to stay back and he hadn't.

There were two of them on the far bank, in the trees, half hidden. Two crossbows. She saw the other three a little further up, on her side, in the bracken, waiting for the line to come down into the cut after her.

"I see you," she said. Loud. River-watch voice. "All five. I see you. You'll not get them. Go home."

"There's a hundred crowns on the father." A voice from the far bank. Young. Nervous. "The crown's men at the fort'll pay for the brats as well. Ten a head, they say. Nobody needs to get hurt."

"Nobody will, if you go home."

"That's a lot of crowns, girl."

"That's a lion," said Maren.

The young voice laughed, high and frightened.

Then the other man on the far bank stood up out of the trees with his crossbow at his shoulder and loosed at her.

---

She didn't see it go. She heard the clack. She was already moving, sideways, the way Pell had taught a thousand times at the straw men, and something went past her ear with a sound like a torn sheet, and hit a tree behind her.

And Orrun went past her.

She didn't see him go either. She felt him through the bond the instant before he went, a great gathering, like a wave drawing back down a beach before it comes in, and then he wasn't behind her. He was over the beck. He went over the stepping-stones and the water in one bound, grey, enormous, and he was in the trees on the far bank, and she heard a sound that she would hear for the rest of the year in the dark when she couldn't sleep, a short sound, two short sounds, and then nothing.

Then a crashing in the bracken on her own bank, behind her, three men running. Away. Up the slope, back along the track, through the trees, as fast as men can run.

She stood at the edge of the beck with her sword half out of its scabbard. Haverly's sword. She didn't remember starting to draw it.

On the far bank, Orrun came out of the trees.

There was blood on his muzzle and on his forelegs. Not his. He came down to the beck and stood in the water up to his knees and put his head down and drank, and the water went pink round him and the pink went away downstream.

She went across the stones to the far bank and into the trees, and looked, and came back.

---

She didn't say anything until the camp was made, and the fire was lit, and the children were asleep.

She got through the afternoon. She got them all across the beck and up the far side and a league on, away from the place, without letting Wren or Pip or Nan go near the trees on the far bank. Ada knew. Ada looked at the lion's forelegs and at Maren's face and took the children by the hands and walked them up the track and didn't look back, and didn't ask. Bel knew. Bel walked past the place with Nan's face pressed into her shoulder and her own face white.

Wren knew. Wren looked at the trees and then at the lion and then at Maren, and said nothing, and walked on beside Old Grey, storing it.

At dusk Maren made camp in a hollow under an outcrop, and Ada lit the fire with her word, and the children ate and slept. Then Maren got up and walked out of the firelight into the dark under the trees, a little way, where the others wouldn't hear, and stood there.

Orrun came after her. She'd known he would.

---

"You didn't have to," she said.

She said it out loud. She found she wanted to say it out loud, so it was in the air and not only in her head where he could touch it.

*He loosed at you.*

"I know he loosed at me. I moved. He missed. I'd have had him on the ground before he wound again. I'd have had both of them." She heard her voice going up and couldn't stop it. "I'd have had their bows. I'd have sent them back down the track with their hands empty and a story about a lion, and they'd have told every company on the Way, and nobody would have come after us again. That's how it's done. That's how Pell taught it. Subdue. Don't kill."

*Unless you have to.*

"We didn't have to!"

He looked at her in the dark. His eyes caught the firelight from the hollow behind her, two pale points.

*They came for the child,* he said.

"I know what they came for."

*For the child who puts her hand on me. Who said *you're her* at the gate. Who walks with the grey one who has lost himself.* He was very still. *Men with crossbows came for her through the trees, and one of them loosed at you. And you wanted me to stand behind you and wait while you talked.*

"Yes."

*I have waited,* said Orrun. *Three hundred years I waited. Outside a door. While they chained my kin under the river and said words over it every day at noon. I waited, because the old ones told me to keep the door, and I kept it, and they chained it anyway.* The words came slowly, one at a time, like stones going down into deep water. *I will not wait while men with crossbows come for that child.*

She stood in the dark under the trees and felt it come through the bond: not anger. Something older and colder and much more certain than anger. Like the ground itself, refusing to move.

"You are not my hand," she said.

It came out of her before she knew she'd say it. It came from somewhere at the bottom of her, from the yard at Greyhold, from two years of being told what to do with her hands and her sword and her temper and doing it, every time, because that was what a knight was. A hand. You were the hand, and somebody else decided where the hand went, and you went there.

"You don't decide," she said. "Who lives. Not on my road. Not with me. You don't go past me and decide, and come back with blood on your mouth and drink."

*And you,* said Orrun.

He didn't raise his voice. He'd never raised his voice. It came into her head as slow and as deep as it always did, like stones turning in a riverbed.

*You are not my teeth.*

---

They stood there in the dark.

She understood it. That was the worst of it. She understood exactly what he meant. It landed like a blow. Three hundred years at a door under a hill, while men who thought they owned the world's use chained his kin beneath it and bled it for their lamps. Then a collar on his own neck, and bond-forcers' smoke on the knowe. He had been somebody's hand. He had been somebody's teeth. Kept at a door, told to keep it, and they'd come through it anyway with fire and chains. And he'd come out of that into the world and bonded to a knight, and the knight was telling him where to put his teeth.

And he understood her. She felt that too. He understood the yard and Pell and the straw men and *subdue, don't kill*. He understood that she had been made, for two years, into a thing that put men down without killing them, and that she was proud of it, and that it was the best thing she'd been given. He didn't think it was wrong. He thought it was hers.

He didn't think it was his.

Neither of them gave way.

She didn't know what you did, in a bond, when neither of you would give way. Nobody had ever told her. Her father had never quarrelled with his hound in his life, as far as she knew, and if he had, he'd never said. She'd thought, if she'd thought about it at all, that a bond meant you wanted the same things. That was the point. One life between two.

One life. Two minds. She hadn't understood, until now, standing in the dark with the blood still drying on his forelegs, how far apart two minds could be and still be sharing a life.

"I'm going back to the fire," she said.

*Yes.*

She went back to the fire.

---

She lay down on the far side of it from the children, with her back to the outcrop and her sword beside her, and pulled her cloak over her, and shut her eyes.

After a while she heard him come. The soft enormous pad of his feet in the leaves. He came round the fire and lay down behind her. Not against her, as he'd lain every night since the island. Behind her. Back to back. She felt the warmth of him through her cloak, along her spine, a hand's width away.

Neither of them moved closer.

Across the fire, Ada was awake. Maren could see her eyes in the firelight, open, looking at the two of them, the woman and the lion lying back to back in the dark with a hand's width of cold between them.

Ada didn't say anything. She'd been a wife once, Maren thought, and a widow a long time. She'd know what it looked like.

---

On the fifth day they went north along the Fellwood's edge, and the land began to rise.

The oaks gave out to birch and then to rowan and then to open heather, going up in long slopes toward a line of high brown hills under the sky. The Scarfell. The Clan's country. Somewhere up there, beyond the first ridges, Varrhold, and the ledges, and the farmer, and the old dragon who'd reached the hold.

Ada walked beside her in the afternoon.

She didn't say why. She came up from the back of the line with her hazel stick and walked at Maren's shoulder for a quarter-league without speaking. Then she said, looking ahead at the hills:

"His wife was Senna."

Maren looked at her.

"Calder's," said Ada. "Wren's mother. Senna Holt, from over by the Lowe. Black hair. Laughed like a gate swinging." She walked on. "The fever took her. Five years gone, in the wet spring. Took three in Harrow's End that spring and she was the last. He sat with her four days." She was quiet. "He doesn't say her name. Not to Wren, not to me, not if he can help it. I thought you'd want to know it."

"Why?"

Ada looked at her sideways. An old woman's look, dry, measuring.

"Because you're carrying his child up a hill," she said. "And you'll want to know whose child she is."

They walked on.

---

That night Wren came round the fire to Maren.

She came in her blanket and sat down beside Maren with her knees up, and looked across the fire at the lion. He was lying on the other side of the hollow, alone, with his head on his paws, looking at nothing.

"Is he sorry?" said Wren.

Maren looked at the lion.

She reached for him through the bond. Not words. Just reaching, a hand put out in the dark to see if someone was still there. He was there. She felt him. Vast and still and grey, like the ground, and under it, very deep, something that might have been grief, and might have been the old cold certainty that wouldn't move, and might have been both, wound together, the way the roots of the fallen oaks had been wound round the stones they'd torn up.

She didn't know.

"I don't know," she said.

Wren thought about that.

"Are you?"

Maren didn't answer for a long time.

"I don't know that either," she said.

Wren nodded, as if that were a proper answer, and leaned against Maren's side in her blanket, and after a while she was asleep. Maren sat with the child against her and the fire going down, and on the other side of the hollow the lion lay with his eyes open in the dark, and neither of them crossed it.
