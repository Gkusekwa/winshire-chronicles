---

They left the Ram's Head before it was light.

He'd slept sitting up against the stove barrels in the paymaster's yard, if it was sleep, with the old one's breath going in and out behind him in the dark like the sea. When the sky went grey over the moor he got up and went to her and put his hand on her jaw.

*I know,* said Aurveth, before he'd said anything. *Today.*

"Five leagues and a half. Three and a half to the ford. Two more up the glen."

*I know where Varrhold is, farmer.*

She got up. It took a long time. The front legs first, slowly, one and then the other, and her head swinging low, and then the back, with a sound out of her chest that he felt more than heard. Kharrak came round and put his shoulder under hers on the side of the torn wing, as he'd done every morning since the river, and she leaned on him, and the red dragon took it without a word. Lisska went up and sat on the sacked inn's roof-ridge and watched the Way north with her head turned.

The ram's head on the sign swung on its one chain over the door as they went out of the yard.

---

The Way climbed.

Below the Ram's Head it had run along the valley floor beside the Skell Water through farms and copses. Above it, it went up. Not steeply. The drove-road had been made for sheep and cattle and drays, and it went up the side of the glen in long patient slants, turning back on itself at the corners, with the beck going down beside it in a gully full of alder and stones. The farms gave out. The walls gave out. There was bracken and then heather and then the long brown flank of the moor going up on the left hand into cloud.

He walked at her head. He'd walked at her head for five days and he'd got so he could feel, through his hand on her jaw and through the bond, the moment before she was going to stop: a heaviness that came down on her like water filling a boat. When it came he stopped before she did, so she wouldn't have to be the one, and they stood in the road with the wind going over the heather and he talked to her. About nothing. About the barley, which he hadn't sown and wouldn't now. About the price of wool at Cotter's Cross in his father's day. About Wren's ribbon. She didn't answer. After a while the heaviness would lift a little and she'd lean on Kharrak and they'd go on.

There was nobody on the Way above the Ram's Head. The companies hadn't gone up. The Clan's country began at the falls on Hollow Beck, and the Clan had dragons, and the companies had found that out last autumn.

Under his boots the ground was different.

He'd felt it on the Saltreach bank at Tolly's Staithe, the first morning: a thin faint pulse, a long way down, like a heart heard through a wall. It had been with him all the way up the Way, thin, sometimes so thin he lost it, near the burned farms and the stripped villages. Here it was stronger. Not much. As if the wall were a little thinner. He didn't know what it was. He'd never known it was there until there'd been a whole bank of the world without it.

*The Scarfell's old,* said Aurveth, when he asked her. *Older than the river. The Deep runs close under it.* She walked. *That's why the eggs hatch here. Why there are dragons here and nowhere else.* A pause. *Why I came here, before there was a Clan, and sat down.*

---

They came to the ford at noon.

He'd known they would. There was no other way up the glen. The Way went down off its slant to the floor of the glen and crossed Hollow Beck on the smooth brown stones under the alders, and went up the far side, and there was no going round it with a dragon who couldn't fly.

He hadn't been here since the second time. Since Hallam came down the hill with his stick, and the bolt took him, and Morrow lay down.

The alders were in small leaf. The water was high and brown with the snow-melt off the moor and loud over the stones. The shieling on the slope was a black square in the bracken where Ulric's men had burned it in the winter. Somebody had cleared the bodies. Somebody had taken the broken shafts out of the shallows. There was nothing at the ford to show that anything had happened there, twice, except that he knew.

*A ford remembers everyone who crosses it,* the Clan said, *and how heavy they were.* That was why the stones were smooth.

Aurveth went down into the water and stopped in the middle of it.

The beck went round her legs, brown and white. Kharrak stood beside her with his shoulder under hers and the water up to his chest, and waited. Lisska came down off the wind and sat on an alder branch on the far bank and didn't say anything, which for Lisska was a great deal.

Calder stood on the bank with the water at his boots.

He didn't ask her what she was doing. He felt it. Not grief, exactly. Something like grief that had been sitting in her so long it had worn smooth. Teo coming down alone at first light because he wanted to be first. Hallam's stick in the water. Morrow's head on Hallam's chest. And under it, older, the sense of a great many other fords in a great many other years, all the way back, and people coming down to them and not going up again.

*I am remembering,* said Aurveth. *Let me.*

He let her.

After a while she came up out of the water on the far side, and they went on up the glen.

---

They came in sight of the hold at dusk.

The glen opened. The cliffs went up on the right hand, grey and black and streaked with white, with the cave mouths along them in rows like the holes in a sand-martin bank, and the ledges below the caves. The Roosts. Below the cliffs, in its hollow, Varrhold: the turf wall, the ring of black longhouses inside it, the smoke going up from the roof-holes, the yard in the middle where they'd burned the old chief, and then Teo, and the long steep stair cut in the rock with the iron rail on its outside going up from the back of the yard to the ledges.

There were dragons on the ledges. More than there'd been in the winter. Two dozen perhaps, sitting outside the cave mouths in the last of the light with their wings folded, the ones who'd come back from the high glens for the spring.

One of them saw her.

He didn't see it happen. He heard it. A single high cry from the cliff, like a curlew's, but bigger than any curlew. Then another. Then all of them.

Every dragon on the Roosts was crying at once.

He stopped in the road. Kharrak stopped. The sound came down off the cliffs and filled the whole glen, high and thin and many-voiced, rising and falling, going on and on, and it went into him through the bond from all three of them at once so that for a moment he couldn't tell where he stopped and the sound began. He'd heard them do this before. At the old chief's burning. At Teo's. At the ford, the second time, all night on the hill. He'd thought it was the sound dragons made for the dead.

This was not that. He didn't know how he knew. It went up at the end.

"What is it?" he said. "What are they saying?"

Aurveth had lifted her head. Her one gold eye was on the cliff. For the first time in five days, she stood on all four of her legs without leaning on anyone.

*They are saying I came home,* she said.

---

Arn's men wouldn't open the gate.

There were six of them on the turf wall above it with torches, and crossbows, and red thread round their armbands, and they looked down at the farmer from Harrow's End and his three dragons in the road below with their faces shut. Calder knew two of them. One had sold him a ewe once. The other had stood across Teo's pyre from him in this yard in the autumn and not taken his eyes off him all night.

"We're to send for the chief," said the one who'd sold him the ewe. He didn't call Calder anything.

"Then send for him."

"Steward's gone."

So they waited in the road below the gate in the dark, with the old one lying down again in the heather by the track and the cry going on above them on the cliffs, quieter now, here and there, one dragon and then another, like the last of a rain.

The steward came first. Gorm Askell, long and sour and ink-fingered, limping. He'd been hurt at the ford; Calder had seen him go down in the water. He looked over the wall at Calder for a long time and then down at the old one in the heather and said, "Damp," to nobody, and went away again to fetch Arn.

And while they waited, the ledges came down.

---

He saw the lights first, on the stair. One lantern, then another behind it, coming down the long steep stair in the rock below the Roosts, slowly, as old people come down stairs. Then he saw who was carrying them.

Gunna Tarne came first. A small old woman in a grey cloak with a face like a walnut and her white hair in a plait to her waist and no thread in her armband, coming down the stair with the lantern in one hand and the iron rail in the other. Above her, on a ledge, he saw Tamsin's grey-green head come out of a cave mouth into the lantern light and stay there, watching.

Behind Gunna, the others. Old men and women, and three or four of middle years, and one young one, a boy not twenty. Ten of them. The ten pairs of the ledges, who'd sat on the hill above the ford as keepers under the old law and seen Hallam die. They came down the stair one after another, on foot, and across the back of the yard, and the men on the wall drew back from the gate to let them through the postern without being asked, because nobody in the Clan stands in the way of the bonded.

They came out into the road.

They didn't say anything to him. Gunna looked at him once, a short dry look. Then she went past him, to the old one in the heather, and put her hand on Aurveth's jaw, and said something in the old tongue he didn't know, and Aurveth closed her eye.

Then Gunna came back and stood behind him.

Not beside him. Behind him. A pace back and a pace to the side, the way Hallam had come down this stair in the winter and stood, and the others came and stood behind her, ten old riders of the ledges in a ragged line in the road in the dark with their lanterns, and above them on the cliff ten dragons came out of their caves one by one and sat on the ledges with their heads down, looking at the gate.

Nobody said anything.

Calder stood in front of all of them with his hands at his sides and felt the hair go up on the back of his neck.

---

Arn came to the gate by torchlight.

He came up onto the wall above it and stood there, and the torches lit him from below. He'd got thinner since the winter. His face had been long and sharp then; it was longer and sharper now, and there was something in it that hadn't been there at the ford, a kind of tiredness that went all the way down. He wore black. He'd worn black since Teo.

He didn't look at Calder. He looked at the old one in the heather, for a long time, by the light of the torches.

Then he looked at the ledges in the road.

"Gunna," he said.

"Arn."

"You're out of the Roosts."

"We're in the road," said Gunna Tarne. "Last I knew, the road was anybody's."

He looked at her a while longer. Then, at last, at Calder.

"She can't fly," Arn said.

"No."

"She walked from the river."

"From the Staithe. Five days."

Arn looked at the old one again. Something moved in his face and was put away.

"My grandmother took me up to see her when I was four," he said. "On the ledge. Sigrun had her then. I put my hand on her nose and she breathed on me." He said it flatly, as if he were telling the price of something. "I thought she was the biggest thing in the world."

Nobody said anything.

"The setters will see to her," said Arn Varr. "Ivar Leck and the others. On the ledges. Tonight. Whatever she needs." He looked down at Calder. "And when the wing's set, the three come back to the Clan."

Calder didn't move.

"Back to the ledges. Back to the Roosts, where they came from. They fly when the Clan flies." Arn's voice didn't change. "The law says a bonded dragon goes where its partner goes. The law was written when every partner was Clan. So you come back too, farmer. You come back and you're Clan, and you fly when the Clan flies, and that's the end of the barley man going where he likes with three of our dragons." He paused. "That's the price. Say yes or take her back down the glen."

The torches cracked in the wind.

Behind him Calder felt the ledges not moving. He felt Kharrak, in the road, very still, with the red tide down low in him and watching. He felt Lisska on the wall-top, where she'd gone to sit, frightened. And he felt the old one, in the heather, with her eye shut.

*Yes,* said Aurveth.

He hadn't asked her.

*Say yes, farmer.* Her voice in his head was slow and very tired. *I sat on those ledges before there was a Varr. Before there was a Clan to keep the law. I can sit on them again.*

"It'll be the pens," he said, under his breath. "You told me. In the autumn. You told me what they do on the ledges in the spring. You called it the Clan's shame."

*It is the Clan's shame.* A pause. *It is also where the setters are.*

He stood in the road and thought of the five days. The two falls. The smell out of the wound. The bone in the wing grinding when she turned.

"Yes," said Calder.

Arn looked at him a moment longer. Then he turned and said something to the men on the wall, and went down out of the torchlight, and the gate began to open.

---

They carried her up that night.

She couldn't climb the stair. Nobody had thought she could. There was another way, round the side of the cliff, that he hadn't known about: a long ramp of beaten earth and old stone going up across the face from the end of the glen, wide enough for a dragon to walk, that the Clan used for bringing hurt ones up and dead ones down. She walked it. Kharrak under her shoulder, and Tamsin and an old black-and-grey called Harl on the other side, pressing their shoulders to hers where the wing was, and Calder at her head. It took till midnight. When she got to the top and the long ledge outside the setters' cave, she lay down on the warm stone and didn't move again.

The warm stone. He put his hand flat on it, and it was warm, like a hearthstone an hour after the fire's out. Under it, very far down, the pulse.

The setters were waiting.

There were three of them. Two old women he didn't know, and Ivar Leck. He knew Ivar. He'd seen him in the autumn, on this ledge, with his hands on a young dragon's foreleg, singing, and the young dragon with its eyes half shut. A little bent old man with a bald brown head and a white beard trimmed square and hands like roots. His dragon, an old dun called Brindle, lay along the back of the ledge against the cliff, too old to do anything but watch.

Ivar looked at the wing first. Then he looked at the wound under the foreleg, and smelled it, and sat back on his heels.

"That first," he said. "The wing'll wait a day. That won't."

He looked at Kharrak.

"You. Red. Can you make a knife hot without melting it?"

---

He held her head while they did it.

He didn't have to. She didn't move. He sat on the ledge with her great jaw across his knees and his arms round as much of it as his arms would go, and talked to her, and Ivar Leck cut the rot out of the horn-wound with a long thin knife that Kharrak breathed on till it glowed, and the smell came up and filled the cave mouth, and Aurveth didn't make a sound.

He felt it. All of it. Through the bond, as he'd felt the horn go in on the fort's roof. He felt the knife and the heat and the bad flesh coming away. He sat there with his jaw shut so tight his teeth hurt and held her and didn't make a sound either, because she wasn't.

When it was done the old women packed the wound with something green that smelled of moss and honey, and bound it with linen soaked in a brown water that Ivar said a word over. A Clan word, a herd-word, the kind you said over a ewe's cut. On the other bank, Calder thought, it would have done nothing at all.

Here, something. He couldn't have said what. A coolness. The heat in the wound, which had been going up into the bond for days like smoke from a smouldering rick, went down a little.

*Better,* said Aurveth. It was the first thing she'd said in an hour.

"Is it?"

*A little.* She breathed. *Go to sleep, farmer.*

He didn't. He sat with her head across his knees on the warm stone till it was light.

---

They set the wing on the second day.

It took all morning. The long bone of the outer finger, the one that held the edge of the wing out, was broken in two places, Ivar said, and the pieces had ridden over each other in the twelve days since the fort and started to knit wrong. They had to come apart before they could go together.

They did it with ropes. There was a beam set into the rock above the setters' cave, old black oak, polished by a hundred years of ropes, and they ran three ropes over it and down to the wing, padded with fleeces where they went round. Five of the ledges' dragons lay down on her, on her back and her good wing and her tail, carefully, with their weight, to hold her still: Tamsin and Harl and three others, old dragons all of them, lying on her as gently as hens on eggs. Then the riders of the ledges took the ropes. Gunna and the others, and Calder, and the boy not twenty, whose name was Leif.

"When I say," said Ivar Leck.

He put his hands on the bone, one either side of the first break.

"Now."

They pulled.

---

He'd thought the knife was the worst thing he'd ever felt through the bond. It wasn't.

He didn't remember much of it after. He remembered the rope burning through his palms. He remembered Kharrak behind him on the ledge with the red tide coming up and up in him, so that Calder had to hold the red dragon with one part of himself and pull the rope with another and feel the bone with a third. He remembered Lisska screaming somewhere above them on the cliff, high and thin, as if she were the one. He remembered the sound the bone made.

And he remembered Aurveth, under the five dragons, not moving at all, and her voice in his head through all of it, very quiet, saying a name. Not his. *Sigrun. Sigrun. Sigrun.* Over and over, like a woman counting.

Then Ivar said, "Hold," and they held, and Ivar said, "There," and they let the ropes go slack, and it was done.

---

They splinted it with ash poles bound in hide, two along the bone and one across, and then the setters knelt down round the wing and put their hands on it and began to sing.

He'd heard the setting-song once, in the autumn, from Ivar alone. This was three voices, and then more: Gunna came and knelt, and put her hands on the wing beside the setters' hands, and sang, and then the others of the ledges, one by one, until there were a dozen old voices on the ledge outside the setters' cave singing over the old one's wing with their hands on the splints.

It was a herd-song. That was all. He knew the shape of it. It was the song his own father had sung under his breath when he set a ewe's leg in the lambing shed, only longer, and older, and in the old tongue all the way through instead of only at the end, and with verses in it he'd never heard, that went on and on, round and round, like a beck going round a stone. It wasn't the kind of thing they taught in the lowland schools. The Clan didn't hold with lowland magic. This was older than that. This was what you said over a beast's hurt when you loved the beast and didn't know what else to do.

He knelt by the wing with the rope-burns on his hands and listened to it and felt it go in.

That was the thing. He felt it go in. Through the bond, through her. Not like the herd-word on the wound, a coolness. Something slower. A warmth in the bone itself, deep in it, the two ends of each break under the splints. And a drawing. As if each piece were reaching for the other through the dark inside the wing, slowly, two hands reaching across a bed in the night. A drawing together. Like a cut pulling shut.

Not much. A little. He could feel how little. But it went in, and it held, and when the song stopped after a long time, it didn't stop. It went on in the bone by itself, slow, faint, like the pulse in the ground under the ledge.

Ivar Leck sat back on his heels and wiped his hands down his beard.

"Well," he said. He sounded surprised. "That took."

"Doesn't it always?"

"Not like that." The old man looked at the wing for a long time. "I've been singing that song on this ledge fifty years. Ten years now it's been like singing into a sack. The ground's been poor. Everybody's songs have been going thin." He shook his head. "That's more than I've had out of it in ten years."

"It's poor everywhere else," said Calder. "You should feel it on the other bank. Over there it's nothing. Over there your song wouldn't do anything at all."

Ivar looked at him.

"Then I'll stay this side," he said.

---

He told Arn on the third day.

He hadn't meant to wait. He'd meant to tell him the first night, at the gate, in front of everyone. But the old one had needed carrying up, and then cutting, and then setting, and he hadn't left the ledge for two days, and on the morning of the third day Gorm Askell came up the stair, limping, and stood at the end of the ledge and said, "The chief'll see you. In the hall. Now, he says." He looked at the old one, lying with her head on her forelegs and the splinted wing out along the ledge. "If she can spare you."

*Go,* said Aurveth. *I am not going anywhere.*

---

The hall was half dark.

It was the long hall at the heart of the hold, with the roof-tree going down the middle of it and the two great hearths, east and west. When he'd come here in the autumn there had been two fires, one either side, Arn's and Ulric's, with the space between them getting wider. Now there was one. The east hearth burned. The west hearth was cold and swept and black, and nobody sat on that side of the hall, and the benches there were pushed back against the wall.

Arn sat on the east side, at the high table, with Gorm beside him and two of his captains standing. He didn't get up.

"Gunna read me a paper in my yard," he said. "Before the winter was out. In front of my people. In your mother's hand." He turned a cup on the table. "I put her out of the gate for it. I'll say that now, so you know I know I did it."

"I know."

"Say it, then. Whatever it is. You've come up the Way with it."

So Calder said it.

He said it the way he'd heard it. Lying on the boards in the tower at the Old Ruin, with his face to the crack, and the fire below, and Rook's pleasant ordinary voice coming up. He'd said it to Maren on Liss's floor, the first night, and it had come out of him in pieces. He'd said it to himself every night on the Way since, so it would come out whole.

He told Arn about the horse. Rook taking a horse from Ulric's camp the night before the first ford, saying he was going to the coast for his mother. Lying up all night in the gorse above the water. He told him about Teo coming down alone at first light, before his men, because he always did, because he wanted to be first. About Teo being pleased to see him. Waving. Thinking Rook had come down from Ulric's people to hold the horses. Getting off his horse at the edge of the water and holding out the reins.

"He never drew," said Calder. "That's what Rook said. *He never drew. He went in the water like a stone.*"

Arn didn't move.

He told him the rest. The grey armband on the alders, plain, no thread, left there on purpose. The Clan axe dropped in the shallows. *The farmer found them. Arn found the farmer. It went the way you said it would.* He told him about the mill. The crossbow in the hedge at the bottom of the field, bought, one of Delling's, before the rest of the company was hired, to put the woman on the green dragon out of the fight. Imra Sallow. So the war-pairs would break. So the dragons would come loose.

He took the knife out of his coat and put it on the table. Plain, round pommel, and the ring of six dots cut in it. He put the iron ring beside it, on its string. The ring of the man who'd shot at the second ford.

"That's the knife he had on the spit," he said. "When he died. That's the ring off the man at the ford. They wear the six. All of them."

Arn looked at the knife a long time.

He didn't touch it.

"Where's Rook?"

"Dead. On the spit at the Ruin. He came at me with that knife so Kharrak would kill him, and Kharrak did."

"So he'd not have to say it in my yard."

"Yes."

Arn nodded slowly, as if that were something he'd expected. Then he looked up from the knife at Calder, and his face was very tired, and very hard, and there was something under both that Calder couldn't see the bottom of.

"A dead man's words," said Arn Varr, "in a living man's mouth, aren't proof."

---

Nobody said anything.

The east fire cracked. Gorm Askell looked at the table.

"You understand me," said Arn. "I'm not saying you're lying. I'm saying I can't know you're not. You came to my brother's burning with three of our dragons in your head and a story. You stood in the water at the ford with a story. You sent my yard a paper with a story on it in your mother's hand. And now you come up the Way with the story again, and the only man who could have said it in front of me is dead, and it was your dragon that killed him." He put his hand flat on the table, beside the knife, not on it. "If I go to Skarrow with this, and stand in my uncle's gate, and say: *Uncle, I was wrong, it wasn't you, it was a man with a knife, the farmer says so, the farmer heard it through a crack in the floor*: what does Ulric say?"

Calder didn't answer.

"He says, *Prove it.* That's what he says. That's what I'd say." Arn looked at the cold hearth across the hall. "You found him. In the water. I found you. With him. That's all I know, farmer. That's all I've known since the autumn. Everything else is what people say."

He sat back.

"Rook said there was a book," said Calder.

Arn looked at him.

"On the spit. Before. He said every bag of silver he paid out went in a book. What for. Who to. And the book went into Delling's chest with the silver, the morning after the flight from the Ram's Head." He heard his own voice, flat. "Delling's at Cotter's Cross. A man on the Way told me. Delling sells anything."

"And you can't read," said Arn.

"No."

Arn turned the cup on the table, once, twice.

"Bring me the book," he said. "If there's a book. Bring me the book, and put it on this table beside that knife, and I'll ride to Skarrow with it myself." He stood up. "Until then you're Clan, and you'll fly when the Clan flies, and you'll not say my brother's name in my hall again."

He went out.

---

Lisska came back that evening.

He'd sent her south on the second day, while they were setting the wing, because he couldn't bear for her to be on the cliff screaming, and because he'd promised Wren every few days. *Find them,* he'd said. *Find them and stay a while and come back and tell me.* She came up the glen at dusk, low and fast, and he felt her a league off, a bright quick rush in the back of his head. Then, as she came closer, the rush went wrong. Frightened. Worse than frightened.

She came down on the ledge beside the old one in a clatter of wings and stood there shaking.

*The lion,* she said. *Calder. The lion killed two men.*

He went still.

*In the trees. Off the Way. At a beck. Men with crossbows, five of them, waiting, for the children. I saw them waiting. I saw them from high up and I couldn't tell anyone, I couldn't, there was nobody to tell, the lion was too far, and then the lion-woman went down to them on her own to talk, and one of them shot at her.* The words came faster and faster. *And the lion went over the water. And I heard it. From high up. And two of them didn't get up again, and three ran away, and the lion stood in the beck and drank.*

"Wren," he said.

*Wren's all right. They're all all right. The lion-woman didn't let them see.* A pause. *Wren saw the trees after. Where it was. She looked at the trees and then she looked at the lion.* Another pause. *She didn't say anything. She didn't make me say anything twice.*

He sat down on the warm stone.

He thought: five men with crossbows. For his children. On a beck in the Fellwood, waiting. And a knight with a sword and a lion, between them.

He was frightened. That came first. Frightened in his whole body, as he'd been at the Ram's Head, for Wren and for Pip and Nan and his mother and his sister, out there on the wrong bank with the whole Fellwood round them full of men like that.

Under the fright there was something else. He felt it and didn't want it, and it was there anyway. Two men who'd come for his children through the trees with crossbows weren't going to come for anyone's children again.

He was glad.

He sat on the ledge with the dark coming down over the glen and the cliff warm behind him and was glad, and was ashamed of being glad, and was glad anyway. He didn't know what to do with it. He'd never been glad a man was dead in his life. Not even Rook, on the spit.

*Are you angry?* said Lisska.

"No," he said. "No. I'm not angry."

*She's angry. The lion-woman. With the lion. I saw it. They didn't sit together after. They sat apart.*

He thought about that for a long time.

---

He couldn't go himself.

He knew it before he'd finished thinking it. He sat up most of the night on the ledge thinking it anyway, round and round, like the setting-song.

He couldn't leave the old one. The wing was two days set. The song was still going in the bone, faint and slow, and the wound was still packed, and if he went more than a league or two from her he felt her pulling at him through the bond, not on purpose: a hurt child pulling at your sleeve in its sleep. Five days down the Way and five days back. She'd have had to do it without him.

And he couldn't bring them up here. Not with the lion. Not into Arn's yard, with Arn saying *a dead man's words aren't proof* and *you'll fly when the Clan flies*, and the red thread on the wall and the crossbows. A lion in the gate of Varrhold, with a Hearthmere knight beside it, and the farmer Arn still called a liar standing with them. He could see how that went. He could see it as you see a wall going over before it goes.

And there was the other thing. The thing he didn't say even to himself until near morning.

*Your road's that way. So's yours.*

He'd said the first half at the landing, and she'd said the second, and not looked up when he went down to the water. He'd thought, walking up the Way, that it was the last thing she'd ever say to him. That was all right. People said last things to each other every day and went off down their roads. And then she'd turned round, for no reason he knew, and gone back for his family, and carried them over the river in the night, and was walking them up the Fellwood to him with a lion that killed men.

He didn't know what he'd say to her. He didn't know what you said.

He thought she'd know that too. He thought, from what Lisska said, she was coming up the Way to hand his family over at the top and turn round and go, without coming the last leagues. He thought that was what he'd do.

---

He asked Gunna in the morning.

She was sitting on her own ledge, below the setters' cave, with her back against Tamsin's flank and a bowl of porridge in her lap, looking at the glen. She didn't get up when he came along the ledge to her.

"My family's coming up the Way," he said. "On the Saltreach bank, in the Fellwood. My mother and my sister and her two and my girl. And the greys." He paused. "And a knight. With the lion."

Gunna ate some porridge.

"I heard," she said. "The little green one tells everybody everything."

"I want them taken off her hands. At the moor's edge, where the Way comes out of the trees. Before they get anywhere near the hold." He heard himself say it, plain. "And taken up to the caves. The hatching caves, above Coldtarn. The ledges keep the caves. Arn's men don't go up there in the spring. Nobody does but you."

"And the lion-woman?"

"She'll not come in. I'd not ask her." He looked at the glen. "She's going east."

Gunna put the bowl down on the ledge.

"You'll not go down yourself?"

"No."

She looked at him for a long time. The short dry look, the one she'd given him in the road at the gate. Then she looked past him, along the ledge, to where the old one lay with her wing out in its splints.

"Hallam would have gone," she said.

"Hallam's dead."

She didn't say anything to that. Behind her, Tamsin opened one grey-green eye and looked at him, and shut it again.

"All right," said Gunna Tarne. "I'll take Tamsin, and Harl, and the boy Leif, and his. We'll go down tomorrow and sit on the edge and wait." She picked the porridge up again. "And I'll tell your girl her father's sorry he couldn't come himself, because she'll ask."

"She'll ask," he said. "Tell her twice."

---

They went on the fifth day, at first light. He stood on the setters' ledge and watched them go: Tamsin and Harl and Leif's young brown dragon going out off the Roosts one after another and down the glen low over the beck, three shapes, smaller and smaller, until the morning took them.

Aurveth watched them too. She'd lifted her head off her forelegs for it.

*She is a good woman, Gunna Tarne,* she said. *She sat on the ledge below Sigrun's forty years and never once came up to ask how we did. She thought it would be rude.* A pause. *I liked her for it.*

"Will she bring them?"

*She will bring them.*

He sat with her all day. Ivar came at noon and put his hands on the splints and listened to the bone, with his head on one side, like a man listening at a door. He nodded, and went away, and came back at dusk and did it again. Calder sat on the warm stone and felt the song still going in the bone, faint, slow, and the pulse in the rock under it, and didn't think about anything.

At dusk Lisska came back up the glen.

He'd sent her down again with Gunna, to go on ahead and find them and come back. She came up out of the dark of the glen low over the water, crying, and he felt her two leagues off: not frightened this time. The other thing. The bright, high, quick thing she'd had in her before the mill, before any of it, that he hadn't felt in her for months.

She came down on the ledge and ran three steps along it and stopped in front of him with her wings still half open.

*I saw them,* she said. *Calder. I saw them. Coming up the Way, the last bit, where it comes out of the trees under the moor. Ada with a stick. Bel with Nan. Pip on the old grey mare. All of them.*

"Wren."

*Wren's on Old Grey.* Lisska's voice went high and bright. *Not walking beside him. On him. Up on his back. He let her. She's sitting up on his back with her hands in his mane, and he's walking up the Way with her on him, slow, and he's lifting his feet.* She stopped for breath. *And the lion's walking beside them. On the other side. Right beside her. Like a wall.*

He didn't say anything. He couldn't.

*Say it twice,* said Lisska. *She'd want you to. She'd make you.*

"Wren's coming up the Way on Old Grey," said Calder Fenn, on the ledge outside the setters' cave, with his hand on the old one's jaw. "With a lion beside her."

He said it again.
