Lisska came back on the morning of the seventeenth day, early, with the sun not yet over the fells. He'd sent her back down to the edge the day before, to sit on the rocks and watch.

He was on the setters' ledge with the old one, as he'd been every morning, sitting with his back to the warm rock and his hand on her jaw, and he felt Lisska coming a long way off: a bright quick flicker in the back of his head, like a fish turning in a shallow. Then she came round the shoulder of the cliff low and fast and landed on the ledge in a scatter of grit, and folded her wings, and didn't say anything.

That was how he knew it was something.

"Well?" he said.

*She went,* said Lisska.

He didn't say anything.

*Before it was light. I was up. I couldn't sleep on the edge, it's too windy and Leif's brown one snores. I was up on the rocks above the fire.* She stopped. *And the ground shook. A long way off. From the east. And the lion got up.*

"I felt it," said Calder. "Here. Before dawn. The old one felt it."

*And then the lion came back to the fire and lay down by her. Right by her. And then they went.* Lisska shifted her feet on the rock. *She didn't wake anyone. Only the old woman was awake. Your mother. She put up her hand.*

He looked out over the glen. The light was coming up grey over the moor. Somewhere out there, east.

*I went up,* said Lisska. *High. To see. I watched them go across the moor. A long way. Until they were very small.* Her voice went odd. *Calder. The heather was moving behind them.*

"Wind."

*There wasn't any wind. It was still. Everything was still.* She was quiet. *Only behind them. Behind the lion. A long way back, the heather moving, as it moves when the wind goes over it, in a line, following. As if the ground was humming. As if the ground under the moor was ringing where he'd walked and it went on ringing after he'd gone.*

He sat with that.

Beside him the old one had opened her eye.

*It is waking in him,* said Aurveth. *What he is.*

He didn't ask her what that meant. He thought he didn't want to know yet.

"Did she look back?"

*No,* said Lisska. *Not once.*

---

Gunna brought them over the moor on the eighteenth day.

He went up to meet them. He hadn't been off the ledge for six days, and the old one let him go for one. *Go,* she said. *The bone's knitting. It doesn't need you to watch it.* So he walked up past Coldtarn in the morning, four leagues, up the long brown slope above the hold where he'd walked behind the old chief's bier in the autumn, past the black water, where the burned stones on the shore were cold, and on up, into the fells, to the old hatching caves.

He'd never been inside them. He'd seen them from Coldtarn's shore: a row of black mouths along the cliff above the tarn, high up, with a stain of green going down from the middle one where water ran out of it. In the winter the dragons had slept there, Aurveth said. Not the bonded ones. The others, the ones who hunted in the high glens and came back when it got cold.

He came up the last of the slope at noon, and there was a warm wind coming out of the middle cave.

Warm. On a cold spring day in the fells with snow still in the gullies, a wind coming out of the hill as warm as the air over a bread oven. He went in.

---

It was enormous.

It went back into the dark further than he could see, a great hall of rock with a roof he couldn't make out. The floor was smooth and dry and warm under his boots. Warm all the way through, as if a fire had been lit under it a long time ago and never gone out. Down the middle of it ran water, a stream a pace wide, steaming a little, going out of the cave mouth and down the cliff to the tarn: the spring that never froze. And on either side of the water, in the floor, there were bowls.

Great stone bowls, worn into the rock. Dozens of them. Each one big enough to lie down in, smooth as the inside of a cup, going back in rows into the dark. The old hatching hollows. Where the eggs had been laid, for longer than there'd been a Clan, in the warm floor over the place where the Deep ran close.

They were empty. Some of them had dust in them. A hundred years, Gunna had said.

At the back, a long way in, he could see dark shapes lying among the bowls. Three or four. Dragons, unbonded, still wintering, who'd lifted their heads when he came in and looked at him and put their heads down again.

He put his hand flat on the floor.

The pulse came up into his palm so strong he nearly took his hand away. Not a heart through a wall here. A heart under his hand.

*Yes,* said Aurveth in his head, very faint, four leagues off. *There.*

---

They came over the rise in the afternoon, at walking pace.

He saw the dragons first, the three of them coming slow across the moor in a line, keeping to the pace of what walked under them. And under them, coming over the brown rise one after another, the greys. Mabbin with Pip on her back. Sorrel, limping a little, with the packs. Bel on foot with Nan on her hip. His mother with a hazel stick. And Old Grey, the great grey dray-beast, walking slow and lifting his feet, with a small figure sitting up on his back with her hands in his mane.

He didn't run. He'd thought he would. He stood at the cave mouth in the warm wind and watched them come up the last slope, and his legs wouldn't.

Wren saw him.

She didn't wave. She didn't shout. She sat up straighter on Old Grey's back and watched him all the way up the slope, very serious, and when Old Grey came to a stop in front of the cave mouth she looked down at her father from up there, from higher than his head, and he looked up at her.

"Hello," said Calder.

"Hello, Papa," said Wren. And then: "Hello, Papa."

He held up his arms. She came down into them off the grey beast's back, all knees and elbows, and he held her. She smelled of peat smoke and heather and the grey's mane. She held on round his neck very tight for a long time and didn't say anything at all, and neither did he.

Over her shoulder he saw Ada come up the last of the slope with the stick, slowly, and stop, and lean on it, and look at him.

---

Wren told him in the cave, by the warm water, when the others had gone to see to the greys.

She'd sat down on the edge of one of the stone bowls with her feet in it, and he'd sat down beside her, and she'd looked at the water going by for a while. Then she said it plainly, as she'd say any piece of news, to have it said.

"She wouldn't come in."

He nodded.

"She brought us up. All the way. Through the woods. And then she wouldn't come in." Wren looked at him. "She wouldn't come in, Papa."

"I know."

"She went east. In the night. With the lion." Wren frowned. "I asked her if she was coming and she said her road was the other way. I made her say it twice."

"Did she?"

"Yes." Wren reached into her shirt. "She gave me this."

She took it out and held it out to him on her palm. A stone. Small, flat, grey, worn smooth, with a white line running clean round the middle of it like a band round a finger.

He took it. It was warm from being in her shirt. He turned it over, and it was the same on both sides.

"She found it on the edge," said Wren. "She didn't say what it was for. I asked. She said she didn't know." She looked at the stone in his hand. "I think it's for you."

"Why?"

"Because she didn't know what it was for," said Wren, as if that settled it, "and she gave it to me anyway."

He sat on the edge of the hatching bowl with the stone in his hand and his daughter beside him and the warm water going by, and turned the stone over, and over.

Then he put it in his coat. In the inside pocket, with Rook's knife and the dead shooter's iron ring on its string. Three things. He didn't know what the third one meant. He thought maybe nobody did.

---

Ada lit the fire with her word that night in the cave mouth, and it caught before her hand had finished turning. She looked at it, and then at him.

"Did you see that?"

"I saw it."

"Forty years I've been saying that word over kindling." She fed the fire a heather-root. "It's never once caught like that. Not even at home. Not even before the lamps." She sat back on her heels. "This place."

"The floor's warm," said Calder. "All the way in."

"I know it is. I've had my hand on it." Ada looked at him across the fire, the long level look. "Well. Say it, then."

"Say what?"

"Whatever it is you've been not saying since we came up the hill." She poked the fire. "About her."

He didn't say anything.

"She'd not have come, son," said Ada. "Not into Arn's yard. Not with the lion. Not to see you. Not for any of it." She put the stick down. "She knew that before you did. Don't be a fool about it."

"I'm not being anything about it."

"No," said his mother. "You're not. That's what I mean."

Bel came and sat down by him at the fire after the children were asleep. She put her arms round him, once, hard, as she'd done when their father died, and when Senna died, and let go, and went back to Nan.

---

He walked back down to the hold the next morning.

The old one was where he'd left her. She'd turned her head to watch the glen for him. When he came along the ledge she put her jaw down on the warm stone in front of him and breathed out, a long slow breath, and he put his hand on her nose.

*They are in the caves,* she said.

"They're in the caves."

*Good.* She shut her eye. *Then I can sleep.*

---

He saw the pens properly on the twentieth day.

He'd seen them since he came. You couldn't not see them. They were on the lower ledges, below the Roosts and above the yard, a row of cave mouths with heavy oak bars across them and gates in the bars, and keepers going in and out with hand-barrows of mutton. In the autumn they'd been open and empty. Now they were shut, and there were dragons in them.

Two to a pen. Chosen. He went down the long stair in the morning with Gunna's old friend Hulda Sarr, who hadn't said a word to him in four days and said none now, and stood on the ledge in front of the pens and looked.

A young blue and an older blue in the first pen, side by side on the straw, the young one with her head turned to the bars. Two blacks in the next. A red and a bronze in the third, not touching. The keepers went in with the barrows and came out again and barred the gates behind them, and the dragons watched them do it. Not angry. Not anything. They lay on the straw and watched the gates, with the light coming in through the bars across them in stripes.

He'd shut ewes in a fold with a tup in the autumn every year of his life. He'd chosen which ewe and which tup. He'd never thought twice about it.

He stood in front of the pens a long time.

*That is the shame I told you of,* said Aurveth, from the setters' ledge above. She couldn't see the pens from there. She didn't need to.

"Why do they stand it?"

*The eggs.* A pause. *An egg can only hatch where the ground runs warm. You put your hand on the floor in the caves. You know. There are a few places in the world where it runs warm enough, and the best of them are here, and the Clan holds them.* Another pause. *And the bargain. The ledges, the caves, the flocks, for flying when the Clan flies. That is older than the pens. That one is fair. I made it myself, before there was a Varr, with people whose names nobody remembers.*

"And the pens?"

*The pens came after. When the Clan wanted bigger dragons and fiercer ones and more of them than came by themselves.* She was quiet. *And habit. They were hatched here and raised on the Clan's mutton and they know nothing else. Habit is the strongest chain there is, farmer. Stronger than iron. The knights should have used habit on the one under the river. It would never have got loose.*

Below him a keeper came out of the red-and-bronze pen with an empty barrow and barred the gate, and nodded to Calder, and went down the stair whistling.

---

He saw the chair the same afternoon.

Gorm Askell sent for him to the hall to give him a cloak. A Clan cloak, grey, with no thread on the armband, because he was Clan again and couldn't go about in a farmer's coat on the ledges. Gorm said so, sourly, as if it pained him, and held it out across the high table. Calder took it.

And then he saw the chair.

He'd seen it in the autumn, at the old chief's burning, with Haldor's sword across it. He'd seen it on the day of the ford, with Teo's armband laid on the seat. He hadn't looked at it since. A high-backed chair of black oak in the middle of the high table, carved with dragons going round the back, with the seat worn pale and smooth by a great many years of Varrs. It stood between where the two fires had been, at the very middle of the hall.

Somebody had put a cloth over it. A plain grey cloth, thrown over the back and the seat, and left there.

Arn's place at the table was beside it. On the right. Not in it.

"He doesn't sit in it," Calder said.

Gorm looked at the chair. Then at Calder. Then at the chair again, with his long sour face, as if it had been an expense.

"A chief's named at Coldtarn," he said. "Both halves pour. The Varrs' kin and the others, all of them, at the black water, and when they've poured, he's chief, and he sits." He sniffed. "Half the Clan's at Skarrow. Half the Clan's pouring for the uncle. Nobody's poured for anybody."

"So nobody sits."

"So nobody sits." Gorm put his inky hands flat on the table. "He had the cloth put over it the night his brother burned. Said nobody was to take it off. It's still there." He looked at Calder. "It'll be there till both halves pour, or till the roof falls in. And the roof's damp."

Calder stood with the grey cloak over his arm and looked at the empty chair under its cloth in the middle of the half-dark hall, with the east fire burning and the west hearth cold, and thought of Teo pouring the water at Coldtarn with one hand on his brother's and one on his uncle's, in the autumn, and making them.

---

Ulric's rider came on the twenty-first day.

A man on a grey hill-pony, alone, in plain grey with no thread, coming up the Way from the south-east in the middle of the morning with his hands where the wall could see them. He stopped below the gate. The men on the wall stood up with their crossbows.

"From Skarrow," the rider said. "For the farmer."

Somebody went for Calder. He came down the long stair and across the yard and out of the postern, in the grey cloak, and stood in the road in front of the rider. Behind him, on the wall, the men with the red thread watched. And Arn. He'd come up onto the wall. Calder didn't turn round, but he knew.

The rider looked down at him from the pony. A lean old man with a white scar through one eyebrow. He didn't get down.

"Ulric Varr says," said the rider, loud enough for the wall: "Tell the farmer I listened to the end. Tell him when he's proof, I'll come and look at it."

That was all.

He turned the pony round and rode back down the Way at a walk, and didn't look back.

Calder stood in the road. Behind him, on the wall, he heard Arn say something short to somebody and go down off the wall and in.

---

The wool-carters came on the twenty-second day, and talked about sheep.

Tuft and Fell, the brothers from Ewe Hollow, with their wain empty and their two oxen tired, coming up the Way from Cotter's Cross the long way round because the short way round went past the Ram's Head and nobody went past the Ram's Head now. They'd taken the spring wool down. They'd sold it for half what it was worth, Tuft said, because there was nobody at the Cross to buy it but sellswords, and sellswords didn't want wool, they wanted drink. Fell said that wasn't true, a sellsword had bought a fleece off them to sleep on, and Tuft said one fleece wasn't a market, and they went on like that up the yard and into the hall and through half a jug of Gorm's ale.

Calder sat across from them and waited. He'd known Tuft and Fell all his life. You had to wait.

Under the sheep, after a long time, the news came out.

Cotter's Cross was full. Full of the companies that had stood on the hill above Harrow's End on the morning of the flight and never been paid. Three thousand of them, Fell said; Tuft said two. Sleeping in the beast pens and the hiring yard and the tithe barns, in every inn, on the floors. Drinking on credit. Waiting.

"For what?" said Calder.

Fell looked at Tuft.

"For him," Fell said. "There's a man come. Come up out of the ground, they say, by an old stair under the Ruin that nobody knew about. Came up the morning after, all over mud. And went south. And now he's at the Cross, in the Fleece, in the big room upstairs, and every night they go up to see him, the captains." He drank. "Under the ring. They've got it painted on the inn sign over the Fleece's ram. Six dots. They've got it on everything."

"Who is he?"

Fell opened his mouth. "Vale," he said, and stopped.

There was a silence at the table.

"There was two," said Tuft, into it. He put his cup down. "Everybody knows there was two. Everybody at the Cross. They'll tell you. Crown hung one in chains on the gate at Greyhold with a board round his neck. And the other one's sat in the Fleece, alive, buying drinks." He shook his head. "Two of them. All that time. Speaking in two places on the same day and everybody thinking it was magic. Wasn't magic. It was two men."

"Which one's at the Cross?"

"The one that's alive," said Tuft.

Fell reached into his coat.

"And there's these," he said. "On every door. Every post. There's a man with a press, a bookbinding man, sits in the back of the Fleece printing them all day long. Reede, they call him." He laid it on the table. A sheet of paper, folded in four, printed in big black letters, with the ring of six at the top. "Took that off the ox-shed door at the Cross. Thought you'd want it, Calder, being as you were the one that started it all."

Calder looked at it.

He couldn't read it. He could see the ring at the top, and some of the letters he knew, from the black book's cover and the signs at Cotter's Cross, and none of the words.

Gorm Askell, who'd been sitting at the end of the table pretending to do the levies, reached over and took it.

He read it aloud. His dry sour voice went down the hall.

"THE LAMPS WERE A CRIME. THE CROWN DRANK YOUR WELLS FOR THREE HUNDRED YEARS AND CALLED IT LIGHT. ASK WHO SOURED THE WATER AT MILLBROOK. ASK WHO DIMMED YOUR CHARMS. ASK WHY THE MARCHES BURN WHILE HALLOWMERE SITS IN THE DARK. THE ONE REALM IS COMING. COME TO THE CROSS." He turned it over. The back was blank. "That's all."

Nobody said anything.

"It's not wrong," said Fell at last, into his ale. "That's the thing. It's not wrong, is it. The lamps. The wells."

"No," said Calder. "It's not wrong."

He took the bill back from Gorm, and folded it in four again, and put it in his coat with the other things.

---

He told Arn on the twenty-third day.

He found him on the wall, alone, at dusk, looking south down the Way. He'd been there a lot, Gorm said. In the evenings. Looking south. As if he were waiting for somebody to come up it.

Calder stood beside him on the turf and looked south too.

"The book's at Cotter's Cross," he said. "With Delling. In the chest. Rook's book. Every bag of silver and what it was for." He didn't look at Arn. "There's a man at the Cross who came up out of the ground, and three thousand men waiting to see what he'll pay them. Delling's there too. Delling sells anything." He paused. "I'm going to buy it."

Arn didn't say anything for a while.

"With what?"

"I don't know yet."

Arn laughed. Not much. A short sound in his nose, the first Calder had heard out of him since the ford.

"That's not flying," Arn said. "You understand. The Clan flies when the Clan flies. That's the price you said yes to. This isn't flying. This is buying." He looked south. "The Clan doesn't fly for that. The Clan doesn't buy dead men's books in Saltreach inns."

"I know."

"So I'll not send riders. I'll not send silver." Arn's voice was flat. "I'll not send anything."

"I know."

Arn turned his head and looked at him for the first time.

"Go and buy it, then," he said.

He went down off the wall.

---

He went up to Kharrak in the morning.

The red dragon had taken a cave of his own on the high ledges, the first night, without asking anybody, and nobody had argued with him. He was lying across the mouth of it in the early sun when Calder came along the ledge, with his chin on his forelegs and his eyes half shut, as a dog lies across a door.

He opened one gold eye.

*No,* said Kharrak.

"I haven't said anything."

*You're going to ask. I can feel you going to ask. You've been going to ask since the wall.* The eye didn't close. *Nobody since Dag.*

Calder sat down on the ledge in front of him.

He knew about Dag. Everybody knew about Dag. Dag Rorke, the hardest rider on the ledges, as hard as his dragon. At the mill, in the dark, over the barley field. Dag standing up on Kharrak's back with his axe to swing at the old one's eye, and the old one taking him off it, in the air, as a heron takes a fish. Kharrak coming down across the Fenn field with nobody on him and the bond torn out of him like a tooth. He'd fallen into the barley a hundred yards from Calder and lain there and looked at him.

*He was a hard man,* said Kharrak. *Dag. I liked him. He didn't talk.* A pause. *He'd have killed her. The old one. If she'd not taken him. He'd have had her eye and then her throat.* Another pause. *I'd have let him.*

"I know."

*And then she took him off me. And I came down in your field.* The eye was on Calder, gold, unblinking. *And now I lie on the same ledge as her and she's got one eye and I've got nobody on my back. And you want to get on it.*

"I've got to go south. To the Cross. Four days by the Way, walking. Less than a day by air." He looked at the red dragon. "The old one can't fly. Lisska can't carry me. She's too small."

*She's not too small. She's too silly.*

"She's too small."

Kharrak lay there a long time.

Then he got up. He got up all at once, the way he did everything, and stood on the ledge over Calder with the sun behind him, broad and scarred and red as a forge, and put his head down and looked at Calder from very close, so close Calder could feel the heat coming off him.

*Get on, then,* said Kharrak.

---

They tried it on the moor above the hold.

It was nothing like the old one. He'd ridden Aurveth in the winter, a dozen times, sitting high on the great bronze neck in the place where her neck met her shoulders, and she'd flown like a ship goes, slow and steady and enormous, as if the air were water and she were heavy enough not to care about the waves. Kharrak was something else. Kharrak was a bull let out of a pen.

He climbed onto the red dragon's neck in the heather, where Dag's saddle-place had been worn smooth on the scales, and took hold of the ridge in front of him with both hands, and Kharrak went up.

Straight up. No run. No slow beating to get the air under him. He crouched and threw himself at the sky, and the moor fell away under Calder like a dropped plate, and the wind hit him in the face so hard it took his breath, and the wings went down on either side of him with a crack like sails filling, once, twice, three times, and they were high, high, the hold a scatter of black roofs below, the Roosts a row of holes, the glen a crack.

And the red tide came up.

He felt it come, through the bond: the thing under Kharrak's anger that the anger was made of. Height. Speed. The air going past. A great hot joy in it that went up through the red dragon like fire up a chimney and into Calder and filled him, so that for a moment he wanted, more than he'd ever wanted anything, to go higher, and faster, and to find something below and go down on it with fire.

He held it.

He didn't know how. He'd held it at the Ewe Hollow turn with the sixteen and their crossbows, and it had felt like holding a gate shut against a bull. This was different. This was holding it from inside. Sitting on its neck with it coming up through his legs and his hands, and saying, not out loud: *Yes. I know. Not now. Not yet. Not that.*

Kharrak went over in the air at the top of the climb and came down.

Not fast. Not falling. Down in a long slant, with the wings out, over the moor, with the heather going by under them, and the red tide going down a little, and down, until it was only the heat coming off the scales under Calder's legs.

*Hm,* said Kharrak.

They came down in the heather a quarter-league from where they'd gone up. Calder slid off and sat down, because his legs wouldn't.

*Dag never held it,* said Kharrak. *He let it go. Every time. He liked it.* A pause. *You held it.*

"Was that wrong?"

The red dragon looked at him.

*No,* said Kharrak. *It was different.*

---

They went south on the twenty-fifth day, at dawn.

The old one watched them go from the setters' ledge. She'd lifted her head for it, as she had for Gunna. The wing lay out beside her in its splints, and under the splints, faint, the song still going in the bone.

*Bring it back,* she said. *The book.*

"I'll bring it."

*And bring yourself back with it.* A pause. *That one will try to bring you somewhere else. He likes it too much. Don't let him.*

*I can hear you,* said Kharrak.

*I know you can,* said Aurveth.

Then they were up, the red dragon going off the high ledge in one great drop and his wings catching and the glen going by, and Lisska behind them, crying with excitement, a green spark in the morning. They went south over the hold and down the glen. The falls went under them, white. The ford, small, with the alders round it like a ring of green. Teo's ford, where it had started. The Ram's Head, with its sign hanging by one chain, and the charm-board in the mud, and the stove barrels in the yard where he'd sat in the dark fifteen nights ago and been frightened.

Then the Way, going on south, down the long valley, pale. And at the bottom of it, where the valley opened, a scatter of roofs by the river with no smoke over most of them and the mill's wheel stopped. Harrow's End. And the top field above it, his own, gone to weed, and the longhouse with its door hanging open and nobody in it.

He didn't ask Kharrak to go down. He didn't ask him to go lower.

Beyond Harrow's End the Way went on south along the Saltreach bank, toward a smudge on the horizon where the land flattened out and a lot of smoke went up into the sky together.

Cotter's Cross.

*Lot of fires,* said Kharrak.

"Three thousand men," said Calder. "And one."

He put his hand in his coat, against the knife and the ring and the folded bill and the stone with the white ring round it, and held on to the red dragon's neck with the other, and they went south.
