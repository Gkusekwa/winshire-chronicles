---

They came down off the far side of the Scarfell in the rain, and for eleven days after that Maren didn't see a roof that wasn't a shepherd's.

The moor went down east in long steps, each one lower and greener than the last, into country where nobody seemed to live but sheep. Becks ran down out of it south-east, toward the Ysse's far feeders, and she and Orrun waded them where they were shallow and went up and down their banks where they weren't. She walked eight leagues a day. Less, when Orrun stopped. He stopped often, at first, and stood with his head up into the wind and his eyes half shut, feeling for it, and she stood beside him and waited.

*There,* he'd say, and they'd go on.

She bought bread at shepherds' doors with the last of her silver. They looked at the lion standing in the beck below their crofts and then at her and gave her the bread and wouldn't take the coin. One old woman gave her a whole cheese and shut the door in her face before she could thank her. At night Orrun went out into the dark and came back before dawn with blood on his muzzle, and she didn't ask what it had been. Sheep, she thought. She thought of the shepherds and their bread and didn't ask.

On the eighth day out from the edge, the land stopped going down, and flattened, and went on flat, and there was grass.

---

She'd heard of the Hallow Plains all her life and never once imagined them.

Grass. That was all. Grass from her feet to the edge of the world, green and silver in the wind, going away east under the sky without a hedge or a wall or a tree in it, so that the wind came across it in long waves you could watch coming from a league off. And at the back of it, so far off it might have been cloud, a line of white along the whole eastern sky. Mountains. The Worldsend. Every morning she looked at them, and every evening, and they never got any nearer.

There were stones.

Standing stones, grey, taller than a man, alone or in twos and threes, or in long rows going off across the grass in lines too straight to be anything but meant. She walked past the first one on the morning of the twenty-eighth day and stopped to look at it. Lichen. Old. Worn smooth by the wind into something like a shoulder.

*Something is under it,* said Orrun.

"What?"

*A beast.* He stood by the stone with his head down, smelling the grass at its foot. *Old. A long time.* He was quiet. *It's a grave.*

She looked at the long row of stones going off across the plain.

"All of them?"

*All of them.*

They walked on between the graves.

---

The herds didn't run from him.

That surprised her. She'd thought anything with sense would run from a lion the size of a dray-beast. On the second day on the plains they came over a long swell of grass and there was a stonehorn herd below them, a hundred head or more, grazing: massive grey beasts with plated shoulders and one great horn each and a smaller one behind it, short-sighted, short-tempered, as big as haywains. She stopped. She'd seen one stonehorn in her life, in a Hallowmere beast-pen, and it had nearly killed its keeper.

Orrun walked down into them.

They lifted their heads. The nearest ones turned their great short-sighted eyes on him, and snorted, and then put their heads down again and went on grazing. He walked through the middle of the herd. They moved aside for him, a few paces, without hurry, without breaking step, as cattle part for a farmer walking through a field. He came out the other side. She followed him, with her hand on Haverly's hilt, through a hundred head of stonehorn that didn't look at her once.

"Why didn't they run?"

*Why would they run?* He sounded faintly surprised. *They know what I am.*

"And what are you?"

He didn't answer that.

---

The horse clans watched them for three days.

She saw them first on the third morning: three riders on a rise a long way off to the north, black against the sky. Not on horses. On elk. Great elk, taller at the shoulder than a man, with antlers like the branches of winter trees, and riders sitting on them as if they'd been born there. They sat on the rise and watched her and Orrun go by, and didn't come closer.

The next day there were five. The day after, eight, on a different rise, to the south. Always a long way off. Always watching.

"Should we go to them?"

*No.* Orrun didn't turn his head. *They will come when they decide.*

That night a shadow went over the fire.

She looked up. It was very high, so high it was only a shape against the stars, and it went over and back and over again. A bird. A bird so big that at that height its wings were wider than her outstretched arms held up against the sky. Rust-brown and white, she thought, when the moon caught it.

*A roc,* said Orrun. *From the mountains.* He watched it go. *It is looking at me.*

"What does it want?"

*To see,* said Orrun. *Everything here wants to see.*

She lit the fire with Ada's word that night. On the plains it took on the second try. Two fingers, three turns, the catch in the middle. The stems smoked, and then, small and yellow, there was a flame, and she sat and looked at it as if she'd made it out of nothing. Which she had.

Through the bond she felt Orrun, lying out on the dark grass, lift his head and look at it too.

---

The Thornwood began on the thirty-first day, all at once, like a wall.

One moment it was grass. Then, across the whole of the north and the north-east, as far round as the white of the mountains, a dark line that got darker as they came toward it, and taller, until it stood over them: oak and black pine and thorn, so close together that she couldn't see a hand's depth into it. There was no edge to it, no thinning, no scrub going out into the grass. The plains stopped and the forest started, and the line between them ran away east and west as far as she could see.

The hum came out of it.

She'd been feeling it for days, through him. Now she felt it herself. In her own feet, through her own boots. A faint, steady ringing in the ground, as a stone floor rings when someone's working a forge a long way under it. She stood at the edge of the trees and put her hand flat on the bark of an oak, and it was in the oak too.

"It's in there," she said.

*It's in there,* said Orrun.

He went in. He went in between two black pines as if there were a door there, and there was, she saw when she followed him: a path. Narrow, soft with old needles, going in under the trees. A path made by feet.

---

The duskcat came on the first night in the trees.

She didn't see it come. She saw its eyes. She'd made a fire in a little clearing a league in, where the path crossed a stream, and she was sitting with her back to an oak and Haverly's sword across her knees, and she looked up and there were two lamps in the dark beyond the firelight.

Two green-gold lamps. Very still. A man's height off the ground.

She didn't move. Orrun, lying on the far side of the fire, didn't move either. But she felt him through the bond: not the gathering, not the wave drawing back. Interest. Mild, patient interest.

The lamps came closer. Into the edge of the firelight.

It was long and low and black. Not black: blacker than black. Its coat drank the firelight so that it was only a shape, a cut-out of the dark, with the eyes in it and the long tail going slowly side to side behind. It was the size of a big hound. It sat down at the edge of the light, as a cat sits, and looked at the lion.

Then a voice came out of the trees behind it.

"Grandfather."

A woman's voice. Speaking Maren's own tongue, but with something under it, a lilt, the words going up at the ends like water over stones.

"Grandfather. May I come to your fire?"

It wasn't speaking to Maren.

Orrun lifted his head and looked into the dark behind the duskcat for a long moment. Then he put his head down again on his paws.

*Tell her yes,* he said.

"Yes," said Maren.

---

Keeper Hesper came out of the trees on bare feet.

She was fifty, perhaps. Lean, brown, weathered as a fence post, with grey-streaked dark hair in a single long braid down her back, bound every hand's width with a turn of red cord. More cords at her belt: a dozen of them, of different colours, knotted. A short spear with a leaf-shaped blade. A cloak of some grey cloth that had been patched so many times it was more patch than cloak. She came to the edge of the firelight and stopped beside the duskcat and put her hand on its head, and looked at the lion.

She looked at him for a long time. Maren watched her face, and saw it do something she'd never seen a face do: go still all over, like water after a stone, and then, very slowly, fill up with something that might have been grief and might have been joy.

"Grandfather," she said again. Softly.

Then she bowed. The same bow Gunna Tarne had made on the Scarfell's edge: one hand flat on her chest, the other at her side, palm open.

Orrun looked at her.

*She means it,* he said to Maren. *The word.*

"What does it mean?"

"It means we thought you were all gone," said Keeper Hesper. She'd straightened. She was looking at Maren now, at last, with dark eyes in her brown face, measuring. "It means a long time ago, when the world was young, the grey-maned ones came out of this wood and walked west, and didn't come back, and we've been telling our children about them ever since and none of us believed it." She sat down by the fire, cross-legged, without being asked, and the duskcat lay down beside her with its chin on her knee. "This is Hush. I'm Hesper. Of the Keepers. I've walked this end of the Thornwood twenty years alone." She looked at the lion again. "And you've brought one home."

"He brought me," said Maren.

Hesper looked at her a while longer.

"Yes," she said. "I can see that."

---

She took them to the Barrows the next morning.

"He'll want to see them," Hesper said. "Before anything. Before the elders, before the other." She didn't say what the other was. "They're his."

Two days in, under the black pines, by paths Maren couldn't see until Hesper's bare feet were on them. Hush went ahead, a shadow among shadows. Hesper walked behind the lion and talked to him sometimes, in a language Maren didn't know, soft, a lilting run of words, and once he answered, Maren felt it, not in words, a sort of low acknowledging hum through the bond, and Hesper laughed.

"What did you say to him?"

"I told him the name of the stream we're crossing," Hesper said. "It's called Where the Grey One Drank. He said he didn't remember it." She stepped from stone to stone across the water. "I said that's all right. We do."

On the evening of the second day the pines opened.

---

It was a long clearing, longer than the yard at Greyhold, under the black pines, with the evening light coming down into it slant and gold. Grass. Mounds in the grass. Long low mounds, in rows, each one as long as a barn, and at the head of each one a stone. Grey stones, taller than a man, worn so smooth by the weather that they'd lost their corners and looked like something grown. No letters on them. No marks. Only grey stone, and lichen, and the grass going up the sides of the mounds.

Dozens. She counted forty in the first three rows and stopped counting.

Orrun stood at the edge of the clearing a long time.

Then he walked out among them.

She didn't follow. Hesper put a hand on her arm, lightly, and she didn't need it. She stood at the edge of the pines and watched him go down the first row slowly, putting his nose to the foot of each stone, one after another. Stopping. Going on.

She felt it through the bond. She'd thought it would be grief. It was something emptier than that, and worse.

*I don't know them,* he said.

He'd stopped by the tenth stone in the second row. He stood with his head down.

*I don't know any of them. I have smelled every one. They smell of me. Of my kind. Of the old earth.* A long pause. *And I don't know a single name.*

"Maybe you never knew them," said Maren, very quietly. "Maybe they were before you."

*Some.* He moved on to the next stone. *Not all. Some of them were here when I was here. I know that. I can feel how old the stone is.* He stopped again. *I was here. And they were here. And I don't remember.*

She didn't know what to say. She stood at the edge of the clearing with her hand on the rough bark of a pine.

*Three hundred years at the door,* said Orrun. *And before that, longer. And it took them out of me. The waiting. It took them out one by one, like stones out of a wall, and I didn't notice.*

---

He walked among them all night.

She sat with Hesper at the edge of the clearing with a small fire, and Hush lying between them, and watched him in the dark. He went down every row. He came back up. He stood a long time by some of the stones and a short time by others, and once, near midnight, he lay down by one at the far end and stayed there an hour, and she didn't know why that one.

Near dawn he came to the end of the last row.

There was a space there. At the end of the row, under the pines, where the next mound would have been. Grass, and no mound, and no stone.

He stood looking at it.

Then he lay down in it.

He lay down in the grass in the space at the end of the row, with his head on his paws and his eyes open, facing back along the row of graves toward the clearing and the dawn coming up gold through the pines.

Maren understood.

She understood all at once, coldly, the way you understand that the ice under your feet has gone. What the space was. Who it was for. Why there were no more mounds after it. She understood that he understood it too, and had walked all night down the rows to come to it, and had lain down in it to see if it fit.

She didn't say anything. There was nothing in the world to say.

Beside her, Hesper had seen it too. The Keeper sat very still. Then she reached out, without looking, and put her hand over Maren's on the grass, and kept it there.

After a while the sun came up over the pines, and Orrun got up out of the space, and came back to them, and lay down by the fire, and slept.

---

Hesper's knots wouldn't hold.

Maren saw it on the third morning, at Hesper's platform. The Keeper lived in an oak a day north of the Barrows, on a wide floor of split pine built across three great branches halfway up, with a roof of bark and a ladder of knotted rope. It was the first roof Maren had slept under since Liss's. In the morning Hesper sat at the edge of the platform with her legs over the side and took a length of grey cord from her belt and began to tie it.

A finding-knot, she said. For her path. She tied one every morning. You asked the ground where you needed to go that day and tied the asking into the cord, three turns and a twist and a word, and the knot told you, if you held it loose in your hand, by which way it pulled.

She tied it. Three turns, a twist, the word, soft. She held it up.

The turns slid. Slowly, as Maren watched, the knot came undone in her fingers, the cord unwinding itself like a snake uncoiling, until it hung from her hand in a plain grey string.

Hesper looked at it.

"That's the fourth day," she said. "It's never done that. Not once in thirty years." She tied it again. It held a moment longer, and then the turns slid again, and it was string.

She showed Maren the fold-ward on the platform's edge. A knot of red cord tied round the rail, every night, to keep the platform safe from anything that climbed. In the morning it hung loose. Not untied: loose, slack, the turns gone soft as wet string, as if all the strength had gone out of the cord in the night and left only the shape.

"It's as if the ground's being drunk," said Hesper. She looked east, through the trees, where the hum came from. "As if something's put its mouth to the ground and it's drinking. And everything that stood on what it's drinking is going slack."

She didn't say what. She didn't need to.

---

That afternoon Hush wouldn't hunt.

Maren watched it happen. The duskcat got up off the platform in the late afternoon and went down the trunk headfirst and set off east into the trees. It went fifty paces. Then it stopped, with one forefoot raised.

It stood like that a long time. Then it turned round and came back and went up the trunk and lay down by Hesper and put its chin on her knee.

"She won't go east," said Hesper. "Not for three days now. She goes north and she goes west and she goes south, and she comes back with nothing, because everything's gone east or gone away." She put her hand on the duskcat's head. "She won't go east."

"Why not?"

"Ask your lion," said Hesper.

---

Maren knew where it was. She'd known since the morning after the river. But it was Hesper who told her, that night, and it was the first time she'd heard it said in words.

"It came out of the west," Hesper said. "The morning after your river. The Plains' riders saw it go over. The size of a hill, they said, and the colour of a hill in winter, and flying so low the stones shook in the ground." She looked at the fire. "It went over the Thornwood like a cloud going over. It came down in the east, four days from here. On the Hollow Ring."

"What's the Hollow Ring?"

"Stones," said Hesper. "Old stones. In a clearing in the deep wood. Twelve of them in a ring, older than the Barrows, older than anything." She was quiet. "It's ours. It's the clans'. We hold it. Not as you'd hold a town. We keep it. We go there once a generation, at midsummer, and sing to it, and leave it alone. The owls nest in the stones." She paused. "Nobody knows what it's for. It's for itself."

"And it's lying on it."

"It's lying on it. It hasn't moved since it came down. Not once in five weeks." Hesper looked east, into the dark. "The Keepers watch it from a long way off. Nobody goes close. The elders haven't said. And the ground round the Ring is going grey."

"Grey?"

"Dead," said Hesper. "The trees round it. The grass. The ground. Going grey, from the Ring outward, a little more each week, like frost spreading on a pane." She turned her dark eyes on Maren. "And it's lying in the middle of it, drinking."

Through the bond, Maren felt Orrun, lying at the foot of the oak below them in the dark, go very still.

---

*I want to go,* he said.

She went down the ladder to him in the night. He was lying among the oak's great roots, with his head east.

*Now. I want to go now.*

"I know."

*It is my kin. It is four days away. It is lying in the middle of a dying wood, drinking, and I can feel it, all the time, and I am here, lying under a tree.* He didn't move. *I want to go.*

She sat down on a root beside him.

She thought of the beck in the Fellwood. *You are not my hand.* She thought of him lying in the space at the end of the row with his eyes open. She thought of everything she'd been taught in the yard and everything she hadn't been, and of how far two minds could be from each other and still share a life.

"The clans keep the Ring," she said. "It's theirs. They haven't said yet. Hesper says the elders will meet." She looked into the dark. "If we go now, without them, it's like walking into someone's house. It's like what the knights did to your door."

He was quiet.

"I'm asking you," said Maren. "I'm not telling you. I couldn't tell you if I wanted to. I'm asking. Wait for them."

He was quiet for a very long time. The hum went on under them in the dark, through the roots, through the ground, through both of them.

*I have waited before,* he said at last. *Three hundred years. Because I was told to. Because the old ones said keep the door and I kept it.* A pause. *I will wait now.*

She let out her breath.

*Not because you say,* said Orrun. *Because I choose it. This time I choose it.*

"That's what I meant," she said.

*I know it is.* He put his head down on his paws. *That is why I chose it.*

---

The Keepers came and went at Hesper's oak for a week, and everything they brought was rumour.

They came out of the trees at all hours, by ones and twos, men and women in patched grey cloaks with bare feet and cords at their belts and spears, some with a wolf at heel, one with a brown bear walking behind him as quietly as a dog. They came up the rope ladder and sat on the platform and ate Hesper's flatbread and talked in their own tongue, and looked at the lion below in the roots, and talked more, and Hesper told Maren after what they'd said.

The ground had shaken in the north of the Thornwood, three days' walk away. Twice. A long low shake, felt in the feet, gone in a moment. Nobody had seen why.

The deer were moving west, in ones and twos, a few each week, out of the eastern Thornwood, toward the Plains. And the boar. And the small things, the martens and the badgers. As if something had told them to go. As if a smell had come up out of the ground that nobody else could smell.

The hollow owls were restless in their stones at the Ring. They flew at noon, which they never did. They came back at night and sat in the stones and cried.

That was all. Rumour. Earth-shakes and restless beasts and owls crying at noon.

Nobody said *another*. Maren noticed that only afterwards, months afterwards, looking back. Nobody said it, because nobody thought it. There was one great beast, lying on the Ring, drinking the Thornwood grey. That was enough for anyone to think about. Nobody in the Thornwood that week, nobody in the world, thought that the ground might be shaking for any other reason.

---

On the thirty-eighth day a young Keeper came in from the west.

Corran, his name was, a boy of twenty with a fox-red beard and a hollow owl on his shoulder as tall as a child, pale, with great dark eyes that turned and turned. He'd been out to the western edge of the Plains with word for the horse clans, and he'd come back with word from them, passed rider to rider along the grass.

Hesper translated. Maren half listened. Riders gathering. The elders called. Five days.

Then Corran said something, and Hesper stopped, and looked at Maren.

"He says the horse clans saw a dragon," she said. "A family of them, over the Scarfell trading for wool, saw it from the high moor. Over the Marches. A red dragon, going south, alone. With a man on its back."

Maren didn't move.

Corran said something else.

"Going south fast," said Hesper. "Down the river valley. Toward the smoke." She was watching Maren's face. "They thought you'd want to know. The horse clans. They said the lion-woman would want to know about the dragon."

Maren looked down through the platform's edge at the lion in the roots below.

*The red one,* said Orrun. *Kharrak.* A pause. *He's riding the red one.*

She thought of the farmer on a ledge with his hand on an old dragon's jaw. She thought of him on the red one's back, going south, alone, toward smoke. She could think of a dozen reasons for that and none of them good.

"Thank him," she said to Hesper. "Thank Corran. Tell him I'm glad to know."

She didn't say the farmer's name. She found she didn't want to say it here, in front of strangers, in a language that wasn't his. She kept it.

---

On the fortieth day Hesper sat down beside her on the platform's edge and said the elders would meet in five days, on the Plains, at the Speaking Stones, and that Hesper would take her.

"They'll want to see him," she said. "And you."

"What will they ask?"

"What he is. What he wants. What he'll do." Hesper swung her bare feet over the drop. "They know what he is. They'll ask anyway. That's how the clans do things. You ask what you know, out loud, in front of everyone, so it's been said." She looked sideways at Maren. "And they'll ask what you are to him."

"What will you tell them?"

Hesper thought about it.

"That he lets you sit with him," she said. "That's enough. For the clans, that's everything."

Below them, in the roots, the lion lay with his head east, waiting, because he'd chosen to. Maren sat on the edge of the Keeper's platform with her feet over the drop and the hum coming up through the oak into her, and looked east too.
