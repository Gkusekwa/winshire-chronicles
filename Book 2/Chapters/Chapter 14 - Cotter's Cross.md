Kharrak came down at dusk in the Ashlings, and Calder slid off his neck into a dry gully full of thorn and sat there a while with his hands shaking.

Not from fear. From the flying. Most of a day on the red dragon's neck, though the Cross was a morning off: twice he'd made Kharrak come down to let the tide go out of them both, and at the last they'd lain up in a fold of the hills till the light was going. The wind in his face, and the red tide coming up under him every time they caught a warm draught over a hill, and his own self pushing it down every time, and his hands on the ridge of Kharrak's neck so tight they'd gone white. He sat in the gully and looked at his hands until they stopped.

*You'll get used to it,* said Kharrak.

"Will I?"

*No.* The red dragon lay down along the bottom of the gully with his chin on a rock. *Dag never did. He just liked it more.*

The Ashlings were scrub woods, low and grey and thorny, on the dry country north-east of Cotter's Cross where nothing grew taller than a man. Two leagues of it between the gully and the town. You couldn't hide a dragon in the Cross. You could hide one here, in a gully, if he lay still and didn't light anything.

*I'll lie still,* said Kharrak. *I'll not promise about the lighting.*

---

He sent Lisska over the town at dusk.

She went up out of the gully in a green flicker and was gone toward the smoke. He sat and waited in the dark with his back to Kharrak's flank, which was warm, like sitting against an oven wall, and felt her through the bond, going away, a bright quick thread getting thinner. Then, after a while, getting thicker again.

She came down in a scatter of thorns.

*Fires,* she said. *Everywhere. In the yards. In the street. On the roofs. Men sleeping in the beast pens, in the straw, with the beasts.* She shook herself. *It smells. It smells of men and ale and sick.*

"How many?"

*Thousands. I couldn't count. I tried to count and I got to four hundred and they kept moving.* She was quiet. *And there's a sign on the big inn, over the door, with a ram on it. And they've painted the six on it. Six dots, round the ram's head, white.*

"The Fleece."

*And upstairs, the shutters are shut. All the shutters.* Another pause. *And Calder. In the hiring yard. Under a yellow flag with a tower on it. Sitting on a barrel, eating an onion.*

"Who?"

*The one from the Ewe Hollow turn. Who talked them down. Who owes you his legs.* Lisska put her head on one side. *Brask.*

---

He walked in at dawn with the hood up.

He'd done it every market day of his life. Down the Way from Harrow's End in the dark on the wain, with the barley or the wool, and in at the north gate with the other drovers as the sky went grey, and you didn't look at anybody and nobody looked at you. He did it now. He'd borrowed a crook off a shepherd's hut in the Ashlings that had nobody in it. The grey Clan cloak was the same colour as half the cloaks in the Marches. He walked in at the north gate behind a man with a cartload of turnips and a boy driving six goats, and nobody looked at him.

He'd never seen the Cross like this.

It had always been rough. A market town where the Drover's Way met the Saltreach Track, inns and a beast pen and a hiring yard where sellswords sat on the wall waiting for contracts, and nobody kept the peace but whoever had paid most recently. He'd sold barley here twenty autumns. He'd been robbed here twice and cheated more times than he could count. It had always been rough.

Now it was full.

Men in every street. Men sleeping in doorways in their cloaks, men sitting on the steps of houses that weren't theirs, men round fires in the middle of the road with their boots off. Company coats on all of them: the red hand, the black ship, a green boar, a blue fish, the yellow tower. Crossbows stacked against walls. Pikes leaning in corners. And over all of it, on every door and every post and every wall, the bills.

Reede's bills. The same as the one in his coat. The ring of six at the top and the big black letters under it, pasted up so thick in places that new ones had gone up over old ones and the wind was peeling them back in layers.

At the bottom of the main street, the Fleece.

The biggest inn in the Cross, three storeys, with a gallery round the yard. Over the door hung the sign he'd seen every autumn since he was a boy, a fat white ram on a green ground. Somebody had painted six white dots round the ram's head, in a ring.

The shutters on the top floor were closed. All of them.

---

The beast pen was across the square from the Fleece, and there was a board nailed to its gate.

New. The paint was barely dry. Men were standing in front of it in a crowd, reading it, or having it read to them, and talking. He stood at the back of the crowd with his hood up and looked at it.

He couldn't read it. He could see the mark at the top, painted in blue: a wave, curling over, and under the wave, a coin. And under that, big letters, and numbers. He knew numbers. A one and four noughts.

"Ten thousand," said a voice at his elbow.

He didn't turn round.

"Ten thousand silver," said Coll Brask, quietly, eating an onion. "The great beast of the Marches. Alive." He took a bite. "That's the Tide Council's mark. Out of Corsa. The wave and the coin. They've put up contracts like it in every town on the Track. Somebody's paying for it." He chewed. "Means the lion, if you were wondering. Every company in the Cross has read it twice."

Calder looked at the board.

He thought of a woman on a moor, walking east, with the heather moving behind her where there wasn't any wind.

"Morning, Brask," he said.

"Morning, farmer," said Coll Brask. "You've a hundred crowns on your head and you've walked into a town of three thousand men who haven't been paid since the spring. I'd say that was brave, if I thought you'd thought about it."

---

There were men selling charms on the pen rail.

Half a dozen of them, sitting along the top rail of the beast pen with trays round their necks, calling out. Calder had seen them every market day of his life: the charm-sellers, with their tin discs and knotted cords and little bags of herbs. *Warranted*, they'd shout. *Warranted against the fever, against the foot-rot, against the evil eye.* Saltreach sold charms the way it sold everything. Some of them had even worked, once, a little, when the ground was better.

These were new.

Tin discs, stamped with a shape. A great beast, wings out. Hung on red cord. *Warranted against the walker,* the sellers called. *Against the earth-shakes. Against the great one coming back. Warranted, warranted, one copper, keep it on you and it'll pass you by.*

Men were buying them. Big hard men in company coats with crossbows on their backs, buying tin discs for a copper and hanging them round their necks and tucking them under their shirts.

Calder bought one. He didn't know why. To show Ada, he thought. He held it in his hand on the way out of the square and it was nothing. Cold tin. A shape stamped in it by somebody who'd never seen the thing they were stamping.

He showed it to Lisska that night in the gully.

She put her nose to it and sniffed.

*It smells of nothing,* she said. *Not even tin, really. Nothing.*

"No word on it?"

*There's a word stamped on the back. But it's not a word.* She sniffed again. *It's just the shape of one.*

---

Brask told him about the army in the hiring yard, sitting on a barrel under the yellow tower with the onion finished and a heel of bread instead.

"Three thousand," he said. "Near enough. Every company that stood on the hill above your village on the morning that thing went up. And never paid. Not a copper. The paymaster's chest went off south with Delling, and the paymaster's dead on a spit, and the man who hired us all is hanging in chains on a gate in Hearthmere." He chewed. "And the other one's upstairs."

He jerked his chin at the Fleece across the square. At the closed shutters.

"Came up out of the ground," said Brask. "That's what they say. The morning after. By an old stair under the Ruin that nobody knew about, all over mud, and walked south down the Way on his own, and came in here two days after, and sat down upstairs, and sent for the captains." He tore the bread. "And they went. Every one. And came down again and told their companies: wait. The silver's coming. Saltreach silver, up the Track from the coast, enough for all of us and back pay besides." He shrugged. "So we wait."

"And when it comes?"

Brask looked at him.

"You don't know? Every tavern in the Cross is saying it out loud." He leaned back against the wall of the hiring yard. "When the silver comes, we cross the Wend. Into Hearthmere. Into dead ground." He said it flatly. "No wards over there now. No words. No battle-charms, no lamp-light, no warding on the town gates. The knights have got their beasts and their steel and nothing else. We've got steel and three thousand." He shrugged. "Cross at the fords below Greyhold, go west, take the Candle Downs. Their whole kingdom's grain. They can't stop it. They've nothing to stop it with. And the man upstairs says it's justice, because it's their lamps that killed their own ground, and he's right."

"Is he?"

"About the lamps?" Brask looked at the bills on the wall. "Yes. About the rest, I don't care. I'm paid to fight, not to care." He paused. "I'm not paid. That's the thing. I'm owed."

---

Delling saw him on the second day, in the back room of the Plough.

Not the Fleece. Delling wouldn't go into the Fleece, Brask said, because the Fleece was the man upstairs's house now and Delling liked to sit in his own. The Plough was a low dark alehouse at the back of the hiring yard with a back room that smelled of tallow and old beer, and Delling sat in it at a table with his back to the wall and a lamp at his elbow and an account book open in front of him.

He was older than Calder had thought. Sixty. Heavy, soft, with a soft voice and small careful hands and a pair of spectacles on a black cord round his neck that he put on to look at the book and took off to look at Calder. A yellow tower was stitched on his coat over the heart. He didn't look like a sellsword. He looked like a factor. Like the grain factors who came over on Liss's ferry every autumn and bought Calder's barley and paid him less than it was worth and wrote it down.

"The barley farmer," he said. "Sit down."

Calder sat.

Delling took the spectacles off.

"I know what you want," he said. "Brask told me. I'd have known anyway." He reached under the table and brought something up and laid it on top of the account book. "This."

It was a book. Black-bound, the size of the hymnal at Harrow's End shrine, swollen at the edges with damp, the leather gone soft and grey at the corners. It had a strap round it with a brass buckle.

Calder didn't move.

"Rook's," said Delling. "He kept it in the paymaster's chest. Every bag of silver he paid out for a year and a half and what for and who to, in a clerk's hand you could read in the dark." He put his small soft hand flat on it. "I've read it. Cover to cover. Twice. I can read, farmer. I keep my company's books myself; nobody robs me but me." He paused. "I know what's in it. I know what you want out of it. I know who's named."

"Then you know why I want it."

"I know why you want it." Delling put the spectacles on again and looked at him through them, and took them off. "Here's my price."

He said it as the factors said it, every autumn. As if it were already written down.

"You."

---

Calder sat very still.

"There's a man upstairs at the Fleece," Delling said, "who'll pay more for the barley farmer with three dragons in his head than anyone in this world will ever pay for a damp book. He hasn't got the silver yet. But when it comes, he'll pay it for you before he pays my men. I know him. I've known him two years." He folded his hands on the book. "I hand you up those stairs, I get four months' pay for my company, and the man upstairs gets a halter for three dragons. That's my price. I'm telling you straight, because I don't cheat at the table. Only off it."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you walk out of here, and you go home without the book." Delling shrugged. "I'll not stop you. You came in alone with your hood up, you'll go out the same way. I don't hand men up stairs by force. It's bad for business. Nobody'd sit at my table again." He put the spectacles on. "But you'll not have the book."

Calder looked at it. Black, swollen, with its brass buckle. A hand's breadth from his own hand on the table.

"I'll think about it," he said.

"Think, then," said Delling. "I'm here. So's the book. So's the man upstairs."

He watched the Cross for four days.

He didn't know what else to do. He walked the streets with his hood up and his crook, among the drovers and the goat-boys and the three thousand, and slept in the gully at night with his back to Kharrak, and every day he came back and watched.

He watched them drill. In the hiring yard, in the mornings, the captains had the companies out in lines with pikes and crossbows, under the ring of six painted on a sheet hung from the hiring-yard wall. They drilled badly, most of them, like men who knew they weren't being paid to. Some drilled well. The black ship company drilled like a machine, and so did the yellow tower, Delling's, and Calder watched them and thought of the crossbows in the gorse at the ford.

He saw Reede. On the third day, through the arch into the Fleece's back yard, a little round man with ink to his elbows and spectacles pushed up on his forehead, working at a press on a cart. A big iron screw-press with a wooden frame, and a boy feeding paper into it, and Reede pulling the bar down and lifting it, down and lifting, and the bills coming out of it one after another, black and wet. He'd been at Millbrook, Calder knew. Maren had seen him there, in the autumn, at the Kettle. A travelling bookbinder.

He saw the shutters open once. On the third night, late, with the yard below full of fires. One shutter on the top floor of the Fleece, folded back, and lamplight behind it, and a man standing at the window looking down. Lean. Still. Only a shape against the lamp. He stood there a long time looking down at the fires and the men round them, three thousand men who'd come to his call, and then he shut the shutter.

Calder stood in the dark of an alley across the square and didn't breathe until it was shut.

---

Everybody said *Vale*. And stopped.

He heard it a dozen times a day. In the alehouses, in the street, round the fires. A man would start to say it, the name, and get halfway, and stop, and look round, as if he'd remembered something. Then he'd say *the one upstairs* instead. Or *him*.

On the fourth day two men fought about it outside the Plough. A big man from the green boar company and a little one from the blue fish. They'd been drinking. The big one said Vale was dead, he'd seen the body himself, hanging on Greyhold's gate in chains with a board round its neck, he'd been over the river to look at it before they shut the crossings. The little one said Vale was upstairs at the Fleece and he'd seen him too, alive, giving the captains their orders.

"There's two," said a third man, pulling them apart. "Gods, there's two. Everybody knows there was two. One's on the gate and one's upstairs."

"Which one was it, then?" said the big one. "On the Tidesteps? At Wendmouth? Which one did we follow?"

Nobody answered that.

---

The ground shook on the sixth day, at noon, in the hiring yard.

Calder was sitting on the wall with Brask, watching the yellow tower drill. He felt it first in the wall, under him. A long slow shudder. Then in his feet. Then everyone felt it: the drilling stopped, the lines broke, men looked down at the cobbles, and along the side of the yard a cartload of barrels began to walk, one after another, rocking on their rims with a hollow knocking sound, and a stack of pikes leaning against a wall slid down with a clatter.

It went on. It went on longer than a cart going past, longer than anything. Then it stopped.

The yard was quiet.

"There," said a crossbowman near the wall. "That's it again. Felt it last week too, in the night."

"Where's it from?"

"East." The crossbowman nodded at the eastern wall of the yard, over which there was nothing but sky. "Far east. Out past the Scarfell. Past the edge of anything." He spat. "Wyldmarch, my mother's people'd say. Something walking."

"Something lying down," said somebody else.

They went back to drilling, slower.

That night in the gully Calder asked Lisska.

*The lion's way,* she said at once. *That way. Where she went.* She was quiet. *Where it went too.*

Kharrak, lying along the gully with his chin on the rock, didn't say anything. But Calder felt him, through the bond, turn his head a little toward the east in the dark.

---

It was Brask who thought of the road.

He came out to the gully on the seventh night. Calder hadn't asked him to. Lisska saw him coming through the scrub a long way off, a man alone with a lantern held low, and Calder went up the side of the gully to meet him and brought him down.

Brask stopped at the bottom when he saw Kharrak.

The red dragon opened one gold eye and looked at him.

"Evening," said Brask, after a while, to the dragon.

Kharrak shut the eye.

Brask sat down on a rock as far from the red dragon as the gully allowed. He put the lantern down and looked at it.

"Delling wants out," he said.

Calder waited.

"Of all of it. The muster. The man upstairs. The Wend. Hearthmere." Brask rubbed his face. "He's not said it. He'll not say it. But he wants out. He's sixty and he's owed four months and he doesn't believe there's any silver coming up the Track from anywhere, because he's been in this trade forty years and he's never once seen silver come that was promised by a man who wasn't paying yet." He looked up. "He'd take the company home tomorrow. If he could get it there."

"So why doesn't he?"

"Because the man upstairs hangs deserters." Brask said it flatly. "On the Wendmouth road. Three, last week. Out of the black ship company, who tried to walk home in the night. They're on the gibbet at the second milestone, if you want to go and look. And the river's watched, both ways. And west is Hearthmere." He picked up a stone and turned it over. "There's one road out of the Cross that the man upstairs doesn't hold."

He looked north. Into the dark, toward the Scarfell.

"North and east. Over the moor. Down Skarrow's side to the coast. Through Clan land." He turned the stone over again. "Nobody crosses Clan land without the Clan's leave. Not two hundred armed men. Not even in a war. The ledges would come down on them." He looked at Calder. "You're Clan now, they say. They say you came back up the Way with the old one and the chief let you in."

"At a price."

"Everything's at a price." Brask put the stone down. "Give Delling a road home. Across the moor, safe, in the Clan's name. He'll give you the book. I'd stake my legs on it, and they're yours already." He paused. "He'd rather be home than rich. We all would. We just never get the choice."

---

Calder sat in the gully a long time after Brask had gone back.

He knew who was in Delling's company. He'd known since Brask first said *the yellow tower*. He knew from Rook's own mouth, under the arch: *the crossbow in the hedge at the bottom of the field, one of Delling's.* And from Hallam's ford, the second time: sixty crossbows in the gorse on the slope, plain grey, no thread, and the bolt that took the old man off the hill as he came down with his stick. Delling's.

Some of the men drilling under the yellow tower every morning had been in that gorse. Some of them had shot at Hallam. One of them had shot Hallam.

He was going to give them a road home.

*They shot the old man,* said Kharrak.

He hadn't known the red dragon was awake. Kharrak lay along the bottom of the gully with his chin on the rock and his eyes shut, and the words came out of him short and hard, the way they always did.

*Hallam. On the hill. And at the mill. The hedge. Dag went off my back that night.* A pause. *And you'll give them a road.*

"For the book." He looked at the dark. "For what's in it. For Teo. For the name of the one in the hedge. For proof, so Arn will hear it and Ulric will come and look." He heard his own voice, flat. "If I don't give them a road, I don't get the book, and nobody ever believes the barley man, and the war goes on burning my village till there's nothing left to burn. If I do, two hundred men who shot at us go home to Wendmouth and sit by their fires."

*I'd burn them,* said Kharrak. *Tonight. In their yard. And take the book off the fat one's table.*

"I know you would."

*You won't.*

"No."

The red dragon was quiet a long time.

*That's why you'll have the book,* he said at last, *and I've got a gully.* He shifted his chin on the rock. *It's not right, farmer. What you're doing. It's not right.* A pause. *It's not nothing, either.*

---

He went back to the Plough on the eighth day.

Delling was at the same table, with the same lamp, with the account book open and the black book on top of it.

Calder sat down.

"Not me," he said.

Delling took off his spectacles.

"A road," said Calder. "Home. For your company. All of it. North over the Scarfell from the Ashlings, across the moor, and down Skarrow's side to the coast and along it to Wendmouth. In the Clan's name. Safe." He kept his voice steady. "No burning. No taking. Not a hen. Three parties, a day apart, so the moor can feed you. The riders of the ledges will see you over. Nobody'll touch you. Nobody from the Clan and nobody from upstairs, because the man upstairs doesn't hold the moor and never will."

Delling looked at him.

"In the Clan's name," he said. "You can give that?"

"I'm Clan."

"That's not what I asked."

Calder didn't answer.

Delling put the spectacles on and looked at him through them. Then he took them off and folded them and put them in his coat.

"You've not asked your chief," he said.

"No."

"He'll not like it."

"No."

"Two hundred men who've been fighting his people all winter, walking across his moor under his flag." Delling almost smiled. "You'll pay for that, farmer."

"Everything's at a price," said Calder. "Somebody told me."

Delling looked at him a moment longer.

Then he put his small soft hand on the black book and pushed it across the table, slowly, over the account book and the tallow-stains and the rings left by a hundred cups, until it was in front of Calder.

"Done," he said.

---

Calder put his hand on it.

It was cold. Damp. The leather soft as an old glove. He couldn't read a word of what was in it and it was the heaviest thing he'd ever touched.

He got up with it. He put it inside his coat, against his chest, with the knife and the ring and the folded bill and the tin charm and the stone with the white ring round it.

At the door, Delling spoke again.

"Some of mine were in the gorse," he said. "At the ford. The second time. You know that."

Calder stopped with his hand on the latch.

"I know."

"One of mine put the bolt in the old man."

"I know."

"And you'll still give them a road."

"I've said."

Delling sat back against the wall in the tallow-light, with his small hands on his account book, and looked at the farmer in the doorway.

"Then you're a fool," he said. "Or you're something I haven't got a word for. Forty years in this trade and I haven't got a word for it." He picked up his pen. "Go on. Before the man upstairs hears you've been in."

---

He went out of the Cross before dawn on the ninth day, by the north gate, with the drovers, with his hood up and the crook in his hand and the book against his chest.

Nobody looked at him.

He walked the two leagues through the Ashlings in the grey, fast, and came down into the gully as the sun came up, and Kharrak was standing up waiting for him with his wings half open, and Lisska was on a rock above, bright as a coal.

*You've got it,* said Lisska. *I can feel you've got it. You're all heavy on one side.*

"I've got it."

*What did you pay?*

He looked back toward the Cross, where the smoke was going up into the morning from three thousand fires, and the shutters on the top floor of the Fleece were shut.

"A road," he said. "To the wrong men."

Brask was going with Delling's first party, the next day, north over the moor. He'd said so, at the Plough's door, shaking Calder's hand. *Somebody's got to show them which way's north. They're sellswords. They don't know.*

Calder climbed onto the red dragon's neck with the book inside his coat and his hand flat on it, and Kharrak went up, straight up, the gully dropping away, the Ashlings going grey and small, the Cross a smudge of smoke to the south-west.

They turned north for the Scarfell.

Behind them, under the Cross, faint, so faint he wasn't sure he felt it, the ground shook once more, from the east.
