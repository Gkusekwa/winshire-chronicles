---

The Speaking Stones stood on the Plains a day west of the Thornwood, on a long low rise with nothing round it but grass.

Seven of them. Taller than the stones on the graves, taller than two men, grey and lichened and leaning a little, all of them, the same way, as if a wind had been pushing at them for a thousand years. They stood in a horseshoe, open to the east. In the mouth of the horseshoe, flat in the grass, there was an eighth stone, long and smooth, the size of a table.

The clans came in for two days.

Maren sat with Hesper in the grass outside the horseshoe and watched them come. The horse clans first, on the great elk, out of the north and the south, riders in leather and fur with their hair in plaits and their faces painted with red clay in bars across the cheekbones, and the elk walking slow with their heads up and their antlers like winter trees against the sky. Then the forest clans, on foot, out of the Thornwood: Keepers in patched grey, and others in green and brown, with wolves at heel, and once a family with two brown bears walking behind them like dogs. Then, on the second day, the river clans, small dark people who came across the Plains from the south-east carrying their boats on their heads: round hide boats, light as baskets, that they'd paddled up the streams from Lake Ysse and carried over the last league.

Four hundred, Hesper said. Perhaps more. Sitting in the grass in a great loose ring round the outside of the horseshoe, in families, with their beasts lying beside them.

Nobody came near Orrun.

He lay a little way off in the grass, alone, with his head up. They looked at him. All of them, all the time, the whole two days. The children stared. The adults looked, and looked away, and looked again. Nobody spoke to him. Nobody came within thirty paces.

Once, on the first evening, a very small girl from the river clans got away from her mother and walked across the grass to him on her fat legs and stood in front of him and looked up. He looked down at her. Then he put his nose down and breathed on her, once, gently, so that her hair blew back.

She laughed. Her mother came running and snatched her up and carried her away, and didn't look back, and the whole ring of people round the Stones was so quiet you could hear the wind in the grass.

---

The elders came into the horseshoe on the morning of the third day.

Three of them. They didn't sit on the stones. They sat on the grass, at the closed end of the horseshoe, with their backs to the tallest stone, and their beasts behind them.

Elder Aun of the horse clans was the oldest woman Maren had ever seen. White hair to the ground in two plaits, and a face so creased the creases had creases. She was blind. Her eyes were white as milk, and a girl of ten led her in by the hand and sat her down and sat beside her.

Elder Morwen of the forest clans, Hesper's own, was big and slow and heavy, with a broad brown face and a brown bear lying behind her in the grass like a hill. Elder Tolly-Bright of the river clans was small and quick and dark and laughed at things, Maren noticed, that nobody else seemed to think were funny.

"Now," said Hesper, and got up, and Maren got up with her.

---

"We ask what we know," said Elder Aun.

Her voice was thin and dry and carried right round the ring. She spoke the clans' tongue, and Hesper said it after her softly in Maren's ear, a breath behind.

"We ask it out loud, at the Stones, in front of everyone, so it's been said, and nobody can say after that they didn't hear." The white eyes turned toward where Maren stood. They didn't see her. They found her anyway. "So. Lowlander. Knight-that-was. What is he?"

Maren looked at Hesper. Hesper nodded.

"The grey-maned," Maren said. She said it in her own tongue and Hesper said it after her in the clans'. "Grandfather."

A murmur went round the ring.

"What does he want?"

"To be near his kin." She didn't have to think about it. "The one on the Ring. He's been feeling it since the morning it flew. He wants to be near it."

"Why?"

"I don't know," said Maren. "I don't think he knows. It's his kin. It's the only one of his kind for a thousand leagues. He was its door-keeper, I think, once. A long time ago."

"What will he do?"

Maren was quiet.

"I don't know," she said at last. "Neither does he."

The murmur went round the ring again, different. Elder Tolly-Bright laughed, a short sharp bark, and Morwen looked at her, and she stopped.

Elder Aun's face didn't change.

"And you," she said. "What are you to him?"

Maren opened her mouth.

Hesper spoke first. Not in Maren's ear. Out loud, in the clans' tongue, to the elders and the ring, and then, a breath after, in Maren's own, so she'd know what had been said for her.

"He lets her sit with him."

---

There was a long silence.

Then Elder Aun laughed.

It was a dry, thin, cracked sound, like a stick breaking. She put her old hand on the girl beside her and laughed until she had to wipe her white eyes.

"That," she said, when she could, "is the first true thing a lowlander has said at the Stones in my lifetime." She wiped her eyes again. "And it wasn't even the lowlander who said it."

The ring laughed. Not all of it. Enough.

"Bring him," said Aun. "Bring the grey-maned in. I want to feel him."

---

Maren didn't have to bring him.

He got up out of the grass before she'd turned round, and came through the ring, and they made way for him, four hundred people shuffling back on their knees and their beasts lifting their heads. He walked into the mouth of the horseshoe and stopped at the flat stone. He looked at it. Then he lay down on it.

The Stones hummed.

Maren heard it. Everybody heard it. Not through the bond: with her ears. A low note, under the wind, very deep, coming out of the seven stones all at once, like the note a great bell makes long after it's been struck, when you can feel it more than hear it. It went on. The elk shifted their feet and threw up their heads. All round the ring, the bears lay down flat in the grass with their chins on their paws. The wolves whined.

Beside Maren, Hesper had put her palm flat on the grass. "The Grey Mother keeps the stones," she said, under her breath. "That's what we say when the ground shakes."

Orrun lay on the flat stone with his eyes half shut, and the Stones hummed round him.

Elder Aun got up. The girl helped her. She came across the grass on the girl's arm, slowly, a few steps at a time, to the flat stone, and stopped beside the lion, so close her white plaits brushed his mane. She didn't seem afraid. Perhaps, Maren thought, when you were blind you couldn't be afraid of what was big; you could only be afraid of what was close, and everything was close.

She put her hand on the flat stone, by his foreleg.

She stood like that a long time, with her head on one side.

"There," she said. "They know him." She took her hand away. "The Stones know him. They've known him a long time."

She went back to her place on the girl's arm and sat down, and the humming went on, slowly fading, under everything that was said after.

---

The clans' answer came at dusk. Aun gave it, and Hesper said it after her, word for word.

"The great beast on the Hollow Ring is kin. As every beast is kin. As the grey-maned is kin, and the elk, and the bear, and the owl in the stone." The old voice carried round the ring in the red evening. "You don't hunt kin. You don't chain kin. You don't drive kin from the place where it has lain down, even if it lies down on your mother's grave." She paused. "The clans will keep watch. We will keep our people out of the grey. We will do nothing else."

"Nothing?" said Maren.

Hesper didn't translate it. She didn't need to. Aun's white eyes turned toward her.

"What would you have us do, knight-that-was? Fight it?"

"No." Maren didn't know what she'd have them do. "But it's drinking the forest. Hesper says the grey is spreading. If it lies there long enough..."

"Then the forest will be grey," said Aun. "And we will be sad, and we will sing for it, and when it goes, the forest will come back. Slowly. The forest always comes back." She folded her hands. "We have seen beasts come and go for longer than your kingdom has had a name. We are not going to start hunting them now."

"And if the kingdoms come?" Maren said. "With chains?"

It was Elder Morwen who answered, heavy and slow, with the bear behind her.

"They'll come," she said. "They always come. They came for our duskcats and our elk and our sleepwood and our stonehorns. They'll come for this." She looked at the lion on the flat stone. "Then we'll see."

---

Lisska came on the forty-eighth day, at noon, out of the west.

Maren saw her first as a speck. Then as a green spark very high up, catching the sun. Then she was coming down, in a long fast slant over the grass, crying, and every elk-rider on the Plains round the Stones had a bow strung and an arrow on it before Maren had got to her feet.

"No," Maren said. "No. She's his."

Elder Aun, in the horseshoe, said one word. The bows came down.

Lisska landed in the grass beside Orrun in a skid that threw up turf, and folded her wings, and stood there with her sides going in and out, looking round at four hundred people and their beasts as if she'd only just noticed them.

Then she looked at Maren.

Maren couldn't hear her. She knew that. She'd known it since the island: the green dragon's voice went into Calder's head and into the lion's, as far kin, and nobody else's. She stood in the grass in front of the dragon who'd sat on the ferry-house roof and on the wall at Millbrook and flown over the Fellwood crying with joy, and couldn't hear a word.

Through the bond, slow, she felt Orrun listening.

*She says,* he said at last, *she has come from the farmer.*

---

It took a long time.

Lisska talked fast. Maren could see her talking: her head going, her eyes bright, her wings half lifting and settling. And Orrun gave it to Maren slow, in his own words, a piece at a time, with long gaps where he was listening to the next piece. Like a man reading a letter aloud to someone who couldn't read, Maren thought. Stopping. Finding the place. Going on.

*The farmer has bought a book.*

"The ledger. Rook's."

*She says it is a dead man's book with every coin in it.* A pause. *He bought it with a promise.*

"What promise?"

A long gap. Lisska talking and talking.

*A road,* said Orrun. *For bad men. Men who shot at the old man on the hill. He has given them a road home across the moor, to get the book. She says he did not like it. She says the red one wanted to burn them and he said no.* A pause. *She says the red one is still cross about it.*

Maren stood in the grass and thought about that for a while.

"And his family?"

*In the warm caves. Above the black water. All of them.* Another gap. *The old one's wing is knitting. Slowly. She says the old one walked out to the end of the ledge on her own last week, and everybody cheered.*

"And Wren?"

Lisska's head came round at the name. She said something, fast, and her wings came up.

*The child rode the grey one all the way to the caves,* said Orrun. *She would not get down. And she gave the stone to her father.* A pause. *The one you found on the edge.*

Maren didn't say anything.

*He keeps it in his coat,* said Orrun. *With a knife and a ring. The green one says he does not know what it is for.* A pause. *She says she told him nobody knows what it's for, and that is why it's the right stone.*

"She said that?"

*The child said it. The green one is saying what the child said.* Another pause. *Or what she thinks the child said. She talks very fast.*

"What will he do with the book?"

Lisska said something short.

*Have it read,* said Orrun. *He cannot read.*

---

Hesper sent the moss that evening.

She came across from the Keepers' fires with a hide bag the size of two fists, tied at the neck, and knelt in the grass in front of Lisska, who looked at her with great suspicion.

"Keepers' moss," Hesper said to Maren. She opened the bag and showed her: grey-green, soft, damp, smelling of stone and rain. "We put it in wounds. It closes them. On good ground it closes a cut in a night. On poor ground, slower." She tied the bag shut again, and then took a length of green cord from her belt and began to knot it round the bag's neck. Three turns, a twist, and a word, soft. "This tells it what it's for. So it'll wait in the bag till it's put in a wound and then it'll know." She looked at the knot. It held. Out here, on the Plains, a day from the forest and a week from the grey, it held. "Tell your farmer: for the old one's chest. Wet it in clean water, pack it in, and leave it. Don't keep looking. Moss doesn't like being looked at."

Maren said it to Orrun. Orrun said it to Lisska. Lisska listened with her head on one side.

Then Hesper tied the bag round the green dragon's neck, on its cord, under her jaw, where it would ride against her throat in flight. Lisska stood very still and let her. When Hesper's hands came away, she put her nose down and sniffed the Keeper's hair.

*She says thank you,* said Orrun. *She says it smells like the woods.*

"Tell her it is the woods," said Hesper.

Lisska went at dusk, west, low over the grass at first and then climbing, a green spark in the red sky, with the moss under her chin, until she was gone.

---

The kingdoms came on the fifty-first day.

From the west first. Maren saw them from the rise of the Stones in the middle of the morning: a dark line on the grass, a long way off, coming east. By noon she could see what it was.

Knights.

Thirty of them, perhaps, on greyharts, the tall white deer of the knights' stables, with the hounds running at their heels and two falcons up over them, turning in the sky. Greyhold's banner at the head of the column. And behind the knights, slower, six great wains drawn by oxen, eight to a wain, and on each wain a heap of something that caught the sun.

She knew what it was before she could see it.

The chain from the reeds, and more. She'd seen it on its wain below Redwatch on the morning after the river, forty fathoms of ordinary iron; the crown had had it lengthened since, and its links recast at Greyhold's forge, every link now as long as a man's body, bright iron still, wound in heaps on the six wains like a great sleeping snake. And a hundred labourers walking beside the wains with picks and stakes and mallets over their shoulders.

At the head of the column, on a plain grey horse among the greyharts, a small neat woman with iron-grey hair cut short.

Rowan.

Behind her, Maren could see the shape of a man with two hounds running at his heels, a dark one and a pale one. Wynn. Ash and Sorrow.

She stood on the rise with her hand on Haverly's hilt and watched them come across the grass toward the Thornwood, toward the grey, toward the thing lying on the Ring, with a chain.

---

They camped at the Thornwood's edge, in sight of the Stones.

Rowan sent a rider to the elders. A young knight Maren didn't know, with his helm under his arm and his face red, who came up the rise on his greyhart and stopped outside the horseshoe and asked, in the common tongue, very correctly, for passage through the Thornwood to the Hollow Ring for the knights of Hearthmere, in the crown's name.

Hesper said it in the clans' tongue for the elders.

Elder Aun said something short.

"The Thornwood isn't ours to give passage through," Hesper told the knight. "It's its own. Nobody gives passage through it. And nobody should go."

The young knight looked at her, and at the elders, and at the lion lying in the grass, and at Maren standing beside the lion in a river-watch coat with Haverly's sword at her hip.

He looked at Maren for a long time.

Then he turned his greyhart and rode back down to the camp.

Rowan went in on the fifty-third day anyway. Maren watched the column go: the knights first, in single file on the forest path, then the wains, the oxen straining, the labourers cutting the path wider as they went with axes, so that the sound of axes went in under the trees ahead of them and on and on all day. Rowan didn't look up at the Stones once.

---

Isvaren's Wardens came on the fifty-fourth day, from the north.

They came differently. No banner. No column. They came across the Plains in a loose crowd, in robes of blue and grey, on tall shaggy mountain horses. A dozen of them. And at their heels, snow lynx, white, with tufted ears and great soft feet, that ran in the grass like ghosts.

Three birds flew over them. Not falcons. Bigger. Long-winged, grey and white, with forked tails that streamed behind them, and Maren, watching them come in over the Plains on a high wind, thought she could see light crackling faintly along the edges of their wings.

"Stormkites," said Hesper. "Isvaren's. They fly in front of storms."

Behind the riders came mules. Six of them, each with a tall load lashed upright on its back, wrapped in sacking and roped down. Lamps, Maren thought, from the shape. Iron lanterns, taller than a man, wrapped in sacking against the jolting.

And beside the last mule, on foot, a thin young man with a wooden box on a strap over his shoulder.

She noticed him because he was the only one who wasn't looking at the lion.

Every one of the Wardens, as they came past the rise, turned in the saddle to look at Orrun, lying in the grass by the Stones. Some of them stopped. One of them took out a little book and wrote in it. But the thin young man walked beside the last mule with his head down, looking at the ground, and every half-league or so, Hesper said, he stopped and put the box down on the grass and knelt by it and opened it and looked into it, and wrote something, and closed it and caught up.

He did it as they passed below the Stones. Knelt. Opened the box. Looked into it for a long time.

Then he looked up. Not at the lion. At the Stones.

Then he closed the box and went after the mules.

The Wardens went into the Thornwood on the fifty-fifth day, by a different path from the knights, to the north. Nobody asked for passage. Nobody gave it.

---

Hesper took her to the sleepwood on the fifty-sixth day.

Two days south along the forest's edge and then in, by a path so narrow they went single file. Orrun came as far as the edge of the trees. Then he stopped.

*No,* he said.

She stopped too. She could smell it already. Faint. Sweet. Like the inside of a honey-store in late summer, when the combs have been cut and the whole shed is heavy with it.

*That smell,* said Orrun.

She knew. She'd known before he said it. The knowe. The smoke on the knowe, thick and sweet and choking, and the binder's hand on his head, and him going away inside himself, further and further, like a man going down into a well, and her putting her hand where the binder's hand had been and following him down.

The island, before that. The hunters' smoke at the north end, to keep him asleep. Months of it.

"Stay here," she said.

*I will stay a league from here,* said Orrun. *More.* He was already turning away. *Come back to me after. Come back and wash.*

---

The grove was forty trees.

They stood in a hollow in the Thornwood, close together, tall and straight, with smooth bark the colour of bone and branches that went up and out like hands held open. And down the trunks, from every crack and knot, long streaks of amber. Resin. Weeping out of the trees in slow gold tears, hardening into drops and beads and long glassy ropes, thick on the bark, thick on the ground under them. The air in the hollow was so sweet Maren's head swam.

"My grandmother planted this," said Hesper.

She stood at the edge of the hollow with her spear in her hand and looked at the trees.

"Not all of them. The ones in the middle. She brought the seedlings up from the south in a basket on her back when she was a girl, and planted them here, and came every spring to see them, and when she was too old to walk I carried her." She was quiet. "We don't cut them. Ever. The resin's for wounds. For birthing. A little, in a fire, at a burying, so the dead sleep easy. That's all. It's forbidden to sell it. It's forbidden to take it out of the forest." She looked at the long gold streaks down the bone-white bark. "And they take it anyway. The smugglers. To Saltreach. And it comes back as honey-smoke, and it goes in a pot under a beast's nose, and a binder puts his hand on its head."

"The knights have a chain," said Hesper. "The Wardens have their lamps. And somewhere behind them, there'll be the others. The ones who came for the grey-maned on the knowe. You know there will." She turned and looked at Maren. "They'll want smoke. For the one on the Ring. A great deal of smoke, for a beast that size. And here it is. Forty trees of it. Within reach of the grey."

"So you're going to burn it."

"The Keepers decided. At the Stones. Not the elders: the elders would never. We decided, the Keepers, round our own fire, the night the knights went in." Hesper's voice was quite steady. "Every grove from here to the Ysse. Tonight."

---

They burned it at moonrise.

Twelve Keepers, in a ring round the hollow, with torches. Maren stood far back, upwind, on the path, with a cloth soaked in the stream over her mouth and nose, as Hesper had told her.

They didn't put the torches in at once. They stood in the ring with the torches lit and held up, and they spoke to the trees. In the old tongue. All of them together, low, a long running murmur that went on and on, round and round, like the song Gunna had said the ledges sang over the old one's wing. Asking, Hesper said afterwards. Asking the trees' leave.

Maren didn't know if the trees gave it.

Then they put the torches in.

The resin went up like fat in a pan. All at once, with a sound like a great breath drawn in, a roar, and the whole hollow was fire: the long gold ropes on the bark flaring white, the beads on the ground spitting and popping, the bone-white trunks going black from the bottom up, and the flames going up them faster than she'd ever seen fire climb, to the branches, to the tops, until the whole grove stood there burning like forty torches.

And the smoke.

It came up out of the hollow thick and white and gold, and it was sweet, sweet, so sweet it was like being hit. Even through the wet cloth. Even upwind. It went up in a pillar over the Thornwood, straight up in the still night air, white in the moonlight, and leaned east, and went on going up.

She looked round. Away to the north, over the black line of the trees, another pillar. And another, further. To the south, two. She counted. A dozen, white in the moonlight, going up all over the Thornwood. Two dozen. Every grove from here to the Ysse.

Through the bond, a league off, she felt Orrun go.

Not far. A little. Slow and heavy and far away, as if he'd lain down in deep water and was letting himself go under. Like the knowe. Only the thin edge of the smoke had reached him; she could feel he was upwind, a long way. It was enough that he could smell it. That he knew what it was.

She didn't think. She reached for him. Hard, through the bond, as she'd reached on the knowe.

*Orrun.*

A long moment.

*Here,* he said. Thick. Slow. *I'm here.*

*Stay here.*

*I'm here.*

She stood on the path with the wet cloth over her face and held on to him, a league away, while the grove burned.

Beside the hollow, in the ring of Keepers, Hesper stood with her torch still held into the fire and the light on her face and tears going down it, steadily, without a sound. She didn't take the torch out until the last of her grandmother's trees came down.

---

The smoke drifted over the Plains for three days.

It went west on the wind, sweet and thin, out of the Thornwood over the grass, so that the elk at the Speaking Stones stood with their heads low and their eyes half shut and the horse clans led them away north out of it. The beasts went out of the forest faster now: not in ones and twos, but in tens. Deer. Boar. Once, at dawn, a pack of wolves, running west across the Plains past the Stones with their tongues out, not stopping.

At the forest's edge, on the second day, Corran saw the Wardens' snow lynx. Two of them, come back out of the trees for something, standing at the edge of the Thornwood with the smoke going past them. Staggering. Their eyes empty, Corran said, blank as a bound beast's, for a long breath. Then the smoke thinned, and they shook their heads, and they were themselves again, and they went back in.

The knights were in the Thornwood. The Wardens were in the Thornwood. Both of them going east, toward the grey, by their two paths, with their chain and their lamps.

On the sixtieth day Hesper came to Maren at the Stones with her spear over her shoulder and her cords at her belt and Hush at her heel, and her face washed clean.

"Now," she said, "we go too."

Orrun got up out of the grass before Maren could answer. He'd been lying with his head east for twenty days, because he'd chosen to.

*Yes,* he said.
