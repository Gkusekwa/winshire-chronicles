The smoke came down the northern path on the fifty-eighth day, and Warden Marr's lynx stopped dead in the middle of it.

Idony was walking behind the last mule, as he had for thirty-eight days, with the crate on its strap over his shoulder and the ledger in the crook of his arm. He smelled it first: sweet, faint, heavy, like a honey-store in late summer. He looked up. Between the black pines ahead the air was hazed, a thin white drift going through the trees from the south, slow, at about the height of a mule's ears.

The Wardens in front of him had stopped.

Ysolde Marr's snow lynx was standing on the path with one great soft forefoot lifted. Frost, she called it. A pale beast, white and grey, with tufted ears and eyes like chips of ice, the best-trained lynx in the Blue College, Dane said, who'd once tracked a runaway Fourth-ring Scribe across forty leagues of snow by the smell of his ink.

It stood on the path in the drifting smoke and its eyes went empty.

He saw it happen. They didn't close. They didn't change colour. Only, from one moment to the next, there was nobody behind them. The lynx stood with its foot lifted and its pale eyes open, looking at nothing, and it was like looking into a window of a house that had been left.

Ysolde said its name. Then louder.

Idony was counting. He found afterwards that he'd been counting from the moment it stopped, under his breath, as he counted the beats of a glass's fall. *Six. Seven. Eight.*

On eleven, the lynx put its foot down and shook its head, hard, so its ears flapped, and looked round at Ysolde as if she'd spoken to it from very far off. Then it was itself again. It sneezed. It walked on up the path.

He wrote it down. *Day 58. Northern path, two days short of the grey. Sweet smoke from the south (sleepwood, burned?). Marr's lynx: eyes empty for a count of eleven. Returned without harm.* He wrote it down before he'd thought to be frightened. When he'd finished writing it, he was frightened, a little, and then he wrote that down too, in the silk copy, where nobody would see.

---

"Barbaric," said Dane, that night.

He was standing at the edge of the camp looking south, where the smoke had come from. There was a glow on the low clouds there still, faint, and further south, another.

"They've burned their own groves," he said. "The Keepers. The sleepwood. Every grove from here to the lake, the mule-men say." He shook his head. "Do you know what that resin's worth in Corsa? Do you know what the College pays for a single jar of it, for study?"

"No, Magister."

"More than you earn in a year." Dane turned. He was a tall man, broad, handsome in a heavy way, with a short grey beard and the blue robes of his college and the five notches on his ring catching the firelight. "Two hundred groves, perhaps. Burned in a night. So that no one could use them." He said it as if he couldn't make the shape of it fit in his mouth. "Out of spite."

Idony thought about the lynx standing on the path with nobody behind its eyes.

He thought the Keepers had burned their groves for exactly the reason Dane had just said, and that it wasn't spite. He thought it was the most practical thing anyone had done since the morning of the breaking.

He didn't say so.

---

They came to the edge of the grey on the sixty-first day.

He'd known it was there. The two recut glasses had shown the fall for five weeks, and the fall had a shape, and the shape had an edge, and he'd been walking toward the edge with the arithmetic in his ledger. But knowing a number and walking up to it are different things. He'd forgotten that. He was glad to be reminded.

It was a line.

A line through the Thornwood, running away to the left and right as far as he could see between the trees, curving very slightly. On one side of it, the forest: black pine and oak and thorn, green, dark, ordinary. On the other side, the same forest, grey.

Not burned. Not dry. Grey. As if somebody had taken the colour out of everything with a brush and left only the shapes. The pines were grey. The oaks were grey. The moss on the oaks was grey, the ferns under them grey, the grass grey, the earth grey, all of it standing exactly as it had stood, with every needle and leaf on it, and not one of them moving in the wind.

The line between was as sharp as a rule drawn on a page. He could put one boot on green moss and one on grey.

He did. He stood there with one foot on each side of it and looked down.

"Reader Vael," said Dane, behind him, "would you mind not doing that."

---

There were birds.

He found them on the first afternoon, under the grey trees, a few hundred paces in. Small birds. Finches, a wren, a pair of tits, lying on the grey ground under the branches they'd fallen from. Grey themselves. He knelt and looked at the wren.

It hadn't rotted.

It had been dead for days. Weeks, perhaps. It lay on its side with its feet curled and its eye closed and every feather in place, perfect, like a carving of a wren. No flies. No smell. No ants. Nothing had touched it, because nothing alive had come here to touch it.

He thought about that. He thought that rotting was a kind of living. That it was the small things eating, the moulds and the worms, and when they couldn't live on a piece of ground, nothing on it could be eaten, and the dead stayed as they'd fallen, exactly, for as long as the ground stayed dead.

He wrote that down. Then he picked the wren up in his handkerchief, very carefully, and put it in his coat, and didn't know why.

---

The Wardens' wards went out as they crossed.

Every Warden of the Blue College wore a ward on the left sleeve: a band of grey silk with a line of runes worked into it in blue thread, against blows and against working. Cut by a Fourth-ring Scribe, fed from the ground they stood on, renewed every spring. Idony had always wanted one.

As each Warden stepped over the line, the blue thread on his sleeve went dull, like a lamp turned down. One after another, as they came over, he watched it go.

The six binding-lamps' frames had runes cut in the iron. They went dull too, as the mules carried them over.

One of the younger Wardens, a red-haired man called Pask, stopped on the grey side and held up his hand and said a light-word into his palm, the little one every First-ring student learns, that makes a glow the size of a coin.

Nothing.

He said it again. Nothing. He looked at his hand.

"Dead ground," said Dane. "As at Hearthmere. We were told." He didn't look at his own sleeve. "Go on."

Idony took out the small field-glass in its padded case, the one he'd cut on the road for the single word *here*, and set it on the grey earth.

The quicksilver lay in the bottom of the throat like a coin in a dry well.

---

He measured the edge for three days.

Dane wanted a date. Dane always wanted a date. Idony said he couldn't give a date until he knew the shape, and he couldn't know the shape until he'd measured it, and Dane looked at him for a while and said, "Then measure it, Reader Vael. Quickly."

So he did. He took two of the mule-men, who were bored and glad of it, and a knotted line a hundred paces long, and a hatchet, and they walked the edge. Every hundred paces he blazed a tree on the green side, a white cut in the bark, and wrote the number on it in charcoal. They walked south along the line, curving, and then east, curving. He took bearings on the glasses at night. He did the arithmetic in the ledger by candle.

It was a circle.

Very nearly a true circle, with the Ring at its middle. A league in radius. He measured it three ways and got a league three times, give or take a hundred paces.

And it was growing. On the third morning he went back to the first blazed tree, on the northern path, and the grey had come past it. The tree he'd cut on the green side was grey. The white of the blaze was grey. He measured from the tree to the new line with the knotted cord: forty paces. In three days.

A hundred and twenty paces a week, more or less. Outward, in every direction at once, like a stain spreading through cloth.

He sat on the grey ground by the grey tree with the grey blaze on it that he'd cut white three days ago, and did the arithmetic, and found he was smiling, and stopped.

---

He saw it on the sixty-fourth day.

They came up onto a rise in the grey forest in the late afternoon, the whole expedition, the Wardens and their lynx and the mules and the Scribes and Dane and him, a league in from the edge, and the trees thinned on the far side of the rise and there was a clearing below them, and in the clearing there was a hill.

It took him a moment to see that it was breathing.

It lay across the clearing as a hill lies across a valley, grey, enormous, the size of the College hill at home, or bigger, so big his eyes kept trying to make it into land. Then he saw the shapes. A head, laid on great forepaws, with a mane like a ridge of broken rock running back from it. Plates down the back, one above the other, like the courses of a wall. Wings, folded, lying along its sides like a fallen tent of stone and leather, wider than Glasswater's harbour. A tail curled round. And on the forelegs, above the paws, something dark that caught the light. Iron. The broken ends of chains, each link as thick as his body, hanging from great cuffs and lying in the grey grass.

Its eyes were half shut. Pale, as quicksilver is pale at rest.

It breathed. A long slow wind went through the grey trees, and the grey trees didn't move in it. He counted. Eleven counts in. A pause. Nineteen out.

Round it, and under it, the stones.

Twelve, the clans said. He could see ten. Tall grey stones, older-looking than anything he'd ever seen, standing in a ring round the clearing, and the beast lying across the middle of them as if it had been poured there. Two more hidden under its flank. On the tops of the stones he could see, there were owls. Great pale owls, a yard and a half standing, he guessed, sitting quite still with their faces turned toward the thing in the middle.

Nobody on the rise said anything for a long time.

Then one of the Scribes behind him was sick in the grey ferns, quietly.

And Idony stood on the rise with the crate on his shoulder and the ledger under his arm and looked at the thing that had eaten a kingdom's lamps, that he had charted as a hole for eleven years without knowing it had a shape, and felt something come up in him that he had no word for at all. It was a little like terror and a great deal like joy. He thought it was probably the feeling that you were supposed to have in a temple. He'd never had it in one.

He took out the ledger and wrote: *Day 64. The Titan. Breath: 11 in, 19 out.*

His hand wasn't steady. He wrote it again, underneath, more clearly.

---

Dane sent him in to the stones the next morning.

"Measure them," he said. "You're the only one who can read what the glasses say. Go as close as you think wise and no closer." He paused. "If it moves, Reader Vael, run."

So he went down the rise on his own, through the grey ferns, with the small field-glass in its case and nothing else, toward the outermost stone on the western side of the Ring. It stood a long way from the beast's flank. Fifty paces, perhaps. Sixty. Even so, as he came down toward it, he felt the beast's breath go past him. A long slow wind with no smell to it at all.

He stopped forty paces from the stone and set the glass down on the grey earth and looked.

The quicksilver lay in the bottom. Dead.

He went on. Thirty paces. Twenty. Ten. Each time the same. Dead ground right to the foot of the stone, grey grass growing round it in a grey ring.

He stood by the stone. Tall, grey, lichened with lichen that was grey too. He looked up. On the top, far above him, a hollow owl turned its great pale face down and looked at him with dark eyes.

He put his hand on the stone.

It was warm.

Not sun-warm. The sun was hardly up. Warm from inside, like a hearthstone an hour after the fire's out. And under his palm, faint, it hummed. A low steady note, not heard, felt: in the bones of his hand and up his arm.

He took the field-glass out of its case and set it on the stone, on a ledge at the height of his chest, and stood back.

The quicksilver ran up.

All at once. Up the throat of the glass, past every mark he'd cut, to the very top, and stayed there, trembling, pressed against the glass at the top of the throat as if it wanted to go further and couldn't.

Full.

He looked at it. He looked at the grey grass at the foot of the stone, where the glass had lain dead. He looked at the stone, and the glass on it, brim-full.

"Full," he said aloud, to the owl. "Full like a cup."

The owl looked at him.

---

He sat down with his back to the warm stone and worked it out in his head, slowly, a step at a time, as he'd work a long sum.

The beast drew. He'd known that for eleven years; he'd watched the hole under Hearthmere, and the hole was the chains, feeding on it. Now it was free and it drew for itself. It lay on the Ring and drew the Deep in from a league round, from the trees and the ground and the birds, like a drain drawing water from a basin. That was the fall on the recut glasses. That was the grey.

But it didn't drink from the ground. It couldn't. There was nothing left in the ground near it to drink. It drank from the stones.

The stones were old. Old-realm stone, the kind Kessmoor couldn't cut and the College couldn't date. Stone that held the Deep as a cistern holds water. And the Deep came in from a league round, from everywhere, running toward the beast, and before it got to the beast it went into the stones, and filled them. And the beast drank from the stones.

A drain, with a full cup at its mouth.

He saw it whole. He saw it as a drawing on a page: the circle of grey, the arrows going inward, the twelve stones at the middle full to the brim, and the great shape lying across them with its mouth to the cup. And he saw, with a cold turn in his stomach that was mostly delight, that if he knew that shape, he'd know it again. Anywhere. Whatever the beast lay down on next.

He took off his coat in the grey grass, and took out the knife he kept for cutting quills, and opened the stitching of the hem a finger's length, and took out the oiled silk, and wrote it there first, in the smallest hand he had.

*The stir. A drain with a full cup at its mouth. The ground dies outward; the old stone fills; the beast drinks the stone.*

Then he wrote it in the ledger he'd show to Dane, more plainly, without the word *stir*, because he'd only just thought of it and didn't want Dane to have it yet.

---

Dane saw what it meant before Idony had finished telling him.

He stood on the rise with the ledger open in his big hands and read the page twice and looked down at the stones round the beast, and Idony saw his face change. It went still and bright and intent, like a man at a card table who has seen the other man's hand.

"Live," Dane said. "The stones are live."

"The only live ground in the league, Magister. As far as I can measure."

"So a drawer standing on the stones," said Dane slowly, "could draw. From the stone. Without drawing on himself."

Idony hadn't thought of that. He thought of it now, all at once, like a step in the dark that your foot has already missed.

On dead ground, a drawer had nothing to draw from but his own body. Everybody knew what that did. It was how Wardens burned out, in the old stories: drawing on dead ground, on themselves, till they went grey. But a drawer standing on a stone that was full to the brim would have all of it to draw on.

"Yes," Idony said. "I think so."

Dane closed the ledger.

"Ten stones we can reach," he said, half to himself. "Twelve Wardens. Two to tend the lamps." He looked down at the ring. "Yes."

He went away down the rise, already calling for Ysolde Marr.

Idony stood on the rise with his hands empty and the wren in his coat, and wondered for the first time in thirty-one years whether he should have measured something.

Hearthmere's knights were on the western side of the Ring, at the edge of the grey.

He'd seen their smoke on the second day. On the sixty-sixth day he went over with Dane and two Wardens to look, by Dane's arrangement, under a white cloth on a stick, to a fallen grey oak halfway between the two camps.

The knights' camp was very ordered. Rows of tents. Greyharts picketed in a line, tall white deer with branched antlers, and hounds lying by the tents, and two falcons on a perch. All of them alive and well and themselves, on the green side of the line, and when a knight walked his greyhart over onto the grey to water it at a grey stream, it stayed itself there too.

Bonds, Idony thought. Bonds weren't fed by the ground. They were fed by the two lives in them. Dead ground didn't stop them. He wrote that down later and underlined it.

And along the grey, inside the line, there were men driving stakes.

Great iron stakes as long as a man, with eyes at the top, being driven into the grey ground with mallets by gangs of labourers. A line of them, going toward the beast, curving. The stakes went in easily. Too easily. He watched one man drive one in with three blows that should have taken thirty. The grey ground was soft as ash.

On the far side of the camp, on wains, in heaps, he could see the chain.

---

The knight-captain was a small neat woman of fifty with iron-grey hair cut short and an ink-stain on the second finger of her right hand. Rowan, she said. Knight-Captain. Of Greyhold. She didn't say what Greyhold was now, though everyone at the fallen oak knew: a broken school on a river bank, with a dead man hanging on its gate. She had a knight with her, a long-faced man with two hounds at his heel, a dark one and a pale one.

She and Dane talked for an hour.

Idony stood by the oak with his crate and listened, and understood that they were both saying the same thing very politely in two different ways. Hearthmere would chain it. Isvaren would hold it. Each of them would be glad for the other to watch. Neither of them would be glad for the other to do anything else.

They agreed on nothing, except to wait.

"Wait for what?" said Rowan, at the end. She turned her head and looked at Idony. Straight at him, for the first time. "Your reader. What does he say? How long does it lie there?"

Dane looked at him too.

Idony cleared his throat.

"The stones are filling, Knight-Captain," he said. "They're nearly full. When they're full, the draw will change. It must. A cup can't hold more than it holds." He hesitated. "I don't know what it will do then. Nobody's ever seen it before. But from the slope of the fall, from the rate the grey's growing, I'd say about a month." He did the arithmetic again in his head, to be sure. "Near midsummer."

Rowan looked at him a moment longer. She looked at him, he thought, the way he'd looked at the field-glass on the stone. As if he were a thing that told you something, and the question was only whether it told you true.

"Midsummer, then," she said.

She went back to her camp.

---

The walker came in from the south-west in the last days of the month.

He'd been watching it on the green glasses all the way across the Plains. Step by step, a mouthful at a time, going toward the Ring. For nearly three weeks it had hardly moved, somewhere south-west, out on the Plains. He'd marked the place. Then, on the sixtieth day, it had started again. East. Toward the grey.

On the sixty-ninth day it went off the glasses.

He thought for a moment he'd lost it. Then he understood: it had gone into the grey. Into the dead league. The glasses couldn't see into it any more than they could see into Hearthmere. Whatever the walker was, it was inside the circle now with him, somewhere, walking.

On the seventy-first day he found its trail.

He was walking the south side of the league with the field-glass and the knotted cord, measuring, and he set the glass down on the grey earth by a grey stream, and the quicksilver lifted.

A hair's breadth. Not more. But it lifted, off the bottom of the throat, and trembled there.

He moved the glass a pace to the left. It sank. A pace to the right. It sank. He moved it forward, toward the Ring, along the stream, and it lifted again.

A line. A faint live line through the grey, a pace wide, running north-east along the stream toward the beast. As if something had walked here and the ground under its feet had remembered being alive for a moment and was still remembering.

He followed it a quarter-league with the glass, setting it down every twenty paces, until the light went. It went straight toward the Ring.

He sat on the grey ground by the grey stream in the dusk with the glass in his hands and the hair's breadth of quicksilver lifted in it, and thought: something alive walked here. Something that gives back what it takes.

---

He saw them on the seventy-fourth day, at dusk.

He was on the rise. He'd taken to sitting there every evening with the ledger, counting the beast's breaths, eleven in and nineteen out, always the same, and watching the owls on the stones. The light was going. The grey forest was going grey-blue.

On the far side of the clearing, the south side, something came out of the trees.

A lion.

He knew it was a lion because there was nothing else it could be. Grey, like the forest. Like the thing on the Ring. A great grey cat with a mane like an iron-grey cloud, walking out of the grey trees into the clearing slowly, with its head low, toward the beast.

Beside it, walking at its shoulder, a woman.

He couldn't see her face at that distance. He saw a dark coat. A sword at her hip. Tall, and dark-haired. She walked beside the lion with one hand on its shoulder, slowly, out across the open grey grass toward the beast, toward the stones, and didn't stop where anybody sensible would have stopped.

On the rise behind him, he heard one of the Wardens say something sharp, and Dane's voice answer, and then nothing.

The lion walked up to the stones. Between two of them. Into the ring.

Toward the head.

It stopped. It stood there a long moment in front of the great half-shut eye. Then it lay down. Slowly, as a big dog lies down, front legs first, with its chin on its paws, facing the beast.

The woman sat down beside it in the grey grass with her hand on its neck.

The beast on the Ring didn't turn its head.

Idony counted the distance. He did it without thinking, as he'd counted the lynx and the breaths and the blazed trees. Against the spacing of the stones, which he'd measured: ten paces from the lion's nose to the beast's. Eleven of his.

He opened the ledger on his knee and wrote, in the last of the light:

*Day 74, dusk. The walker: a lion, about ten feet at the shoulder, grey, mane iron-grey. Comes in from the south and lies down at ten paces from the Titan's head, within the ring. The Titan does not turn its head. Breath unchanged: 11 in, 19 out. With it, a woman.*

He looked at what he'd written.

Then he looked up again, at the clearing, at the small dark shape sitting in the grey grass beside the grey lion, ten paces from the head of the thing that had eaten a kingdom's lamps, and not moving.

He wrote under it, smaller:

*She has her hand on its neck.*
