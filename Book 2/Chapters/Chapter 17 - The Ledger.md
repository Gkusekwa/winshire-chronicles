He told Arn about the road the night he came back, on the wall, in the dark.

He didn't wait. He'd thought about it all the way up the Way on the red dragon's neck with the book against his chest, and he knew if he waited a day it would come up the Way ahead of him, as news did, and Arn would hear it from a wall-guard. So he went straight up onto the turf wall above the gate where Arn stood every evening looking south, and stood beside him, and said it.

Two hundred of Delling's company. Across the moor, from the Ashlings, by the old peat road east of the hold, down Skarrow's side to the coast. In three parties. In the Clan's name. No burning, no taking. The ledges to see them over.

He said it all, plainly. Then he stopped.

Arn didn't say anything for a long time.

The wind came up the glen. Below them in the yard somebody was singing, badly, by a fire.

"Delling's," said Arn at last.

"Yes."

"The yellow tower."

"Yes."

"The gorse at the ford." Arn's voice was quite flat. "The second time. Sixty crossbows on the slope. Hallam coming down the hill with his stick, under the truce, and the bolt." He didn't turn his head. "You gave my moor to the men who shot Hallam."

"For the book."

"For the book." Arn looked south down the dark Way. "I could turn them back. You know that. I could put a hundred riders on the peat road tomorrow and send every one of them back down to the Cross to be hanged by the man upstairs, or kill them myself on the moor and save him the rope."

"You could."

"But you're bonded. And you gave it in the Clan's name. And the ledges have said they'll keep it." Arn's hands were flat on the turf of the wall-top. "Your green one was up at the caves before you'd got off the red one's neck. Gunna sent word down an hour since. *The farmer's given the Clan's road. The bonded keep a bonded man's word. That's the old law.*" He almost laughed. "The old law. Every time I turn round, there's the old law, and you standing in it."

"So they'll walk over." Arn turned at last and looked at him in the dark. "Your word. Not mine. Remember that. I'll not lift a hand to stop them, and I'll not lift a hand to help." He was quiet a moment. "And when they're over, farmer, you'll have spent everything you had with me. Everything. There'll be nothing left."

He went down off the wall.

He hadn't asked to see the book.

---

Delling's company crossed the moor in three days.

Calder watched the second party from the edge on the thirty-sixth day, sitting on the red dragon's neck on a crag above the peat road with the wind in his face.

They came up off the south slope and over the lip of the moor, two days out of the Ashlings, in a long straggling line, seventy men or so, in their company coats with the yellow tower on the breast, with their crossbows slung and their packs on their backs, walking, like men going home from a fair. Above them, on the heights either side of the peat road, the ledges sat: Hulda Sarr and her black-and-grey Harl on the left, an old man called Brede and his blue on the right, and further on, two more pairs, small against the sky. Sitting. Watching. Not moving.

The men on the road looked up at the dragons on the heights. Then they looked at the crag, and saw the red one.

Calder felt Kharrak's tide come up.

*Seventy,* said the red dragon. *In a line. On open ground. I could do it in one pass.*

"I know."

*I know you know.* The tide stayed up, hot, under Calder's legs. *I'm only saying.*

On the road below, the men had stopped. All of them, in their line, looking up at the red dragon on the crag with its wings half open and its head down. From here Calder could see the white of their faces, turned up.

He put his hand flat on Kharrak's neck.

*Not that,* he said, without speaking. *Not now. Not these.*

The tide went down. Slowly. Kharrak folded his wings.

On the road the men stood a moment longer. Then one of them at the front, the leader, a heavy man, put his hand up, slowly, palm out. Not a wave. The other thing. *I see you.* And they walked on, along the peat road, over the moor, east, with the ledges' dragons sitting on the heights above them, and didn't look up at the crag again.

Brask had gone with the first party the day before. He'd lifted a hand too, at the edge, Hulda told Calder after. Just a hand. As if to say: well. There it is.

---

He took the book up to the caves on the thirty-sixth day, in the evening, and gave it to his mother.

She was sitting at the cave mouth by her fire, with the warm wind coming out of the hill behind her and the tarn below going dark, mending one of Pip's shirts. She put the shirt down when she saw what he'd brought. She wiped her hands on her apron, very carefully, front and back, as if she were going to touch bread dough.

Then she took it from him.

She unbuckled the strap. She opened the cover. She sat there in the firelight with the swollen damp black book open on her knees and looked at the first page.

"It's stuck," she said. "Half of it. The pages. It's been wet and dried and wet again." She turned one, very carefully, with her fingernail. It came away from the next with a small tearing sound. She stopped. "I'll have to dry them. One at a time. On the warm floor. If I pull them they'll tear."

"How long?"

"As long as it takes, son." She looked at the page in front of her. "And it's a clerk's hand. Small. Leaning over. Full of short forms, as the factors write when they don't want you reading over their shoulder." She tilted it to the fire. "I can read it. Slowly. I'll have to go slowly."

"Then go slowly."

She looked up at him.

"You want it read aloud," she said.

"Yes."

"All of it."

"All of it."

Ada looked at the book again. Then she turned it toward the fire, and put her finger under the first line, as she'd put it under the lines of the old song-book when she taught Wren her letters at the kitchen table at home, and began.

---

She read a few pages a night.

That was all she could do. The rest of the day she spent drying the next pages: lying them open one at a time on the warm floor of the cave in a row, with pebbles on the corners, and turning them over at noon, and peeling them apart with her fingernail when they were dry enough to come. The cave floor was so warm that a page dried in half a day. Wren helped her. Wren was very good at pebbles.

At night Ada sat in the cave mouth by her fire with the book on her knees and read.

Calder sat across the fire from her and listened. Every night. Bel came sometimes, with Nan asleep in her lap. Wren always, sitting beside her grandmother with her chin on her knees, watching the words go under Ada's finger.

It was a year and a half.

A year and a half of one man's silver, in order, day by day. Every bag that had come to Rook and every coin that had gone out of his hand. Each line a place, a thing, a number, and often a name. The small slanting clerk's hand going on and on down the damp pages without a blot, without a crossing-out, as neat as a dues-roll.

*Up from the coast, three bags, the ring on every bag. Received.*

*The miller at Harrow's End, through the coin man: the farmer's road and hour, two silver. Paid.*

*The bucket in Ulric's yard: myself, a week, for the listening. Unpaid. (My own time.)*

*Upper Rush, the barns: the red hand, twenty, for the burning. Paid.*

Calder sat across the fire and listened to his own winter go by. Every raid he'd heard of. Every fire he'd seen from Crow Scar. Every man who'd sold somebody's road for two silver. He'd known it was paid for. He hadn't known it was written down, in a neat hand, with the sums.

On the third night Ada read: *The drover, Varrhold, paid, for the winter: the uncle's steward, four silver and keep.*

She stopped.

"That's him," said Calder. "That's Rook. He was Ulric's drover. That's his own wage. Ulric's steward paid him, and he wrote it in his own book with the rest."

"He wrote down being paid by the man he was making a murderer of," said Ada.

"He wrote down everything."

She looked at the page for a while. Then she read on.

---

On the fifth night she came to the autumn.

He knew it was coming. He'd been waiting for it every night. The pages went from summer to harvest, the fair, the old chief's death, the burning, and then: *A horse, from the uncle's lines; to the coast for my mother. Returned.*

Ada read it and stopped.

"The night before the first ford," said Calder.

"There's nothing else," Ada said. She turned the page over, carefully. Then back. "That's all. A horse. To the coast for his mother. Returned." She looked up. "He didn't write down what he did with it."

"No."

"He wrote down two silver for the miller's word," said his mother, "and the price of the burning at Upper Rush, and his own wage. And for that night he wrote a horse."

Calder sat with his hands between his knees.

"He didn't get paid for that one," he said. "That's why. He did it himself. For the one upstairs. There was no silver to write down."

Wren, beside her grandmother, had gone very still.

"Is that Teo?" she said. "Papa. Is that about Teo?"

"Yes."

Wren looked at the page under Ada's finger. At the short line in the clerk's hand. *A horse.*

"That's not enough," she said.

"No," said Calder. "It's not."

---

On the seventh night Ada read the second ford.

*The gorse at the ford, the second meeting: sixty, Delling's. Thirty silver the captain, two each the men. One bolt over the count, for the old one: five.*

She read it twice, because he asked her to. Then she put the book down on her knees and looked across the fire at him.

"Five silver," she said. "For Hallam Brecc."

He didn't say anything.

"I knew him," said Ada. "Did you know that? When I was a girl, at the dues-school. He was a young man then, on the ledges, with Morrow, who was only a little thing. He used to come down to the school once a year and let us touch her. Morrow." She looked at the page. "Five silver."

"Over the count," said Calder. "That's what it says? Over the count?"

"One bolt over the count. For the old one." She put her finger under it. "They paid sixty men to sit in the gorse, and one bolt more than that, for him. Somebody knew he'd come down. Somebody paid for him in particular."

He thought of Delling at his table in the Plough, with his spectacles on their cord. *One of mine put the bolt in the old man.*

Two hundred of Delling's men had walked over the moor under the Clan's road a week ago. One of them had been paid five silver over the count.

He got up and went out of the cave mouth into the dark and stood by the tarn till he was cold.

---

On the forty-fourth day he sent Lisska east.

She'd been asking for a week. She sat on the setters' ledge every evening with her head turned east, toward the Plains, and sighed, loudly, as she did when she wanted him to ask her what was wrong.

He asked her.

*The lion-woman doesn't know,* she said. *About the book. About the road. About Wren on the grey one.* She shifted her feet. *She brought them all the way up. And then she went off east and nobody's told her anything.*

"You want to go and tell her."

*Somebody has to. You can't. You can't even write it.* A pause. *And the lion likes me.*

"The lion puts up with you."

*That's liking, for him.*

He thought about it. Thirteen days to the Plains and back, she said, if she flew hard and didn't stop to look at things, which she would. He thought about the old one, who still needed him close. About Kharrak, who'd sulk. About the bond pulling at her the whole way, getting thinner and thinner, like a rope paid out.

"Don't be gone long," he said.

*I won't.*

"Tell her the family's in the caves. All of them. Tell her about the book, and what it cost." He paused. "Tell her I gave a road to the wrong men to get it. She should know that."

*I'll tell her you were brave.*

"Tell her what I said."

*I'll tell her both,* said Lisska, and went up off the ledge in a green crack of wings, and turned east into the evening, and was gone.

He felt her go. Thinner, and thinner, over the fells. Then only a kind of warmth at the back of his head where she'd been, like a hand on a stove after the fire's out.

---

He went between the caves and the setters' ledge for ten days.

Mornings with the old one. Ivar Leck came every noon and put his hands on the splints and listened, with his head on one side, and nodded. The bone was knitting. The song was still going in it, faint, slow. But the horn-wound under her foreleg wasn't. It wasn't getting worse; it wasn't getting better. It sat there in her chest, hot, sullen, under its packing, and the smell of it came and went.

*It is nothing,* Aurveth said.

"You said that about the wing."

*Yes,* she said. *I am an old fool about my own body. Everyone is.*

Evenings at the caves, with the book.

Wren had started reading with Ada, silently, over her shoulder while Ada read, with her lips moving. By the tenth night she was reading ahead, and once she said the next word before Ada got to it, and Ada stopped and looked at her and said, "Well," and went on.

Calder couldn't follow it. He tried. He sat beside them and looked at the page under Ada's finger and could see that the marks were words, and could find the shape of the ring of six where Rook had drawn it in the margin by the bags from the coast, and could pick out a letter here and there that he knew from the signs at the Cross. That was all. The rest was marks.

His daughter could read it. His mother could read it. A dead man had written it. And he sat beside them in the firelight with his hands between his knees and had it read to him, like a child.

He sent word to Skarrow by one of the ledges. *There's a book.* Nothing came back.

---

Lisska came home on the fifty-fourth day, at dusk.

He felt her coming two leagues off: a flicker at the back of his head, very faint, and then stronger, and then all at once there, bright and tired and frightened and pleased all at once, as she always was, only more so. He was on the setters' ledge. He stood up.

She came round the shoulder of the cliff so low and so slow that he thought she'd fall. She didn't. She came down on the ledge in a clumsy heap of green wings and lay there with her sides going in and out like bellows.

Under her jaw, on a green cord, there was a hide bag.

*Calder,* she said. *Calder, Calder. I flew so far.*

"I know."

*I flew so far and the Plains are so big and there are birds there bigger than me, there's a bird called a roc and it looked at me, and there are elk with trees on their heads, and the lion-woman's sitting with the clans, and there's an old blind woman who stopped them shooting me, and the Keeper tied this on me with a knot and I didn't take it off once, not once, Calder, I wanted to, it itched, but I didn't...*

"Lisska."

*What?*

"Breathe."

She breathed. She lay on the warm ledge and breathed, with her eyes half shut, and he knelt beside her and untied the cord.

The knot was still tied. Green cord, three turns and a twist. It had held, two thousand leagues of sky, or whatever it was.

---

*There were knights,* said Lisska, later, when she'd eaten.

He was sitting with her on the ledge in the last of the light. She'd told him everything twice, the Stones and the elders and the roc and the Keeper and the lion-woman's face when she heard about Wren. Now she was quiet.

*On the grass. Coming from the west. When I flew home.* She shifted. *A long way below. A long line of them, on the grey deer the knights ride, with their dogs. And carts behind them. Big carts with oxen. And on the carts, a chain.*

He went still.

*A big chain. Bigger than the one on the island. Shining.* A pause. *They were going east. Toward the forest. Toward where she is.* Another pause. *Toward where it is.*

"How far off?"

*Three days. Two. I don't know. They were slow, with the oxen.* She was quiet again. *And the clans are at the stones. All of them, the elk and the bears and the boat-people. And she's there. By the lion. With the clans behind her. And the knights coming across the grass with a chain.*

She looked at him with her pale green eyes.

*She's standing in between, Calder. She's standing in between them.*

---

He put the moss in that night.

He wet it in the setters' spring, as Lisska said the Keeper had said: clean water, a double handful of the grey-green stuff soaked till it was heavy and cold and smelled of stone and rain. Then he took the old packing out of the wound under Aurveth's foreleg, and the smell came up, and the old one didn't make a sound.

He packed the moss in. Gently. As much as would go.

Then he did the other thing the Keeper had said. He left it. He tied the linen back over it and put his hand on the old one's jaw and didn't look again.

*It's cold,* said Aurveth, after a while.

"Is it bad?"

*No.* She was quiet. *No. It's like water in summer. It's like putting your face in Coldtarn.*

He sat with her. He didn't look. He sat with his back to the warm rock and his hand on her jaw and felt it, through the bond, slowly, as the night went: the heat in the wound, that had sat in her chest for fifty days like a coal in a grate, going down. Not out. Down. A little. And a little more. Like a fire somebody has put a damp turf on.

Near midnight, for the first time since the fort, she slept without moving her head.

---

Ada found the mill on the fifty-fifth day.

He'd known it would come. He'd known since the first night. The pages went on through the winter, Upper Rush and the Gathering and the Ram's Head and the ford, and every night he waited for the autumn page that wasn't there, the one from the week after Teo. The harvest. The night of the mill.

It was at the back.

Ada turned the last dry page of the night and there was a second page behind it, stuck to the inside of the back cover, that she'd taken half a day to peel off on the warm floor. A separate list. Older ink. Written, Calder thought later, before the rest. Before Rook ever came to Varrhold. Five lines.

Ada read the first four. Payments to the coast. A ship. A name he didn't know.

Then she read the fifth, and stopped, and read it again without being asked.

*The hedge at the bottom of the Fenn field, one bolt, the Sallow rider. Sedge, Delling's, out of Wendmouth. Forty, before the company's hire.*

---

Nobody said anything.

Lisska was lying in the cave mouth by the fire. She'd come up from the setters' ledge with him at dusk, because she said she wanted to see Wren, and she'd been chattering all evening, to Wren, to Pip, to nobody, about the Plains and the roc and the old blind woman and the elk with trees on their heads, until Bel told her to hush and she didn't. She'd been happier than he'd felt her since the autumn.

She stopped.

He felt her stop. Through the bond. All of her, at once, the chatter and the brightness and the warmth, gone still, like a bird going quiet in a hedge when a hawk's shadow crosses it.

*Imra,* she said.

"Yes," said Calder.

*That's Imra. The Sallow rider. That's my Imra.*

"Yes."

*Sedge.* She said it slowly, the name, as if she were learning it. *Sedge. Delling's. Out of Wendmouth.* A pause. *Forty silver.*

Nobody else at the fire could hear her. Ada was looking at the page. Wren was looking at her father's face. Bel had her arms round Nan. Only Calder heard the green dragon say the name of the man who'd put the bolt into the woman she'd shared one life with for years, in the dark, in a hedge, in his own barley field.

And then he understood the rest.

He understood it all at once, coldly, sitting on the warm floor of the cave with the fire in front of him. Sedge, Delling's. Out of Wendmouth. Before the company's hire: one of Delling's own, an old hand. Two hundred of Delling's company had walked over the moor on the thirty-fifth, the thirty-sixth, the thirty-seventh day, on the old peat road, home to Wendmouth. In the Clan's name. Under the road he gave.

Sedge had walked home under his road.

He might have been in the second party. He might have been one of the seventy men on the peat road who'd stopped and looked up at the red dragon on the crag, with their faces white. He might have been the heavy man at the front who'd put up his hand, palm out. *I see you.*

Calder had held the red tide down and let him walk.

---

Lisska didn't say anything else that night.

Not to him. Not to Wren, who crept round the fire to her and put her arms round as much of the green dragon's neck as they'd go round, and held on. Not to anybody. She lay in the cave mouth with her head on her forelegs and her pale green eyes open on the fire and was silent, all night, the whole long night, the first night he'd ever known her silent.

He didn't know what to say to her. He didn't try. He'd given the road. He'd say so, if she asked. She didn't ask.

She didn't joke again for weeks. When she did, it was smaller, and he heard it.

---

Late, when the others were asleep, he sat across the fire from his mother.

The book was shut on her knees. She had her hand flat on the cover, as Delling had, and Arn on the table beside the knife.

"Teach me," he said.

She looked up.

"My letters. All of them." He heard his own voice, low, so as not to wake Wren. "I'll not have it read to me again. Not that page. Not any of it. I'll read it myself. Every word." He looked at the book under her hand. "I'm thirty-two years old and my girl can read a dead man's hand faster than I can see it. I'll not sit and have it read to me like a child."

For a while Ada said nothing.

Then she put the book down on the warm floor, carefully. She reached into the fire and took out a stick with the end burnt black, and blew on it till it stopped smoking, and leaned over and drew on the cave floor between them, in the firelight, slowly, a shape.

A curve. Open on the right, like a new moon lying on its back, or a sickle.

"That's the first of yours," she said. "Of your name. Everybody starts with their own name."

He looked at it.

She held out the burnt stick. "Start there."

He took it.

He leaned over the cave floor and drew the curve, crookedly, beside his mother's.
