He lay down at ten paces, and she sat beside him, and the thing on the stones didn't turn its head.

She'd known it wouldn't. She'd known the whole ten days from Hesper's oak, through the green and then the grey, the last five of them slow, along the grey stream where nothing moved and nothing rotted, with Hush slinking behind them low to the ground and unhappy, lying down every few hundred paces and having to be talked up again. She'd known when they came out of the grey trees into the clearing at dusk and she saw it for the first time since the river.

It was bigger than she remembered. Or she remembered it wrong. On the morning of the flight she'd seen it from below, from the reeds, going up into the sunrise, a shape against fire. Here it lay on the ground in the grey light, and the ground under it was the size of a village green, and it filled it.

Orrun walked out into the ring between two of the stones.

She walked beside him with her hand on his shoulder. She didn't decide to. Her hand was there, and her feet went.

He went toward the head. Slowly. As she'd gone toward him, on the island, in the autumn, every evening for a month, a step at a time, stopping, waiting, going on. And at ten paces from the great half-shut eye, he stopped, and stood, and then lay down. Front legs first. Chin on his paws. Facing it.

Ten paces. She'd lain at ten paces from him on the island, night after night, in the cold, before he let her come closer.

She sat down beside him in the grey grass with her hand on his neck.

The thing on the stones breathed. A long slow wind came out of it and went past them and on through the grey trees, and the grey trees didn't move. She counted without meaning to. Eleven. A pause. Nineteen.

It didn't turn its head.

---

He lay there for five days and didn't eat.

She brought him water from the grey stream in her hat. He drank it. She brought him nothing else, because there was nothing in the grey to bring, and he wouldn't have eaten it. He lay at ten paces from his kin with his chin on his paws and his pale eyes open on the great pale half-shut eye in front of him, day and night.

And he spoke to it.

She felt him do it. Through the bond, it was like feeling a man lean out over a well and call down into it. A great long reaching, out of him, toward the thing on the stones. Not words, not the slow stone-turning words he used with her. Older than that. Kin. The way he'd spoken to Tamsin on the Scarfell's edge and to Lisska on the Plains. Within sight, kin to kin.

Nothing came back.

Not a refusal. Not a turning-away. Nothing. The reaching went out of him and into the thing and stopped, as if it had gone into deep water, and nothing came up.

*It doesn't hear me,* he said, on the second night.

*Or it hears,* said Orrun, *and there's nobody there.*

On the island, on the night of the locks, he'd learned it didn't know him. She'd felt him learn it, under the hill, with the chains coming off: the cold of it, going through him like a blade. *It doesn't know me.* She'd thought then that it might come, given time. That three hundred years of chains had worn it down to nothing, and that something might grow back.

She sat in the grey grass with her hand on his neck and felt him call into the well again, and again, and nothing come up, and thought that she'd been wrong.

On the tops of the stones, the hollow owls watched them. Great pale owls, as tall as children, with dark eyes, sitting quite still with their faces turned toward the woman and the lion. They didn't fly. Hesper said they hadn't flown at noon since the knights came. They only watched.

---

On the third morning she noticed the grass.

She'd got up stiff from the grey ground at first light, and walked a few paces off to stretch, and turned round and looked back at him lying there, and saw it.

The grass where he lay was green.

Not much. Not the grass itself: the blades were grey, like all the grass in the ring, like all the grass in a league. But at the roots, where they went into the ground, close to his body, there was green. A faint green, like the first green on a field after snow. In a ring round him, a few paces across. As if the ground under him had remembered something.

She knelt and looked at it.

That night it had faded, as if the thing on the stones had drawn it out again in the dark. In the morning it was back. A little greener.

She didn't tell him. She thought he knew.

The tide-reader came on the eightieth day.

She'd seen him. She'd seen him on the Plains, walking beside the last mule with his box, looking at the ground. She'd seen him every evening since she came into the ring, sitting on the rise to the north where the Wardens camped, with a book on his knee, looking down at the clearing. Watching. Writing.

He came down from the rise in the middle of the morning, alone, with a small leather case in his hand and nothing else. He came across the grey grass between the stones, slowly, as you'd walk up to a horse you didn't know, and stopped twenty paces off. Well short of Orrun. Well short of her.

He was younger than she'd thought. Thirty, perhaps. Thin, with untidy brown hair and ink on his fingers and a coat that had been good once. He had a plain iron ring on his left hand with two small notches cut in it.

"Good morning," he said.

His voice was light and careful, with an Isvaren way of saying things very exactly.

She looked at him.

"I'm Idony Vael," he said. "Of the College of Tides. In Vael. I'm a reader. Second ring." He held up the hand with the ring on it, a little apologetically, as if the ring needed explaining. "I read the tide-glasses. The Deep. Its rise and fall." He stopped. "I've been watching you for a week. I thought I ought to come and say so."

Orrun didn't lift his head. But she felt him look, through the bond. A slow look. Weighing.

"Maren Ashdown," she said.

"Yes," said Idony Vael. "I know."

---

He asked permission to come closer, and she gave it, and he came to ten paces, the same distance as Orrun from his kin, and knelt down in the grey grass and opened the leather case.

Inside, on a bed of padded cloth, there was a glass. Not like any glass she'd seen. A sealed tube of thick clear glass the length of her hand, with a bulb at the bottom and a narrow throat going up, and lines cut in the throat, and something cut in the bulb that might have been a letter. In the bulb, a bead of quicksilver.

He set it on the grey ground in front of him. The quicksilver lay in the bottom.

"That's dead ground," he said. "As dead as Hearthmere. Everything in the league's like that." He looked up at her. "Do you know why?"

"It's drinking it."

"Yes." He looked pleased, as if she'd got a sum right. "Exactly. It's drinking the ground dead. It lies on the stones and draws the Deep in from a league round, from the trees and the grass and the birds. And the stones fill." He nodded at the nearest one. "They're full. Brim-full. The only live ground in the league. And it drinks from them. And the ground goes grey from the stones outward, a hundred and twenty paces a week." He stopped. "I've measured it."

Idony Vael looked at the lion, lying at ten paces from the thing on the stones. Then he looked at her again, and his face was very polite.

"May I ask you something?"

"Yes."

"Does your lion," he said, "do the same thing? On a smaller scale?"

---

She was so surprised she nearly laughed.

He meant it. She could see he meant it. It wasn't an insult; it wasn't a trap. He wanted to know, the way Wren wanted to know things, all the way down. He was kneeling in the grey grass at ten paces from a lion the size of a dray-beast, in the middle of a league of dead ground, with a Titan breathing on the back of his neck, and he'd asked her, politely, whether her lion drank the world.

"No," said Maren. "He gives it back."

Idony looked at her.

Then he picked up the glass, and leaned forward, and set it down again on the grass at the edge of the green, where the roots were faintly coloured, a few paces from Orrun's flank.

The quicksilver lifted.

Off the bottom. Up the throat. A finger's breadth. And stayed there.

Idony Vael knelt in the grey grass and looked at it without saying anything at all. She watched his face. It went very still. Then something came into it, slowly, from underneath, like light coming up through water. He looked like a man who has been looking for a word in a book for a long time and has turned a page and there it is.

"Yes," he said softly. "Yes. I think he does."

He took a small book out of his coat and wrote in it. She saw him do it: two lines, very small, quick. Then he shut the book and put it away and looked at her.

"Thank you," he said.

She didn't know what for. She thought he'd tell her, some time, and he'd be very exact about it.

She went to Rowan on the eighty-first day.

She went round the ring on foot, alone, in plain sight, along the grey grass outside the stones, to the western side, where the knights' stakes went into the ground in their long curved line. She didn't take Orrun. She didn't take Haverly's sword out of its scabbard. She walked up to the line of stakes in the middle of the morning and stopped, and waited.

The knights at the stakes saw her. One of them went for Rowan.

Rowan came down from the camp on foot. Small, neat, iron-grey, with her helm under her arm and the ink on her finger. She stopped on the other side of the stakes.

They looked at each other.

They hadn't spoken since the fourth day after the flight. At Greyhold, in the yard, with the black book in Maren's coat and Rowan offering her a pardon and the first lance if she'd put the lion at the head of the crown's hunt. She'd said no. Rowan had looked at her and said, quietly: *You're Watch.*

She'd been right.

"Ashdown," said Rowan.

"Captain."

Rowan looked past her, across the ring, at the lion lying at ten paces from the head.

"He's grown," she said.

"Yes."

Rowan didn't say anything else for a while. Then she said it, plainly, the way she said everything, as if each word cost something and she didn't mean to waste any.

"The council's orders. The chain over the forelegs, staked, on the night the Isvaren reader names. Then carted, if it'll be carted, and if it won't, held here till it will." She paused. "Hearthmere's ground is dead. Every well's sour from the Mere to the Wend. The crown thinks a beast in chains can make it live again. It did for three hundred years."

"In chains," said Maren. "Like the other one."

Rowan looked at her.

"It is the other one," said Maren. "It's the same one. You want to put it back where it was."

"Yes."

"For your lamps."

"For the wells," said Rowan. "For the Candle Downs. For children in Hallowmere who've had nothing but sour water for eighty days." She didn't raise her voice. "Better that than loose."

Maren didn't say anything.

"The Wardens mean to stand on the stones," Rowan said. "To hold it, with their lamps, while we put the chain over. Their reader says the stones are the only place they can draw." She looked across the ring to the north, to the rise. "I don't trust them. I don't trust what their lamps are for. But I'll have the chain over its forelegs on the night."

She didn't ask Maren to help. She didn't tell her to go. She stood on the other side of the stakes with her helm under her arm and looked at Maren a moment longer.

"You were Watch," she said. "Weren't you. Before the end."

"Yes."

"Hale's book." It wasn't a question.

"Yes."

Rowan nodded slowly.

"Then you know what it cost," she said, "to keep it under. Three hundred years. Every one of you, every day, at noon." She put her helm on. "And you know what it costs to let it go."

She went back up to her camp.

Lisska came over the dead trees at noon on the eighty-second day.

Maren heard her before she saw her. A long way off, a crying, high and thin, coming from the south-west over the grey forest, and Orrun lifted his head for the first time in a day.

Then the green dragon came in over the treetops, low, so low her wing-tips nearly touched the grey crowns of the pines, and saw the clearing, and saw the thing on the stones.

She stopped crying.

She came down in the grey grass ten paces behind Orrun. Not beside him. Behind. She folded her wings and stood there, very still, very small, with her pale green eyes on the great shape lying across the ring, and didn't make a sound.

It was the quietest Maren had ever seen her.

*She's afraid,* said Orrun.

"So am I."

*So is she of you. A little. She thinks you'll be cross that she came in so low.* A pause. *She says she has come from the farmer.*

It took a long time, as it had on the Plains. Lisska talking, very quietly for Lisska. Orrun giving it to Maren slowly, a piece at a time.

*The farmer has gone to the uncle's steading. The one on the other side of the moor. Skarrow.* A gap. *With the book. The dead man's book.*

"And?"

*The uncle read it. Himself. Not had it read. Read it himself, every page, at his own table, with the farmer sitting across from him.* Another gap. *And the uncle said he'll come and look. At the other one. At the chief. He said he'll stand in the chief's yard and be called a murderer, if the farmer stands next to him.*

Maren let her breath out.

*She says the chief won't open his gate,* said Orrun. *She says the farmer is very tired. She says the farmer is learning his letters from his mother, in the warm cave, and he can write his own name now, and one of the letters is backwards.*

Maren put her hand over her mouth.

---

Lisska stayed two days. She wouldn't go near the Titan's head. She stayed at the edge of the ring, behind Orrun, or up on the rise with the Wardens, who stared at her and wrote things down, or sitting on a grey branch over the stream, looking at the thing on the stones and then quickly looking away.

On the second evening she said she had to go home.

*Why?* Maren asked, through Orrun.

The green dragon didn't answer for a long time.

*She doesn't know,* said Orrun at last. *She says she wants to be home before something. She doesn't know what.* A pause. *She says it's in the air. Like before weather.*

Lisska went at dawn on the eighty-fourth day, south-west, low over the grey trees, fast, and didn't look back.

Hesper taught her the knot at the edge of the ring.

Hesper had stayed with her the whole time. Not in the ring. She wouldn't go into the ring; it was the clans' place, she said, and the clans had said keep watch and do nothing else, and she was keeping watch. She sat at the foot of the outermost stone on the south side, on the grey grass, with Hush lying miserable beside her, and watched. Every morning she tried a finding-knot, out of habit. Every morning it came undone in her fingers.

On the eighty-fourth day, when the green dragon had gone, she said, "I'll show you anyway."

"Your knots don't hold here."

"No. Mine don't." Hesper held out a length of grey cord. "But I'm not bonded to the grey-maned. And you're not a Keeper. I don't know what you are." She shrugged. "So I'll show you, and we'll see."

---

It was simple. That was the first thing.

Three turns. Round two fingers, the cord going over and under, as you'd start a net. A twist, back on itself, to lock the turns. And the word. A short word in the clans' tongue, like water going over a stone: you said it on the twist, not before, not after. And all the while, in your head, the asking.

"What asking?"

"Whatever you need to find," said Hesper. "The way home. Water. A lost child. The place where the deer went." She turned the cord in her fingers. "You hold it in your head while you tie. Not words. The shape of it. The want." She looked at Maren. "And you tie it with something of what you're asking toward, or what you're asking with. A hair. A thread. A feather. So it knows."

Maren looked at the lion, lying at ten paces.

She got up and went to him, and knelt by his neck, and took hold of one long strand of his mane, iron-grey, almost white at the root, long as her arm.

*Yes,* he said, before she asked.

She pulled. It came away, a single hair, coarse as a horse's tail-hair and twice as long. She took it back to Hesper.

She tied the knot.

Three turns, with the hair laid into the cord. A twist. The word, on the twist, the water-over-stone word, and she got it wrong the first time and Hesper said it again for her and she got it nearly right. In her head, the asking. She didn't know what she was asking. She asked anyway. *Where. Which way. What's here.*

She held the knot loose in her palm, as Hesper showed her.

It lay there.

Grey cord, with a grey hair in it. String. It didn't pull. It didn't move. It lay in her hand like any piece of string in the world.

"Yes," said Hesper. "That's the grey."

---

He did it the next morning without being asked.

She was sitting by him in the grey grass with the knot in her hand, the same knot, tied the day before. She'd kept it. She didn't know why. She was turning it over in her fingers and looking at the thing on the stones, and he lifted his head off his paws for the first time that morning and laid his chin on her wrist.

The cord pulled.

Hard. All at once, in her fingers, as if something had hold of the other end and had jerked it. It drew tight across her palm. It pulled down. Toward the ground. And sideways, toward the nearest stone. And back, toward him, toward the great warm weight of his chin on her wrist. All three, at once, so hard her hand shook, so she couldn't tell which, as if the cord were trying to go three ways at once and couldn't choose.

She held on.

He lifted his chin.

String. Lying in her palm. Nothing.

She looked at it. She looked at him.

*Again,* he said.

He put his chin back on her wrist. It pulled. Down, sideways, back. Ground, stone, lion. He lifted it. String.

She did it four times. Each time the same. Each time, with his chin on her wrist, the cord came alive in her hand and pulled three ways at once; each time, when he took it away, it was string.

The fourth time she let go of it, because she couldn't feel her fingers.

---

They were numb.

All of them, the fingers of her right hand, from the tips to the second knuckle. Not cold. Not asleep, the pins-and-needles kind. Numb. As if they belonged to someone else. She could see them. She could move them, a little, if she looked at them while she did it. She couldn't feel them at all.

Hesper took her hand and turned it over and looked at it.

"Two days," she said. "Perhaps three." She put it down. "The knot took from you. It had to. There's nothing in this ground for it to take from." She looked at the lion. "It took from you, and it worked through him. On dead ground it works through him, or not at all."

"Which way was it pulling?"

"You'd know better than me."

"I don't know," said Maren. "Down. Toward the stone. Toward him." She flexed the dead fingers. "All of them. I couldn't tell."

Hesper thought about it.

"Maybe it's the same thing," she said. "Maybe down and the stone and him are all the same thing, here. Maybe that's what it was trying to tell you." She shrugged. "Knots don't explain. They pull. You work out the rest yourself."

Maren held her numb hand in her good one. The knot lay in the grey grass by Orrun's foreleg, where she'd dropped it. Grey cord with a grey hair in it.

She picked it up with her left hand and put it in her coat.

On the eighty-seventh day the Keepers brought word of the smoke-men.

They came to the edge of the grey on the south side, two of them, a man and a woman with wolves at heel, and wouldn't step over the line, and called to Hesper across it. Hesper went and stood on the grey side and talked to them a long time. Then she came back.

"Men in the green," she said. "To the west. A day out, upwind. In plain coats, with mules. Keeping off the knights' path, coming round by the old deer-trails." She sat down by Maren. "The mules carry pots. Closed pots, with lids sealed down with wax. A lot of pots."

Maren felt Orrun go still beside her.

"Smoke," she said.

"Smoke." Hesper looked at the grey grass. "They came for what the groves would have given them. We burned the groves. So they've brought their own." She was quiet. "From the coast, I'd think. Cooked in Saltreach. A great deal of it. For a beast that size."

"The ones from the knowe."

"Or their friends."

Maren went to Rowan that afternoon and told her.

Rowan listened on her side of the stakes. Then she said, "I know. My falconer saw them three days ago."

"What will you do?"

"Watch them," said Rowan. "As I'm watching the Wardens. As I'm watching you." She looked at Maren's right hand, which Maren was holding against her chest. "What happened to your hand?"

"A knot," said Maren.

Rowan looked at her a moment longer, and didn't ask.

Idony Vael's number went round the camps on the eighty-ninth day.

He'd been measuring the stones every morning, going down from the rise with his glass and setting it on one stone and then another, and every morning the quicksilver ran up to the top and pressed there, trembling. He'd been measuring the grey every evening, with a knotted cord and blazed trees. He'd been counting its breaths: eleven in, nineteen out, every day the same.

On the eighty-ninth day the breaths changed.

Eleven in. Twenty out.

He came down from the rise to tell Maren himself. He stopped at his polite twenty paces and told her, very exactly. Then he said the stones were full, as full as they could be, and that when a cup was full the draw had to change, and he thought it was changing. And that from the slope of it, the night the draw would turn was midsummer night.

"Eight days," he said. "Nine at the most."

"And then what?"

"I don't know," said Idony Vael. He looked at the thing on the stones. "Nobody's ever measured it before." He hesitated. "But I've told Magister Dane. And he's told the Knight-Captain. And they've both begun to get ready."

That night she sat by Orrun in the grey grass with her numb hand against her chest and the knot in her coat, and felt him lying at ten paces from his kin, and calling into the well again, and nothing coming up.

Up on the rise, the Wardens' fires. To the west, the knights' fires, and the long line of iron stakes going toward the beast in the dark. Somewhere beyond them, in the green, a day out, upwind, men in plain coats with mules and sealed pots.

And in the middle, the woman and the lion, ten paces from the head.

*They will chain it,* said Orrun.

"Yes."

*Or bind it. Or smoke it.* He didn't lift his head. *Or all three.*

"Yes."

He was quiet for a long time. On the tops of the stones the hollow owls sat with their pale faces turned toward the two of them, not flying.

*I kept the door,* he said at last. *Three hundred years. And they came through it anyway, with fire and chains.* A pause. *I am not at a door now. There is no door. There is only this.*

"I know."

*Then what do I keep?*

She didn't know. She sat beside him in the grey and didn't know, and the thing on the stones breathed out, twenty counts, long and slow, and the grey trees didn't move.
