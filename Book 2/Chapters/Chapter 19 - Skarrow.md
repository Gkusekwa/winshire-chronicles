---

He took the book to Skarrow because Arn wouldn't let Ulric in to look at it.

He'd known that since the wall. *Tell the farmer when he's proof, I'll come and look at it.* Ulric would come. Ulric had said so, at the gate, in front of everyone, through his man with the scar. And Arn would stand on the wall and not open it, and Ulric would sit his horse in the road below and look up, and the book would be in Calder's coat between them, and nobody would read it.

So he'd go to Ulric.

His mother wrapped the book in oiled cloth on the fifty-sixth day, at the cave mouth, very carefully, as she'd wrap a cheese for keeping, and tied it with string, and gave it to him.

"He can read," she said. "Ulric. Did you know? He was at the dues-school with Haldor. They used to fight over who'd hold the slate." She looked at the parcel in his hands. "He'll read it himself. Don't you read it to him. Don't you dare."

"I can't read it to him."

"No," said Ada. "Not yet."

---

He flew on Kharrak, with Lisska.

She came because she always came. She flew behind and a little above, as she did, but she didn't chatter. She hadn't said a word to him in the day since the mill page. Not on the ledge, not at the cave, not in the air. She was there, at the back of his head, as always. She just wasn't talking.

He didn't make her.

They went east over the Scarfell in the morning, over the long brown swells of the moor and the high grey fells, over Coldtarn black in its bowl, and down. The other side of the Scarfell was greener. The land fell east toward the coast in long valleys with becks in them, and stone walls going up the sides of the valleys in lines, and sheep on the slopes like scattered salt.

*Skarrow,* said Kharrak, and went down.

---

It was bigger than he remembered from the winter. Then it had been a stockade in the snow and a man in the gate who wouldn't let him past it. He'd never been inside. Nobody from Harrow's End went to Skarrow. It was the far side of the Scarfell, the coast side, and the Varr who held it was the Varr you didn't sell barley to unless you had to.

It lay in the bottom of a long green valley with a beck going down the middle of it, inside the black pine stockade, its logs gone grey now: a stone hall with a turf roof as long as the Wheel at home, and longhouses, and byres, and folds, and smoke going up from all of them. And outside the stockade, in the fields, camped, the men. Three hundred, perhaps. Plain grey cloaks, no thread. Tents and fires and horses on long pickets. The larger part of the Clan's fighting men, who'd gone east with Ulric in the autumn and never come back.

There were no dragons.

Ulric wasn't bonded. He'd never been. The best warrior in the Clan, Calder had heard all his life, and he'd never stood in front of a young dragon on a choosing day and had it choose him. It had stood in front of him, everyone said, more than once, and looked at him, and turned away.

The red dragon came down in the field above the hall, and three hundred men in plain grey stood up from their fires.

---

The man with the scar met him at the gate.

The same lean old rider on the same grey hill-pony, with the white scar through one eyebrow. He looked at Calder, and at the red dragon in the field behind him, and at the green one sitting on the wall of a sheepfold not looking at anybody.

"Orm Garrick," he said. Not unfriendly. "Of Skarrow." He looked at the parcel under Calder's arm. "That's it, is it?"

"That's it."

Orm Garrick looked at it a moment longer.

"He's in the hall," he said. "He said if you came, you'd come today. He said you'd not wait to be asked." He turned the pony. "He said you never do."

---

Ulric was sitting at his table at the end of the hall, with one lamp.

He was heavier than Calder remembered. Broader, as if the winter had settled on him. His beard had gone greyer. He sat at the high table at the end of the long hall, alone, with a cup in front of him and nothing else, and when Calder came in at the far door he didn't get up. He watched him come the whole length of the hall, slow, with his big hands flat on the table.

He had, Calder thought, the face he remembered from the stockade gate in the snow: a face that held a great deal in it and gave away nothing at all.

"Farmer," said Ulric.

"Ulric."

"Sit."

Calder sat. Across the table. He put the parcel down between them, on the boards, and kept his hand on it.

Ulric looked at it. Then at him.

"Tell it," he said. "From the start. I heard it read in my yard in the winter, in your mother's hand, by an old woman of the ledges, and I listened to the end. Now tell it to me yourself. With your mouth." He folded his hands. "I want to hear how it sounds when it's not written down."

---

So Calder told it.

The third time. The arch first: lying on the boards in the tower at the Old Ruin, with his face to the crack, and Rook's pleasant ordinary voice coming up out of the firelight below. The horse. The gorse. *He came down alone at first light. He always did. He wanted to be first.* The reins held out. *He never drew. He went in the water like a stone.* The grey armband on the alders, plain, no thread, left there on purpose. The Clan axe in the shallows. *The farmer found them. Arn found the farmer.* The mill: the crossbow in the hedge, bought, to put the woman on the green dragon out of the fight. The spit. Rook with the six-knife coming at him so that Kharrak would kill him, and Kharrak doing it. The book, in the paymaster's chest. Delling. The Plough. The road.

He told that too. The road, and who'd walked it.

Ulric didn't interrupt once. He sat with his hands folded on the table and his eyes on Calder's face, the whole time, and didn't move.

When it was done, Calder stopped. The lamp burned.

Ulric looked at him for a long time.

"It sounds," he said at last, slowly, "the same."

He unfolded his hands.

"Give me the book."

---

He read it all night.

Calder hadn't known a man could sit so still for so long. Ulric unwrapped the oiled cloth, and set the book in front of him under the lamp, and opened it, and began at the first page, and read. He didn't move his lips. He didn't trace the lines with his finger, the way Ada did. He read as a man looks at a field, all of it at once, and then turned the page with a wetted thumb, carefully, so the dried pages wouldn't tear, and read the next.

Calder sat across the table and watched him.

Somebody brought another lamp at midnight. Somebody brought bread and cheese and a jug, and Ulric didn't touch it, and Calder didn't either. Outside, the fires of three hundred men burned down in the fields. The red dragon slept in the field above the hall. The green one sat on the sheepfold wall in the dark, not talking.

He stopped twice.

The first time was the drover's wage. Calder saw him come to it. Saw his eyes stop on the line and stay there.

*The drover, Varrhold, paid, for the winter: the uncle's steward, four silver and keep.*

Ulric looked at it.

"My steward," he said. Very quietly. "Paid him. Four silver and keep. Out of my own purse. For driving my sheep down to Cotter's Cross in the back end of the year." He didn't look up. "He was a good drover. That was the thing. He knew sheep."

He turned the page.

The second time was the horse.

*A horse, from the uncle's lines; to the coast for my mother. Returned.*

Ulric stopped, and didn't turn the page, and didn't move, for so long that Calder thought he'd fallen asleep with his eyes open.

"My horse," he said.

The lamp guttered. Somebody had let the oil get low.

"A bay mare. With a white sock on the near hind. Out of my own lines." Ulric's big hands were flat on either side of the book. "He came to me in the yard. The night before the ford. Before Teo's meeting. Said his mother was dying, on the coast, and could he have a horse, and he'd be back in four days. And I gave it him. Myself. In my own yard. With my own hand on the bridle." He looked at the line. "Returned. He brought her back. In four days, as he said. Lathered. I thought he'd ridden hard to see his mother."

Calder didn't say anything.

"He rode her to the ford," said Ulric. "He lay up in the gorse with my mare tied in the alders behind him, and the boy came down at first light, and waved." His voice didn't change. "And then he rode her home to me and gave me back the reins."

He turned the page.

---

He finished at dawn.

He read the last page. Then he turned the book over and opened the back cover and read the list stuck inside it, the five lines in the older ink. He read it twice. Calder saw him read the fifth line twice.

Then he closed the book, and put both hands flat on the cover, and sat there with the grey light coming in through the smoke-hole and the lamps burned out.

"Sedge," he said.

"Delling's. Out of Wendmouth."

"And he walked home over my nephew's moor, on your road."

"Yes."

Ulric looked at him across the table. A long look. The face that held everything and gave away nothing gave away, for a moment, something. Calder didn't know what it was. It was gone before he could see.

"You knew," said Ulric. "When you gave it. You knew who'd walk."

"I knew it was Delling's. Not which."

"And you gave it anyway."

"For the book."

Ulric nodded slowly.

"My nephew will say you sold Hallam for it," he said. "And Imra. And the Sallow girl's green dragon, sitting out there on my wall." He looked toward the door. "He'll say you bought a dead man's book with live men's lives and it's worth what you paid."

"He's said it."

"Yes," said Ulric. "He would." He was quiet. Then: "He's wrong."

---

He gave his answer in his yard, in the morning, in front of his men.

He came out of the hall with the book under his arm and stood on the step, and the yard filled. Three hundred men in plain grey, out of the fields, standing in the mud between the byres and the folds, with Orm Garrick at the front on his pony. And the farmer from Harrow's End standing on the step beside the chief's uncle, in a Clan cloak that wasn't his.

Ulric didn't raise his voice. He'd never needed to.

"In the autumn," he said, "my brother's son was killed at the ford on Hollow Beck, under a meeting I'd agreed to. The red thread said my men did it. They found my grey on the alders and a Clan axe in the water." He let it sit. "I said I didn't. Nobody believed me. My nephew stood in my yard and called me his brother's murderer, and I came east, and you came with me, and we've been at war with our own blood since the harvest."

The yard was quiet.

"I've read a book," said Ulric Varr. "All night. Every page. It's a dead man's book, and it says who did it, and how, and what he was paid, and whose horse he rode." He held it up. "Mine. He rode my horse. I gave it him."

Nobody moved.

"So." Ulric lowered the book. "I'll go to Varrhold. I'll stand in my nephew's yard and be called a murderer again. To his face. Under the Roosts. And I'll stand there and take it until he's read this, if it takes all summer." He turned his head and looked at Calder. "If the farmer stands beside me."

Calder looked at him.

"Not in his hall," said Ulric. "In his yard. Where he can see me."

"I'll stand there," said Calder.

---

The fur-trader came in at noon.

Calder was in the yard, by the trough, when the bell at the gate rang, and he went to look because everyone did. A pony-train coming down the valley from the moor: eight shaggy ponies roped head to tail, loaded high with bundles under hide covers, and a man walking at the head of them in a long coat of some grey-and-black fur, with his hair in plaits and his face painted with two red bars across the cheekbones.

Wyldmarch. Calder had seen them at the Cross, once or twice, in a good year. They came over from the Plains with pelts and amber and horn and went home with iron and salt.

Orm Garrick went down to him. They talked at the gate, in a mix of the common tongue and something else, with their hands. The trader unroped the first pony and showed Orm what was under the hide: pelts, grey and silver and black, and a sack of amber that caught the light.

Then the trader said something, and Orm stopped, and looked back up the yard at Calder.

"Come here," Orm called.

---

"He says the crowns are coming to the Thornwood," said Orm Garrick. "The deep forest. Out east." He listened to the trader, and went on. "Knights, from over the Wend. Thirty, with a chain on six wains, each link as long as a man. Crossing the grass toward the forest when he left. And Isvaren's colleges coming down from the north, with snow-cats, and lamps on mules. Going to the place where the great one came down." He listened again. "He says the clans won't stop them and won't help. The clans' old ones said it was kin and you don't hunt kin, and then sat down."

"And the lion?" said Calder.

Orm looked at him.

"Ask him," said Calder. "Ask him if there was a lion."

Orm asked. The trader looked at Calder, at the farmer in the borrowed cloak with the bond marks on his chest that the trader couldn't see but seemed to know were there. He said something. A long thing. And then a short word, two syllables, that he said twice.

"He says there was a lion," said Orm. "A grey one, as big as a dray-horse. And a woman with it. A lowlander. At the Speaking Stones, in front of the clans' old ones." He listened. "He says the old ones asked what she was to it."

"And?"

"And a Keeper stood up and said: she sits with him." Orm listened again. "He says that's what the clans call her now. That word. *The one who sits with it.*"

Calder stood at the gate of Skarrow with the trader's ponies blowing beside him and the smell of the pelts in his nose and didn't say anything.

On the sheepfold wall behind him, Lisska lifted her head.

---

Ulric rode to Varrhold on the sixtieth day, with twenty men.

Twenty, in plain grey, and Orm Garrick, and Ulric on a big grey horse at the head. Not three hundred. Not an army. Twenty men, the number you'd take to a ford meeting. Calder flew over them on Kharrak, slow, keeping to their pace, with Lisska behind.

They came up the glen to the hold at dusk on the sixty-first day.

The gate was shut.

There were men on the wall. More than usual. Red thread, crossbows, torches. And among them, in the middle, over the gate, in black, Arn.

Ulric stopped his horse in the road below the gate.

Calder brought Kharrak down in the heather by the road and slid off and walked to Ulric's stirrup and stood there. On the near side. Where Arn could see him.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

"Uncle," said Arn at last, from the wall.

"Nephew."

"You've brought him."

"He brought me," said Ulric. "With a book."

Arn looked down at the farmer at his uncle's stirrup. In the torchlight his face was long and sharp and tired all the way down.

"I know about the book," he said. "Bought from the company that shot Hallam Brecc on the hill, under the truce. Paid for with my moor. Two hundred of them walking over my heather, home, with the ledges watching them do it." He didn't raise his voice. "I know what's in the book, uncle. I've been told."

"Then read it," said Ulric.

"I'll not touch it."

"Read it, boy."

"I'll not touch it," said Arn, "and I'll not open my gate to the man who sat in his yard all winter while my brother was in the ground, and I'll not open it to the man who sold my moor to buy him a story." He put his hands on the wall-top. "You can sit there."

"I will," said Ulric.

And he did.

---

He sat his horse in the road below the gate all night.

Calder stood at his stirrup. He didn't sit down. Ulric didn't get down. The twenty men in plain grey stood behind them in the road with their horses' reins in their hands, and didn't sit either. On the wall above, the torches burned, and the men with the red thread stood and watched, and Arn stood among them, in black, and watched, and didn't go in.

Some time after midnight Calder heard feet on the long stair in the rock.

He didn't turn round. He didn't need to. Lanterns, coming down, slowly, as old people come down stairs. Across the back of the yard. Out through the postern, which the men on the wall opened for them without being asked, because nobody in the Clan stands in the way of the bonded.

The ledges came out into the road and stood behind him.

Not Gunna; Gunna was at the caves with his family. Hulda Sarr, with Harl on the cliff above her. Old Brede. Ivar Leck, who'd come down from the setters' ledge on his stick. The boy Leif. Six, seven, eight of them. They stood in the road behind the farmer at Ulric's stirrup, with their lanterns. Above them, on the cliff, their dragons came out of the caves one by one and sat on the ledges with their heads down, looking at the gate.

Arn looked at them from the wall.

He didn't move.

---

At dawn Ulric turned his horse.

He didn't say he was going. He gathered the reins and turned the big grey round in the road, slowly, until he was facing down the glen, and then he looked back over his shoulder, up at the wall, at his nephew in black among the torches gone pale in the morning.

"When you're ready to read it, boy," said Ulric Varr, "you know where I am."

He'd said it before. Calder had heard him say it, in the winter, before the second ford. *When you're ready to say it, farmer, you know where I am.* He said it now the same way. Slow. Deep. Calm.

On the wall, Arn didn't answer. He stood a moment longer. Then he turned and went down off the wall, out of sight, without a word.

The gate stayed shut.

---

Ulric gave him the book back on the road down the glen.

"It's yours," he said, holding it out from the saddle, in its oiled cloth. "You paid for it. Not me. You'll need it, for the next time." He looked at the parcel. "There'll be a next time. He'll read it. He's a Varr. We're slow, but we get there." He paused. "His father took nine years to admit he'd been wrong about a horse. I'm hoping for less."

Calder took the book.

"I'll ride back with you," he said. "To Skarrow. As far as the edge."

Ulric looked at him.

"Why?"

"Because somebody should," said Calder. "Because you sat there all night."

Ulric didn't say anything to that. But he didn't say no.

---

They found the steadings on the sixty-fourth day.

On the moor's edge, on the way east, above the Skell Water where it ran down out of the fells. A morning of low cloud and wind. Lisska saw the smoke first, and said nothing, and Calder felt her see it and looked where she was looking: two smudges on the grey sky ahead, half a league apart, going up thin.

Ulric saw them a moment later, from the saddle, and put his horse to a gallop.

The first steading was an Arn steading. Calder knew it from the gatepost: a red thread wound round it, as the red-thread families had marked their gates since the split. It was a small place, a longhouse and a fold, on the lip of the moor where it went down into the Skell Water's valley. It had burned in the night. The roof had fallen in. The longhouse door was black, and hanging, and tied to the door, on the latch, with a careful knot, was a strip of plain grey cloth.

There were two dead in the yard. A shepherd and a boy. The boy was about Pip's age.

Ulric got down off his horse and stood in the yard and looked at the grey cloth on the door.

His men came up behind him and looked too, and Calder heard them go quiet, and then not quiet.

The second steading was half a league east, along the edge. An Ulric steading. Calder knew it because there was no thread on the gatepost at all, the way the grey families did it. It had burned too. Nobody dead; the family had seen the fire at the first steading and run to the high fold with the sheep, and were still there, in the cloud, crying. On the gate, tied with the same careful knot, a red thread.

---

Ulric's men wanted to ride on Varrhold.

They said it in the yard of the second steading, with the smoke going up behind them. Twenty men, and Orm Garrick, and some of them shouting. The red thread on the gate. A Varrhold thread. Arn's men had done it, in the night, while their master stood on his wall and wouldn't open his gate. They'd done it to burn Ulric's people, and they'd burned one of their own first to make it look like Ulric. Or the other way round.

"Stop," said Ulric.

He didn't shout. They stopped.

He stood in the yard of the burned steading, by the gate with the red thread on it, and looked at the thread. Then he looked east, along the edge, at the smoke from the first steading, half a league off, with a grey cloth on its door.

"Grey on his," he said. "Red on mine. On the same night. Half a league apart." He turned his head and looked at Calder. "Farmer. How many times have you seen that done?"

"Twice," said Calder. "Before."

The ford. The grey armband on the alders. Upper Rush, and the Clan blamed. And Harrow's End, all winter.

"Somebody wants us to ride on Varrhold," said Ulric. "Somebody wants my nephew to ride on Skarrow." He looked at his men. "That's what this is for. Not the sheep. Not the shepherd. This." He put his hand on the gate. "Somebody's tied a knot for us, and wants us to pull."

"Who?"

Ulric didn't answer.

Calder said to Lisska, without speaking: *Go for the ledges. Fast. Bring someone who can read a yard.*

The green dragon went up off the fold wall without a word and was gone over the edge to the west.

---

Hulda Sarr came down in the afternoon, with Harl and old Brede and his blue.

She'd never said a word to Calder. Not on the Scarfell's edge, not on the peat road, not in front of the pens. She came down off the black-and-grey's shoulder in the yard of the first steading and looked at the grey cloth on the door, and at the two dead under their cloaks, and at Ulric. Then she knelt down in the mud of the yard.

She stayed there a long time. Moving slowly, on her knees, from one place to another, with her face close to the ground. Looking. Calder watched her. He'd seen her do it before; the whole Clan had. At the second ford, after Hallam. The boots in the mud by the gorse.

She stood up.

"Wendmouth boots," said Hulda Sarr.

Her voice was rough, as if it didn't get used. Ulric's men went quiet.

"Brass eyelets. Here, and here, and here." She pointed, at marks in the mud that Calder couldn't see. "Like the ford. Like the gorse, the second time." She looked at Calder. "Like the drover's boots. The ones in your mother's paper."

"How many?"

"Forty. More." She looked south-west, down the slope of the moor, toward the Skell Water and the Way beyond it. "Going that way. Toward the Ram's Head. In the night." She wiped her hands on her cloak. "Hired men. Not Clan. Not one Clan boot in the yard."

She turned and walked back to her dragon. At Harl's shoulder she stopped, and looked back at Ulric.

"Not one," she said again.

---

It had come up onto the moor.

Calder stood in the yard of the burned steading in the afternoon with the smoke still going up and understood it. The war. The man upstairs at the Fleece, with three thousand men waiting on silver that hadn't come, and a printing press, and a ring of six painted round a ram. Sending forty hired men in Wendmouth boots up the Skell Water in the dark, to burn two steadings on the edge of the Scarfell and tie a grey cloth on one and a red thread on the other.

To keep the Varrs at each other's throats. To keep the Clan looking at itself. To keep the dragons on the ledges, under the old law, not looking south.

It was the ford again. It was Teo again. It was the same knot, tied the same way, by the same hands.

He thought of Delling at his table. *I've known him two years.*

---

Ulric sent Orm Garrick to Varrhold the next day.

With a strip of the grey cloth from the first steading's door, and a board. A plank from the burned fold, with a boot-print in it: Hulda had found one, clear, pressed into a patch of clay by the trough, and Ulric's men had cut the clay out with a spade and laid it on the plank, and it had dried there in the wind. A Wendmouth boot. The brass eyelets, pressed in the clay like a row of seeds.

Orm took it to Varrhold's gate on his grey pony and left it there, on the step, with the grey cloth on top of it. Under the wall. Where Arn could see it.

Arn sent nothing back.

But on the sixty-eighth day, when Calder came down to the setters' ledge from the caves, Gorm Askell was waiting for him at the foot of the long stair, sour and inky and limping.

"He rode out," said Gorm. "Two days ago. On his own. Nobody with him." He looked at the stair, not at Calder. "To the burned place on the edge. The Brack shepherd's. He stood in the yard an hour. Then he rode back."

"Did he say anything?"

"No." Gorm sniffed. "He took the board in, though. Off the step. It's in the hall. Under the chair."

---

He learned his letters for ten days.

At the caves in the evenings, with Ada, with a burnt stick on the warm floor. Wren beside him, impatient, correcting. He'd had one letter the night of the mill page, and none of it right. By the seventy-fifth day he had all of the ones in his own name, and he could write it, if he went slowly and nobody watched.

*CALDER.*

Crooked. The L too big. The D facing the wrong way.

"That's backwards," said Wren.

"It's not."

"It is. The D. The belly goes the other way. Like this." She took the stick and drew it on the floor beside his. "See?"

He looked at the two Ds side by side. Hers, small and round, going the right way. His, big and bent, going the wrong one.

"I see," he said, and rubbed his out with his thumb, and drew it again.

---

The old one's wound closed under the moss.

Not quickly. He didn't look at it; the Keeper had said not to. But he felt it, through the bond, a little more each night: the heat going out of her chest, the sullen coal in the grate going grey, the smell coming and going and then, one morning, not coming at all.

*It is better,* said Aurveth, on the seventieth day.

"You said it was nothing."

*I said it was nothing when it was something.* She stretched her neck along the warm rock. *Now I'm saying it's better. That's different. That's true.*

Ivar Leck came at noon and put his hands on the splints, and listened, and sat back on his heels and wiped his beard.

"Another month," he said. "Then we'll see if she'll go off the ledge."

---

He sent Lisska east on the seventy-eighth day.

She'd come to him on the setters' ledge in the evening. Not asked. Just come, and sat down beside him, with her head turned east, and he'd known.

"You want to go."

She didn't answer for a while.

*She should know,* said Lisska. *About the uncle. And the book. And the gate.*

It was the first thing she'd said to him since the mill page. Twenty-three days. He sat very still, so as not to frighten it away.

"Tell her the uncle read it," he said. "All night. Every page. Tell her the uncle will stand in the yard. Tell her the chief won't open the gate." He paused. "And the steadings. Tell her somebody's tying knots on the edge."

*I'll tell her.*

"Don't be gone long."

*I won't.* She shifted her feet on the warm rock. Then she said, without looking at him: *Calder.*

"Yes."

*The hedge man. Sedge. Is he home now? In Wendmouth?*

Calder didn't answer at once.

"I think so," he said. "By now."

*Good,* said Lisska.

She stood up. She stretched her wings, one and then the other, green in the last light.

*Then I know where he is,* she said.

She went up off the ledge, east, into the evening, toward the Plains and the forest and the lion-woman who sat with it. He felt her go, thinner and thinner over the fells.

He sat on the ledge a long time with his hand on the old one's jaw. He didn't know what she'd meant. He was afraid to know. He thought he knew anyway.
