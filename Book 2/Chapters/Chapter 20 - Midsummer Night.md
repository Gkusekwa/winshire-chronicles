---

At dusk the owls went.

All of them at once. Maren was sitting by Orrun in the grey grass at ten paces from the head, as she had for twenty-four days, and she saw them go: every hollow owl on every stone, lifting off together without a sound, great pale wings opening in the last of the light. They went up over the ring in a slow wheel, round and round, a dozen pale shapes against the darkening sky. Then they broke and went away east over the grey trees, and didn't come back.

The thing on the stones breathed in. Eleven.

It breathed out. She counted. Nineteen. Twenty. Twenty-one.

Twenty-four.

*It's changing,* said Orrun.

All round the ring, in the grey dusk, she heard men begin to move.

---

She'd watched them get ready for nine days.

She'd watched the Wardens carry ladders down from the rise, long ash ladders lashed with rawhide, and lay them in the grey grass at the feet of the stones. She'd watched the two Scribes set up the lamps. Six iron tripods, as tall as a man, at the feet of six of the stones on the north side of the ring and the west. From each tripod, on a short chain, a lamp: a tall iron cage with panes of thick crystal in it, so thick they were green at the edges, and cut all over with marks she couldn't read. Idony could. He'd looked at them for a long time on the first day and then walked away and not looked at them again.

She'd watched the knights run their stakes.

Two long curved lines of iron stakes, coming in from the west side of the ring toward the forelegs, one line either side, like the two arms of a man reaching to hold something. A hundred stakes, perhaps. Each as long as a man, with an iron eye at the top. Driven into the grey ground with three blows, every one, because the grey ground was soft as ash.

And behind the stakes, on the six wains, the chain. Brought up close now, the oxen gone. Heaped in loops on the wains like a sleeping snake, each link as long as her body, still bright.

She'd watched it all and said nothing. Nobody had asked her anything. Rowan had looked at her once, across the ring, on the seventh day, and then looked away.

Now the Wardens were going up the ladders.

---

Ten of them. One to each of the ten stones they could reach. Blue and grey robes, going up the long ash ladders in the dusk, and kneeling on the tops of the stones, and putting both hands flat on the stone in front of them.

Dane went up the tallest, the north stone. She could see him against the sky: big, broad, his robe dark against the last light, kneeling.

On the stone nearest her on the north side, by the beck, the young red-haired one. Pask, Idony had said his name was. He knelt on the stone's top with his hands flat and his face down.

Nothing happened for a long breath.

Then the lamps lit.

Not with flame. With light. White, cold, coming from inside the thick green-edged crystal, out of nothing, as frost comes on a pane. All six at once. They lit, and the light came out of them, not spreading like lamplight but going straight: six beams, white, thin as spear-shafts, going up from the lamps at the stones' feet and out over the ring and crossing over the great grey back of the thing on the stones.

Where they crossed, they caught on each other. Like threads. Like a net being drawn tight. A web of white light over the thing's back, from stone to stone, with the six lamps burning at its edges and the ten Wardens kneeling on their stones above it, still as statues, with their hands flat and their faces down.

The thing on the stones breathed out. Twenty-four.

Its half-shut eyes, ten paces from her, pale as ice under water, went blank.

She'd seen that before. In the collars, on the knowe. In Old Grey's face, walking behind Wren. In the snow lynx on the forest's edge in the smoke. One moment there was something behind the eyes. The next there was nobody.

Beside her, Orrun made a sound she'd never heard him make. Low. From the bottom of his chest.

---

The chain went over at full dark.

She watched it go. Three teams of labourers, thirty-odd men each, with knights in among them hauling too, came out from behind the stakes with the chain on their shoulders, in loops, as you'd carry a rope on a ship. They ran with it, under the white net, past the stakes, in toward the forelegs. Across the grey grass. Shouting. Rowan on foot behind them, small and neat, with her helm on, pointing. And the hounds working ahead in the dark, Wynn's dark one and his pale one, Ash and Sorrow, running out and back along the lines.

They threw the loops over the forelegs.

Over the great grey paws, the size of haywains, and up over the wrists, where the old broken chain-ends hung from their cuffs. The labourers ran the loops out to either side and dropped them over the stakes' iron eyes. One stake on the left line. One on the right. Pinned. Then the next loop. Then the next.

The thing on the stones didn't move.

Under the white net, with its eyes blank, it lay with its head on its paws and let them do it.

*No,* said Orrun.

She felt him get up. She felt it through the bond before she saw it: the great weight of him gathering, rising out of the grey grass where he'd lain for twenty-four days.

"Wait."

*No.*

"Wait. Not the chain. Not yet." She was on her feet too. She was looking at the stones. At the kneeling Wardens with their hands flat. At the six lamps at their feet, burning white. At the net. "The chain's only iron. It could lift a hill. You saw it go up out of the river with the other chains still on it. The chain's nothing." She swallowed. "It's the lamps. It's them. On the stones. That's what's holding it."

He stood beside her in the dark, shaking.

*Then the stones,* he said.

---

The smoke came an hour after dark, out of the west.

She smelled it before she saw it. Sweet. Heavy. Honey and resin, the grove-smell, the knowe-smell. Coming across the grey on the night wind in a long low bank, white in the light from the lamps, rolling in over the stakes and the labourers and the knights' fires and the chain. Out of the green beyond the grey's edge, a league west, where a line of small red fires had sprung up along the dark of the trees.

The smoke-men.

She'd known they'd come. Hesper had said: they'll want smoke, for a beast that size, a great deal of it. They'd brought their own. They'd waited in the green, upwind, a day out, with their mules and their sealed pots, until the chain was going over and the lamps were lit and the thing on the stones was held. And now they'd lit the pots.

To open it. While it was held. So someone could walk up in the confusion and put a hand on its head.

The smoke came over the stakes. The labourers coughed and swore and kept working. It came over the chain, over the forelegs, over the ring.

It came to Orrun first.

She felt him go. All at once, the way he'd gone at the grove, at a league off. Only he wasn't a league off now. He was here, and the smoke was here, and it went into him like water into a cracked pot.

Slow. Heavy. Far away. Down, into the dark, into the well.

She had a cloth. She'd soaked it in the grey beck at dusk because Hesper had told her to, and it was round her neck, and she pulled it up over her mouth and nose. She didn't think about herself. She reached for him.

*Orrun.*

Nothing. A long way down.

*ORRUN.*

She went after him. Down the bond, down the well, the way she'd gone on the knowe with her hand where the binder's hand had been. Into the dark where he was going, the sweet heavy dark, and she took hold of him there, the whole of him, and held on.

*Here,* she said. *Here. With me. Not down there. Here.*

A long, long moment.

*Here,* he said. Thick. From very far. *I'm here.*

*Stay here.*

*I'm here. I'm here.*

She held him. She'd never held anything so hard. She stood in the grey grass with the wet cloth over her face and the sweet smoke going past her in the white light, and held on to the lion with everything she had, and felt him come up, slowly, out of the dark, like a man coming up out of deep water.

*The stones,* he said, when he was there.

"The stones."

---

She went for the one by the beck.

It was the nearest. The fourth stone, counting round from the north-west, where the grey beck came in under the trees and ran across the edge of the ring between two stones and out to the east, a pace wide, knee-deep, cold. Pask was on it. Kneeling, with his hands flat, with the lamp at the stone's foot burning white and its beam going up over the thing's back into the net.

She ran.

Orrun came with her. Through the smoke, through the white light, past the beck. The ladder was against the stone, the long ash ladder. She went up it. Haverly's sword was on her back; she'd slung it there at dusk. She went up the rungs as fast as she'd gone up the rigging of the river-watch boats in a storm, and at the top she put her hand on the stone's edge.

Pask lifted his head.

He was young. Twenty, perhaps. Red hair, a thin pale face, sweat on it in the cold. His eyes, in the white light, were very wide. He looked at her coming up over the edge of his stone and she saw him understand what she was, and what she was for.

He took one hand off the stone and pushed it at her, flat, palm out.

It hit her like a door.

Not his hand; he wasn't near enough to touch her. Something else. Weight. A wall of it, coming off his palm, slamming into her chest so hard the breath went out of her and her hands came off the stone and the ladder went over backward under her, and she was falling.

She didn't hit the ground.

She hit Orrun. Grey, enormous, standing at the foot of the stone where the ladder had been, and she came down across his shoulders and his mane, and he didn't move under her, and she lay there a moment with no breath in her and the white light going round.

*Again,* said Orrun.

---

He went up the stone himself.

She didn't know he could. She felt him do it through the bond, the earth in him, the stone answering. He set his forepaws on the stone's side and the stone took them. Not gave way: took them, the way the gallery stone under the river had opened for him, the way the floor had held under the knowe. His claws went into the stone a finger's depth, as if it were clay, and held. He went up the side of it, with her on his back, slow, a forepaw and then a forepaw.

At the top, Pask looked up from his hands, and saw a lion coming over the edge of his stone with a knight on its back, and screamed.

He took both hands off the stone. He pushed them at her, both, flat. She felt the weight come, and Orrun leaned into it like a horse into a gale, and it went past them, and the edge of it caught her ear and made it ring.

She came off Orrun's back onto the stone's top and took the Warden round the waist.

She didn't think about it. She didn't decide. Pell had taught it every day for two years in the yard at Greyhold: when a man's got something in his hands you can't stand against, don't stand against it. Go in under it. Take him off his feet. She went in under his arms with her shoulder in his belly and her arms round him and drove with her legs, and they went off the edge of the stone together into the dark.

Down. A long way. Long enough to know she'd misjudged it.

Into the beck.

---

The water was so cold it stopped her heart.

She went under. The Warden went under with her. She felt his body jerk in her arms, and something go out of him: not his breath. Something else. Like a lamp being blown out. Like a string being cut. She felt it go out of him in the water, all at once, whatever he'd been holding, the pull, the weight, the draw from the stone. Gone. Running water under him, and the pull broken outright, as Idony had said it would be.

She came up. He came up beside her, choking, flailing, his robe dragging him down.

Above them, at the foot of the stone, his lamp went out.

Not dimmed. Out. The white light in the crystal panes snuffed like a candle, and the beam that had gone up from it into the net over the thing's back was gone, and the net, where it had been, sagged.

She got the Warden by the collar and dragged him to the bank and pushed him up onto the grey grass. He lay there on his face, coughing up beck-water, shaking.

Alive. She left him there.

Pell would have said: *Subdue. Don't kill.* She thought of the beck in the Fellwood and the two men on its far bank, and of the lion standing in the water with the pink going away downstream.

She climbed out.

---

Orrun had come down off the stone. He was standing at its foot, in the white light of the other lamps, with the smoke going past him. Looking at the next stone. And the next.

On the next stone, a Warden had lifted his head.

A woman, older, grey-haired. Ysolde Marr, with the lynx. Kneeling on her stone with her hands flat and her face turned toward the beck, toward the dark stone where Pask had been, and the dark lamp at its foot. And toward the lion.

Orrun put his head down and walked toward her stone.

Not fast. He didn't need to go fast. He walked across the grey grass toward the foot of Ysolde Marr's stone, slowly, with his head down and his mane up, a lion the size of a dray-beast coming at her out of the smoke and the white light, and the ground under his feet heaving a little with every step, so that the stone she knelt on rocked.

She stood up.

She took her hands off the stone, and stood, and looked at the lion coming, and Maren saw her face and saw her decide.

She went down the ladder. Fast. Off the stone, onto the grey grass.

Her lamp went out.

On the next stone along, the Warden there, a man, watched her do it, and watched the lion turn his head toward him, and didn't wait. He came down his ladder too. His lamp went out as his feet touched the grey.

Maren understood. Down on the grey ground they had nothing to draw from but themselves. And they'd seen what that did. Every Warden had heard the old stories, the drawers on dead ground going grey in a night. They wouldn't.

Three lamps out of six.

Over the thing's back the net sagged, and frayed, three white threads where there'd been six, swinging loose.

---

The fourth lamp hung on its tripod at the foot of the north-west stone, burning white.

Nobody was going to come down off that stone. The Warden on it was kneeling with his face down and his hands flat and wasn't looking at anything. She ran to the foot of it, round the outside of the ring, through the smoke, with Haverly's sword out.

The letters on the blade were dark.

They'd been dark since the morning after the river. Since Greyhold. Dark at the reeds, dark in the black book's cavern, dark on the Saltreach bank and the Scarfell and the Plains and the grey. Lucan Haverly's quickened edge, that had burned in his hand, that Grieve had given her in the yard. On dead ground it was only steel.

Steel was enough for a lamp-chain.

The lamp hung from the tripod's top on a short chain, four links, as thick as her finger. She swung the sword two-handed, the way Pell taught you to cut a rope on a boat, and the edge went through the chain at the second link with a sound like a bell.

The lamp fell.

It came down on the grey stones at the tripod's foot, the great iron cage with its thick green-edged panes, and the panes broke. All of them, at once, with a sound she'd remember: like ice breaking on a pond in spring, all over the pond at the same moment. The white light went out of the crystal and was gone.

On the stone above her, the Warden screamed, once, and fell forward on his face on the stone's top and lay there.

Four out of six.

Over the thing's back the net was two threads, crossing, swinging.

Ten paces from where she'd sat for twenty-four days, the thing's eyes came back.

---

*Now,* said Orrun.

He was at the stakes.

She didn't see him go there. She felt him. On the western line, the left arm of the knights' reaching, where the chain came off the foreleg in its loops and dropped over the iron eyes of the stakes. He set his shoulder to the first stake, and she felt the earth go through him.

It came out of the grey ground like a carrot out of sand.

The loop of chain on it went slack, and slid off the eye, and fell in the grey grass with a sound like a dropped anchor.

He went to the next. And the next.

And on the other line, the right arm, at the same moment, there was a lion.

She saw it. Through the smoke and the last two threads of white light. A second Orrun. Grey, enormous, the same size, the same mane, the same pale eyes, with its shoulder to a stake on the right-hand line, and the earth going through it, and the stake coming out. And the next.

She'd seen it on the knowe. Once. A lion of earth and stone rising out of the hill and fighting beside him for a few minutes, and sinking back, and going out of him like blood. She'd seen it at the Long Barn, and on the roof over the old dragon. Each time she'd felt it cost.

She felt it now.

She was stretched. That was the only word. She stood at the foot of the north-west stone with the sword in her hands and felt herself pulled out thin between two bodies, a hundred paces apart, both of them him, both of them pulling stakes out of dead ground, and her half of the one life stretched across both, like a skin stretched over two drums. She was cold. Not the beck. Deeper than that. Cold in the middle of her bones, as if something were drinking the warm out of her from the inside.

Something ran down her lip.

She put her hand up. It came away black in the white light. Blood. Running from her nose, warm, the only warm thing in her, down her lip and her chin and into her collar.

She didn't stop it. She stood and bled and held on, to both of him, while the stakes came out.

---

It was two minutes. Idony told her afterwards. He'd counted.

It felt like the whole night.

Then the second lion was gone. She felt it go, out of him, back into him, like water going down a drain. And all its cold came with it, and went into her, and her knees went and she sat down in the grey grass at the foot of the stone with the sword across her lap and the blood running into her collar.

On both lines, the stakes were out. Half of them. Enough.

The loops of chain lay slack in the grass round the great forepaws.

---

The thing on the stones stood up.

She'd never seen it stand. On the morning of the flight it had come up out of the river already rising, a shape against fire. Here it stood up out of the ring as a hill would stand up, if a hill could. The forelegs first. The ground heaved under her like the deck of a boat. The loops of chain slid off its wrists and fell. The stakes still in the grey ground came out of it on their own, all down both lines, and went flying. The six wains at the end of the stakes went over, one after another, with their loads of chain, and the sound of that was like a landslide.

It stood. Higher than the stones. Higher than the trees. With its mane like a ridge of broken rock against the stars, and its pale eyes open now, wide, and nobody behind them that she could see, and something else instead.

It put its head down.

Between its forepaws. Into the middle of the ring, where it had lain for ninety-eight days. It put its great head down, slowly, to the grey ground, and she felt, through Orrun, the stone under the ground answer it.

The ground opened.

Not cracked. Opened, like a mouth. In the middle of the ring, between its paws, the grey earth fell inward, and kept falling, a great round sink going down into the dark, wider and wider, with a sound under it of stone grinding on stone and something very old breaking. The stones nearest it leaned. Leaned in, toward the opening, slowly, like trees in a flood before they go.

The thing put its head down into the dark.

It was there a long time. Then it lifted its head.

In its jaws, small against it, something grey. Round. A stone. She thought it was a stone. Grey, and round, and smooth, the size of a cart-wheel's hub, maybe, though against that mouth it looked like a pebble.

It held it. It lifted its head up to the stars, with the thing in its jaws.

Then it spread its wings.

---

She'd seen them on the morning of the flight, going up. She hadn't seen them open from the ground.

They went out, and out, and out. Wider than the ring. Wider than the clearing. Stone and leather, grey, with the stars showing through the thin places at the edges like light through a torn sail. They covered the sky. They went over her head and over the ring and over the Wardens' rise and the knights' camp and the grey trees on every side, and the whole clearing was under them, in their shadow, in the dark.

It went up.

The downdraft hit like a wave. It threw her flat on her back in the grey grass. It threw the Wardens off their stones, the ones still on them. It flattened the grey trees round the clearing outward in a ring, every one of them going down in the same direction, as the Fellwood had gone down in its long swath, crowns pointing out. It blew the smoke away. All of it, in one breath, the whole sweet heavy bank of it gone east over the trees like a sheet off a line.

She lay on her back and watched it go over.

Low. So low she could have hit it with a stone. West, over the Thornwood, under the midsummer stars, with the thing in its jaws, and its great wings beating slow, once, and again, and the sound of them going away like thunder going away.

West.

Then it was gone, and the stars came back.

---

Behind it, the ring fell in.

She heard it before she could see. A grinding. A long, deep, sliding roar. She got up on her elbows. In the dark, in the middle of the clearing where the thing had stood, the sink it had opened was going on falling. The stones that had leaned were going down into it, one after another, slowly, tipping in, sliding, vanishing. The ground round them going with them. The edge of the sink coming outward across the grey grass toward her.

The men on the stones went with them. The Wardens. She saw one go, a dark shape on a tipping stone against the starlight, and then not.

On the north stone, the tallest, a big man stood up as it began to lean, and looked down, and jumped.

He hit the grey ground outside the falling edge, and rolled, and lay still, and then got up on his hands and knees.

Dane.

The north stone went down into the sink behind him.

---

She found Rowan at the lip.

She'd got up. She didn't remember getting up. She was walking along the edge of the sink in the dark, with the sword in one hand and the other over her nose, and the ground still sliding in pieces under her feet, and Orrun beside her.

He was one again. She could feel it. Heavy, cold, shaking. But one.

The chain-teams had been under the forelegs when it stood. They'd been at the lip. She could see what was left of them: the slack loops of chain lying in the grey grass, half of them gone over the edge with the stakes still in them, dragging. Men lying in the grass. Some of them moving.

Rowan was at the very edge. On her back, with a loop of the great chain across her legs and a stake on top of the loop, half over the drop. Her helm was gone. Her face was white in the starlight, and set, and she wasn't making a sound.

Maren knelt.

"Leg," said Rowan. Quite steady. "Left. Below the knee. It's broken." She looked at the chain across her. "I can't move that."

Maren put her hands on the chain. One link of it was as long as her body and as thick as her arm. She couldn't move it either.

Orrun put his head down beside her.

He set his forehead to the loop of chain, where it lay over the stake, and pushed. Slowly. She felt it go through him. Not the earth now; there was hardly any left in him. Only his weight, and his will. The loop of chain slid. Off Rowan's legs, off the stake, a hand's breadth, two hands, and then the whole loop went over the edge into the sink with the stake still in its eye and was gone into the dark with a sound like a bell falling down a well.

Rowan lay in the grey grass and looked up at the lion.

He looked down at her.

She didn't say anything. Neither did he. Maren didn't know what passed between them, the knight-captain of Greyhold and the beast that had kept the door for three hundred years. Something. Rowan closed her eyes.

Maren got her by the shoulders and dragged her back from the edge. Somewhere further along the lip a hound was howling, Wynn's dark one, Ash, standing over something pale that lay still in the grey grass.

---

Idony Vael was in the sink.

She heard him before she saw him. A voice, from below the lip, from the dark, very polite. "Excuse me. I'm sorry. Is anyone there?"

She went to the edge and looked down.

The fifth stone had gone in slowly. It lay on its side on the slope of the sink, three body-lengths down, half buried in the sliding rubble. And clinging to it, half buried too, with his arms round the stone and his legs in the loose grey earth, and the strap of a wooden crate still over his shoulder, was the tide-reader.

"Hold on," said Maren.

"I am holding on," said Idony. "It's the only thing I'm doing."

She looked round. A loop of the great chain lay at the lip near her, slack, one end still on a stake that hadn't gone in. She took hold of it and went over the edge with it, down, hand over hand on the links as long as her body, with the rubble going away under her boots into the dark. She got to the stone. She got her arm round his chest under his arms.

"Let go of the stone."

"I'd rather not."

"Let go of the stone, Reader Vael, or I'll leave you here."

He let go.

Orrun pulled the chain from the top. She felt him do it, slow, shaking, a link at a time, the two of them and the crate coming up the sliding slope out of the dark. At the lip he got his teeth in the shoulder of her coat and hauled, and they came over the edge and lay in the grey grass, and behind them the fifth stone slid the rest of the way down into the sink and was gone.

---

Idony sat up first.

He took the crate off his shoulder and opened it, in the grey dust, in the starlight. Most of the glasses in it were broken. She heard them clink as he moved them, the sound of broken glass in straw. He took them out one by one, and looked at each, and put it down.

"Broken," he said. "Broken. Broken." His voice was quite steady. "That one's broken. That one."

He took out a green one. A small one, bottle-green, with a thick base. He held it up.

It wasn't broken.

He held it up to the stars, very still, with the sink behind him and the dead and the dying in the grey grass, and the knights' camp burning where a lamp had gone over into a tent, and Rowan lying where Maren had dragged her with her eyes shut and her jaw set, and Dane on his hands and knees in the dark somewhere shouting names. He held the green glass up and looked into it as if none of that were there at all.

"There," he said.

Maren looked at him.

"South-west. A long way. On the high moor." He turned the glass a little in his fingers. "It's been there since the spring. Every night. A stir I couldn't account for. Three together, close, as if three things were drawing on the Deep in the same place at once, and always together." He was frowning. "Three, together. The way they've been all spring."

"What is it?"

"I don't know." He didn't take his eyes off the glass. "It's going ragged." He was quiet. "Look. It's coming apart. One of the three. It's pulling away from the others. As if it were coming loose."

She didn't know what he meant. She didn't know what was on the high moor, south-west, three together.

Then she did.

She sat in the grey dust at the lip of the sink with the blood drying on her chin and the cold in her bones, and thought of a farmer on a ledge with his hand on an old dragon's jaw, and a red dragon, and a green one who'd landed ten paces behind Orrun and said she wanted to be home before something.

*Like before weather.*

Orrun lay down beside her in the grey grass. All at once, as if his legs had gone. He put his head down on his paws and shut his eyes, and she felt him go: not into the dark, not into the well. Into sleep. Deep, and sudden, and enormous.

She put her hand on his neck and kept it there, and watched the tide-reader watch his glass, and the west where the thing had gone, and the south-west where something was coming loose, until the sky began to go grey and Hesper came in across the flattened trees from the edge with Hush at her heel.
