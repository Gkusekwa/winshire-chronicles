---

The knot pulled in her pocket on the morning after Pask.

She felt it before she knew what it was. A tug, at her side, inside her coat, like a small animal turning over. She put her hand in and her fingers closed on it: grey cord, with a grey hair laid into it, tied fifteen days ago at the edge of the ring and kept since because she didn't know what else to do with it.

She took it out.

It pulled. On its own. Without the lion's chin on her wrist, without anything. It lay across her palm and drew tight, the cord straightening as if somebody had hold of the far end and was walking away with it. Toward the sink.

"Hesper."

The Keeper was sitting by the lip with her bruised fingers in her lap. She looked at the knot.

"The ground's alive again," she said. "So it'll pull on its own now." She looked at her own fingers, at the red rings on them. "Too hard. Hold it loose. Don't let it wind."

Maren held it loose. In her open palm, as Hesper had shown her, not closing her fingers.

It pulled down. Into the sink. And along, toward one place: the north-east side of the great slope of rubble, a third of the way down, where nothing looked any different from anywhere else.

---

They went down to it in the middle of the morning.

The four of them, and the duskcat, who stopped at the lip and lay down there and wouldn't come further. The sink was forty paces across and steep, a great bowl of broken stone and grey earth going down to a heap in the middle, with the fallen ring-stones lying in it every which way like dropped pegs. It shifted under their feet. Every step sent a trickle of stones running down ahead of them.

The knot pulled. She followed it.

Halfway down the north-east slope it stopped pulling forward and pulled straight down, at her feet, into a patch of rubble no different from the rest. She knelt. She put her free hand on the stones.

Under them, something squared.

She cleared it with her fingers. A stone edge. Straight, and smooth, and dressed so close that when she ran her fingertip along it she couldn't feel where one block met the next. Old stone. The kind under Greyhold, the kind in the gallery under the river. The kind that had opened for him.

"A lintel," said Idony, kneeling beside her. He'd come down after her with his crate on its strap, slipping, apologising to the rubble. "That's a lintel. Over a door." He looked at it with his face very close. "Old-realm. Look at the joins. Look at it. You can't get a hair in there."

---

Orrun laid his forehead on the rubble.

He was thin. He'd slept a day and a night after the stakes, as if he'd been killed, and woken grey at the roots of his mane. She'd never seen the grey there before. Iron-grey at the tips, as it had always been, and then, close to the skin, a band of something paler, like ash. He was hungry and he hadn't eaten. He came down the slope of the sink slowly and stood over the place where the knot pulled, and looked at it, and put his head down and laid his forehead on the stones.

They moved.

All at once, and too fast. The loose stones over the lintel slid aside, not tumbling, sliding, like water sliding off a roof, a great sheet of rubble going away down the slope with a rushing sound. And kept going. The slope round them began to go too, the stones under their feet sliding, and Idony said something sharp and grabbed at her arm.

*Stop,* said Orrun. Not to her. To the stone.

It stopped.

She felt him do it, through the bond. He was holding. Like a man who's leaned on a door expecting it to be stuck, and it's flown open, and he has to catch it before it hits the wall. In the surge the stone answered him too fast. Too much. It wanted to move. He held it.

When it was still, there was a doorway in front of them.

Square, dark, set into the slope of the sink as if the sink had been dug to find it. The lintel over it. And beyond it, going down into the dark, steps. Cut in the same close-jointed stone. Going down.

*Here,* said Orrun.

The knot in her hand lay quiet.

---

She went first, with the knot. Then Orrun, who had to bow his head under the lintel and then didn't, because the stair widened as it went. Then Hesper with a torch of pine-pitch she'd lit with a word that took so hard the torch flared up to her wrist and she had to hold it at arm's length. Then Idony, with his crate.

Forty steps. She counted them. The air coming up from below was warm, and dry, and smelled of nothing. Not damp. Not earth. Not old. Nothing at all, as the air in a new-blown glass would smell, if you could smell it.

At the bottom, the stair came out into a room, and Hesper's torch went out.

It didn't blow out or gutter. It went out as if it had decided to, all at once, and Hesper said a word in her own tongue and stood still in the dark.

It wasn't dark.

---

The walls were glowing.

Faint. Grey. A light that came from nowhere, that was in the stone, as if the stone had soaked up something and was giving it back, like a hearthstone giving back heat. Enough to see by. More than enough, once her eyes had got used to it.

It was round. A great round chamber, as big as the yard at Greyhold, under a dome. She could see the dome's curve going up into the grey glow on every side. And in the middle, where the top of the dome should have been, it wasn't. It was broken open. A great ragged hole, and through it, a long way up, daylight, a round of white sky, and down from it, in a heap that filled the middle of the floor, rubble. Broken dome-stone and grey earth and the roots of grey trees, lying in a mound in the middle of the chamber, with a beam of daylight coming down onto it like water out of a jug.

That was where its head had come through.

She stood at the foot of the stair and looked at the hole in the dome and thought of the thing standing up out of the ring on midsummer night and putting its head down between its paws, and the ground opening like a mouth.

It had come in here. Through the roof. And reached down.

---

The plinth was under the rubble, at the heart of the room.

Idony found it. He went straight to the heap in the middle as if he'd known it would be there, and began moving stones off it with his hands, one at a time, carefully, as he moved his glasses. Hesper helped him. Maren stood and watched, because she couldn't make her feet go.

Under the stones, a drum. Squat, round, grey, as high as her waist, cut out of one piece of the same old stone as the walls. In its top, a hollow.

Smooth. Rounded. Egg-shaped, the long way down. The size of a cart-wheel's hub.

Empty.

Idony put his hand into it. Then he took it out quickly, and looked at his palm, and put it back.

"Warm," he said.

Maren put her hand in beside his.

It was warm. Warmer than the floor, warmer than the glowing walls. Warm as a bed is warm when someone's just got up out of it. Smooth as the inside of a cup.

She thought of the thing lifting its head out of the ring with something in its jaws. Small, against it. Grey. Round.

"It was here," she said.

"Yes," said Idony. "Whatever it took. It was here." He moved his hand round the hollow's smooth edge. "A long time. Look how smooth. Look how it fits." He was quiet. "It was made to hold it. The whole room was made to hold it."

---

Then she looked at the walls, and forgot about the plinth.

She'd seen them already. She'd been seeing them since the torch went out, at the edges of her eyes. She'd thought they were cracks in the stone, or the way the glow lay on it, or lichen. She turned round, slowly, with her hand still in the warm hollow, and looked at them properly.

They were beasts.

Carved. All round the curve of the wall, at the height of a man's head and above, going up into the dome. Low, shallow, the stone hardly raised at all, so fine that she couldn't see the marks of any tool. As if they'd been grown out of the wall, or pressed into it from inside. Great shapes, each one longer than the stair was high, lying round the room in a ring.

Six of them.

Lying down. Each one with its head on its forepaws, or laid along its coils, or resting on its own great side. With their eyes shut.

Asleep.

---

She walked round them. Slowly. Left to right from the stair, with the grey glow on them. Orrun came behind her. She felt him look, through the bond. Slow, enormous, still.

The first was a stag.

A great stag, lying down, with its legs folded under it and its head on the ground, and its antlers going up the wall above it, branching and branching, like a winter wood. And round it, carved in the same shallow stone, trees. A forest. Lying down with it. The trees' trunks leaning in toward the stag, all of them, their branches folded over it, as if the forest had lain down to sleep when the stag did.

The second was a serpent. Coiled. Ring on ring on ring of it, filling its piece of wall, with something carved on the coils that might have been scales and might have been frost. Its head was in the middle of its rings. Its eyes were shut.

The third she didn't understand at first. Long and low and heavy, with a body like a boar's but plated, plate over plate, and its snout down. Not on the ground. In it. Its snout buried in the stone of the floor-line, as if it had gone to sleep in the middle of digging.

The fourth was a horned beast. Something like a horse, or a deer, but bigger than either, bigger than any of them, with one horn going up from its brow, long and straight and twisted. Round its head the stone was cut in lines, going out, like the rays of the sun in a child's drawing.

The fifth was so big she had to step back to see it. A hump. A long low hump, like an island, filling more of the wall than any of the others, with things carved on its back: weed, she thought, or coral, or rock. A fin. A great dark eye, shut.

The sixth was a lion.

---

Grey-carved. Lying down, with its head on its forepaws. Its mane going up the wall behind its head like a ridge of broken rock. Plates down its back like the courses of a wall. And along its sides, folded close, wings.

Its eyes were shut.

Orrun stopped in front of it.

She stopped beside him. She didn't say anything. She felt him through the bond, very still, the stillness of the ground under snow.

He stood there a long time.

*That is my kin,* he said.

He'd said it otherwise at the Barrows. *I don't know them.* That had been the cold, empty not-knowing that went through him walking down the rows of grey stones. This was the other thing. The thing she'd felt in him on the island, in the autumn, before the bond, when he dug at the north end night after night with his claws, toward something under the earth that he could feel and she couldn't.

He knew this.

*That is how it was,* said Orrun. *Asleep. Like that. With its head on its paws.* He was quiet. *That is what I kept. That is what I was keeping. At the door.*

She looked at the carved lion on the wall, asleep, with its wings folded.

*It was not chained then,* said Orrun. *It was not a thing that bled. It was asleep. And I kept the door, so nobody would wake it.* A long pause. *And they came, with fire. And they woke it. And then they chained it.*

He lifted his head and looked at the carved face, the shut eyes.

*It was asleep,* he said again. As if he were trying to remember how to say it.

---

There were tall shapes over the sleepers.

She'd taken them for part of the wall. Pillars, or the edges between one carving and the next. They weren't. Over each of the six sleeping beasts, standing behind it, there was a figure. Tall, much taller than the beasts, going right up the wall into the curve of the dome. Slender. Standing. Without faces: their heads were smooth ovals of stone, with nothing on them at all.

And they were cut differently.

The beasts were cut into the stone, shallow, pressed in. The tall shapes were cut so that the stone round them was lower, and they stood out a little from the wall, and the grey glow lay on them more brightly than on anything else. As if they were made of the glow itself. As if they were light, standing in the stone, and the carver had only cut away the dark round them so you could see.

Their hands were held out. Over the sleepers. Palms down, open, over each sleeping head.

Like a hand held over a child's head in the dark, to see if it was still breathing. Or to keep it asleep.

---

Idony found the script.

He'd been kneeling at the foot of the wall under the stag, with his face so close to the stone that his breath fogged it. Under the stag's folded legs, at the bottom of its carving, there was a line of marks. Small. Hooked. Cut deep. She'd seen marks like them on the lamps the Wardens had brought, and on the lock under Greyhold, and on the arch at the Old Ruin, and she couldn't read any of them.

Idony could.

He put his finger in the cuts and traced them, slowly, one after another, as a child reads, with a finger under the line. His lips moved. He sat back on his heels and looked at it, and then got up and went to the serpent and knelt under it and found the line there, and traced it.

Then the plated one. The horned one. The great hump. The lion.

He came back to the middle of the room and stood by the plinth with his hand on its edge and looked round at all six.

"It's the same," he said. "The same word, under every one. Six times." He sounded very calm, as he'd sounded at the lip of the sink, counting broken glasses. "It's not a name. I thought they'd be names. It's a word. The same word."

"What word?"

"*Sleep,*" said Idony Vael.

---

There was more. Over the plinth.

Round the rim of the broken dome, on the one side where the rim was still there, where the Titan's head hadn't broken it away: a longer line. The hooked marks going round the curve of the stone, high up, in the grey glow, above the hole. Idony stood under it with his head tipped back for a long time.

He couldn't reach it. He didn't need to. He read it standing, with his lips moving, and then he read it again, aloud, slowly, word by word, the way Maren had heard Grieve read the names of the dead on the hill outside Greyhold.

"*Here,*" he said. "*The breath.* Then a word that's *held*, or *kept*. Kept, I think. The way you'd keep a thing safe, or keep a fire in." He read it again. "*Here the breath is kept.*"

The room was very quiet.

Maren stood by the plinth with her hand on its warm edge and looked up at the hooked marks going round the broken rim of the dome in the grey glow.

*Here the breath is kept.*

She thought of the cavern under Greyhold. Half full of still black water, with six iron rings in the walls and the stumps of chain hanging from them, and the lock on the far side with its sentence still burning, white, in the old marks she couldn't read, though everything else had gone dark. She'd copied it in charcoal into the back of the black book. She'd asked Orrun what it said.

He couldn't read it. Nobody could. But he'd looked at it, burning in the dark, and said: *Something is shut there. Like a breath held.*

She didn't say it. She stood with her hand on the warm stone where something had been kept for a very long time, and something was taken, and looked up at the line round the broken dome, and held it in her head. *Like a breath held. Here the breath is kept.*

She thought she'd say it later. When she knew what it meant.

---

Over the plinth, in the middle of the dome, there had been something else.

She could see where. The carvings of the tall shapes went up the walls into the curve of the dome, and their heads leaned in, all six, toward the middle, toward the place over the plinth. And their arms. Over each sleeper, one hand held out, palm down. And the other hand, the one she hadn't noticed, going up. Into the dome. Toward the top. Toward something they'd all been reaching to, or holding up, together, in the middle.

It was gone. That was where the hole was. It had fallen with the roof, when the Titan's head came through, and lay somewhere in the heap of rubble under the beam of daylight, broken.

Idony went through the rubble for an hour looking for it. He found a piece. A fragment of dome-stone the size of a dinner-plate, with a hand carved on it. One hand, open, palm up, in the grey glow, with the long fingers curved a little, as if it had been holding something round.

Nothing else. Whatever it had been holding was gone.

He sat on the rubble with the piece of stone on his knees and looked at the hand.

"I'd have given a year of my life," he said, "to see what was in it."

---

Hesper was standing at the wall under the stag.

She'd been there since the torch went out. Maren hadn't seen her move. The Keeper stood with her bare feet on the warm floor and her hand flat on the carved stone of the stag's folded foreleg, as she put her hand on the ring-stones. Under the great antlers like a winter wood. With the carved forest lying down round the stag, and round her.

She was singing.

Under her breath. So low that Maren only heard it when she came close. A few words, in the clans' tongue, over and over, a little tune, rising and falling, as you'd sing to something you were trying to rock.

"What is it?" said Maren.

Hesper stopped.

She didn't take her hand off the stone. She looked up at the stag's shut eyes for a long time.

"It's what we say to babies," she said. "When they won't sleep. Every mother in the Wyldmarch says it. My mother said it to me. I've said it to other women's children when they were teething." She said the words again, softly, in her own tongue. Then in Maren's. "*Hush, now. Hush. Like the six who sleep.*"

She took her hand away from the stone and looked at it.

"I never knew it was about anything," she said. "It's what you say. *Like the six who sleep.* You don't ask what six. You don't ask who they are." She looked round the room, at all six of them, lying round the walls in their ring with their eyes shut and the tall shapes of light holding their hands out over them. "Nobody ever asked."

"The six who sleep," said Idony, from the rubble. Very quietly.

Hesper looked at the stag again. At the antlers. At the carved trees lying down round it, with their branches folded over.

"That one's ours," she said. "That one's the forest."

Maren looked at the stag. Then at the empty hollow in the plinth. Then at Idony, sitting on the rubble with the carved hand on his knees, who'd told her that morning, at the lip of the sink, with his four glasses climbing on their blanket, that something was rising under the Thornwood toward the lake, and that it would come within seven days.

Nobody said anything.

---

They stayed below most of the next day.

Idony copied everything. He'd brought oiled silk in his crate, a roll of it, and a stick of charcoal and a pen and a little horn of ink, and he sat at the foot of each carving in turn and drew it, and copied the line of marks under it, stroke by stroke, and the long line round the rim of the dome, standing on the rubble heap on tiptoe with Orrun beside him to steady him. He drew the carved hand on its fragment. He measured the plinth and the hollow with his knotted cord and wrote the numbers down.

Maren sat by the lion.

The carved one. She sat on the warm floor at the foot of the wall with her back against the stone under its folded paws, and Orrun lay down beside her with his chin on his paws, the same as the carving, and they sat there together under it, a long time, without speaking.

The grey glow was fading. By the evening of the second day she could see it going. The walls dimmer. The tall shapes of light harder to tell from the stone round them. The surge flattening, Idony said, slowly, as a flood goes down. When it was gone, the room would be dark again, as it had been dark for a thousand years, with nobody to see it.

Her fingers were numb.

This time it lasted only the evening, not two days: a coldness in the tips, the right hand, where she'd held the knot loose all the way down the slope. It had pulled on its own, Hesper said. It had taken from the ground, which was full. Only a little from her.

She flexed them, and looked at the carved lion, and didn't mind.

---

They came up at dusk on the hundred-and-first day.

Up the forty steps, out under the lintel, into the bottom of the sink. The rubble slope going up green now, with moss on every stone, and the round of sky at the top gone gold and grey, and Hush at the lip, standing up, with her lamp eyes on them. The birds were singing in the pines round the clearing. Maren hadn't heard birds in the Thornwood for a month.

Idony came last, with the crate on his shoulder and the roll of oiled silk under his arm.

At the top of the steps he stopped.

He put the roll down on a stone. He opened the crate and took out the green glass, the one that had come through the sink whole, and held it up to the evening sky, and turned it, slowly, toward the south-west. And frowned at it.

"Still there," he said.

Maren looked at him.

"South-west. A long way. On the moor." He turned the glass a fraction. "Three. Together." He kept frowning. "Thin at one edge. The one that was coming loose on midsummer night. It's thin. But it's there." He lowered the glass and looked at it in his hand. "They're still together."

He didn't know what it was. She could see he didn't. He stood at the top of the forty steps in the bottom of the sink with the green glass in his hand, frowning at three far-off draws on a moor he'd never seen, as if they were a sum that wouldn't come out.

She knew.

She didn't tell him. Not yet. She stood beside him in the dusk with her numb fingers and the lion and thought of a farmer on a ledge with a green dragon's rope in his two hands, holding on, and held it in her head with the other things, the breath and the six and the hand holding something round. For later. When she knew what any of it meant.

Then they climbed out of the sink, and went to sleep, and in the morning Hesper said: we go to the Moot.
