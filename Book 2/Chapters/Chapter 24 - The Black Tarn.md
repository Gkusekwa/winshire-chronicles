The binder they'd taken alive had twelve coins in his purse, and Calder knew them before Ulric had them out on the trough.

It was the morning after the smoke. The ninety-ninth day. Ulric had kept the man all night in the byre under the hall with two of his own men on the door, and in the grey light he brought him out into the yard and stood him against the trough, and the whole of Varrhold came to look. Red thread and grey. Nobody spoke.

He was small, the binder. Thin, about forty, with a clerk's face and a clerk's hands and burn-scars on the backs of them from the pots. Wendmouth boots. He stood against the trough with his wrists tied and looked at the ground.

Ulric asked him who'd hired him. He said Wendmouth. Ulric asked who in Wendmouth. He said a man. Ulric asked what man. He said a man who paid in advance and didn't give his name, and that was all he said, and he kept on saying it in a flat dull voice, looking at the ground, until Ulric stopped asking.

Then Ulric cut the purse off his belt and tipped it out on the trough's edge.

Twelve coins. Silver. New, the burr still on their edges, so fresh from the die they caught the morning light like water.

Calder picked one up.

A ship on one face. A wave on the other. Saltreach coin, ordinary, the kind the grain factors paid in when they paid at all. He turned it on its edge.

Round the rim, struck small, so small you'd miss it if you didn't know to look, six dots in a ring.

He'd held one before. In the autumn, at his own gate, with a soft-spoken stranger in a good dark coat holding it out to him and smiling. He hadn't taken it. He'd turned it over in his fingers and seen the six on the rim and given it back.

"That's the coin man's silver," he said.

The yard looked at him.

"The autumn," he said. "At my gate. Before the mill. A man came, a soft man in a good coat, and offered me this for my barley and my road. Same coin. New. With the burr on it." He held it up so the light caught the rim. "The six on the edge. Struck in. That's not scratched on after. That's in the die."

Ulric took it from him and looked at it, turning it so the dots caught the light.

"In the die," he said.

"Somebody's minting it," said Calder. "Somewhere. With the ring in the rim."

Ulric looked at the coin a while longer. Then he put it in his own purse, and the other eleven, and didn't say anything.

---

Arn read the book that night.

Calder hadn't given it to him. He hadn't asked. The book had been in Calder's coat, in its oiled cloth, since the gate, since Ulric gave it back on the road down the glen. On the night of the ninety-ninth day, after the binder, Gorm Askell came to the setters' ledge and stood at the end of it, limping, sour, and said: "He wants the book."

Calder looked at him.

"I'm to fetch it," said Gorm. "He said: fetch the farmer's book. He didn't say to fetch the farmer." He sniffed. "I'm saying it in case you were going to ask."

Calder gave it to him.

---

At midnight he went down to the hall anyway.

He couldn't sleep. Lisska wouldn't leave him; she hadn't left him since the smoke, not further than a ledge away, not for a moment, as if the rope were still loose and she had to keep her end of it in sight. She came down the long stair behind him in the dark and lay down outside the hall door and put her chin on the step.

He went in.

The hall was dark except for the east fire. And by the east fire, at the high table, alone, Arn.

He was sitting in his own place. To the right of the chair. The chair stood beside him in the firelight under its grey cloth, as it had stood since Teo burned. Propped against one of its legs, the board. The plank from the burned fold at the Brack steading, with the boot-print in clay on it, dried, the brass eyelets pressed in it like a row of seeds.

And on the table in front of Arn, open, the book.

He was reading it with his finger.

Calder stopped inside the door. Arn didn't look up. He was bent over the book in the light of the east fire with his finger under the line, tracing, like Ada, like Wren when she was learning. Slowly. His lips moving. He was slow at it. Calder could see that from the door. Haldor's son, who'd been at the dues-school and the hold-school and had a steward to read him his levies, and he read like a boy, slow, with his finger, the way you read when you've never had to.

Calder didn't go any closer.

He sat down on the end of a bench by the door, in the dark, and waited.

---

Arn read till dawn.

Calder watched him turn the pages. Carefully, so the dried paper wouldn't tear. He watched his finger go under the lines. Under the bags from the coast with the ring on every bag. Under Upper Rush. Under the coin man and the miller and the farmer's road and hour, two silver. Under the drover's wage.

He watched him come to the horse.

He knew when Arn got there. He didn't need to see the page. Arn's finger stopped, and stayed, and didn't move. The fire cracked. Outside the door, Lisska shifted on the step.

*A horse, from the uncle's lines; to the coast for my mother. Returned.*

Arn sat with his finger on it for so long that the fire burned down and Calder thought about getting up to put a log on it and didn't.

Then Arn turned the page.

He read the second ford. The gorse, sixty, Delling's. One bolt over the count, for the old one, five. He read it and his face didn't change at all. He turned to the back. To the list stuck inside the cover, the five lines in the older ink. He read the fifth line twice.

Then he closed the book.

He put both hands flat on the cover, as Ulric had at Skarrow, and Ada in the cave. He sat there in the grey light coming in at the smoke-hole, beside the empty chair, with his hands on the book, and the board with the boot-print against the chair's leg.

He didn't look at Calder. He got up, and went out past him, and past the green dragon on the step, into the morning.

He didn't say a word.

---

They carried the dead up to Coldtarn on the hundredth day.

Tor and Skell together, on one bier. A great sledge of ash-poles lashed with rawhide, the boy on it and the young blue under him with the boy's head on his neck, under a grey cloak with no thread. Too heavy for men. Four of the ledges' dragons drew it, on ropes, walking, up the long brown slope above the hold that Calder had walked behind Haldor's bier in the autumn: Harl, and Brede's blue, and two more. Slowly. The ropes over their shoulders, their heads down.

Behind them, on smaller biers, carried by men: three of Arn's who'd died on the stair. Two of Ulric's.

One pyre.

Arn had said it, in the yard, in the morning, before anyone had asked. He'd come out of the hall with the book under his arm, and given it back to Calder without looking at him, and then turned to the yard and said, loud, so that the red thread and the grey could both hear him: "One pyre. On the shore. All of them."

Nobody argued.

---

They burned them on the hundred-and-first night.

On the shore of Coldtarn, under the black cliffs, on the burned stones where Haldor had burned and Teo after him. The pyre was as high as a house. Ada lit it. She hadn't been asked; she'd come up from the caves with Wren and Bel and the children and Old Grey, and when the ledges came down to the shore with their torches she'd walked out of the crowd with her hazel stick and gone to the foot of the pyre and put two fingers to the kindling and turned her hand three times and said the word.

It caught before her hand had finished turning.

The ledges looked at her. Ivar Leck. Hulda Sarr. Gunna, on a litter, bandaged, with half her face under linen and one eye showing, and Tamsin lying beside the litter with her wing bound up in splints. They looked at the old woman from Harrow's End with her hazel stick, standing in the firelight at the foot of the pyre she'd lit with a hearth-word.

Ada didn't look back at them. She went back to the crowd, to Wren, and stood there.

The pyre went up.

---

The whole Clan was on the slopes of the bowl.

Calder had stood here in the autumn and thought the Roosts were a lot of dragons. He stood here now with the ledges at the water's edge and looked up.

Every dragon on the Roosts was on the cliffs. All of them. Bonded and unbonded, the young ones and the old, the thirty-six who'd stood opened on the Choosing Ledge in the smoke, the ones who'd come back from the high glens. Sitting on every ledge and outcrop of the black cliffs round Coldtarn, in the firelight, with their wings folded and their heads down. Hundreds. Watching the pyre.

And at the end of the night, when the fire had burned down and the grey light was coming up behind the fells, they cried.

All of them. At once. The long high many-voiced cry that came down off the cliffs and filled the bowl and went out over the black water, rising and falling, and going on, and at the end it went down. Down, and down, as it had at Haldor's burning, and at Teo's, and on the hill above the second ford when Morrow lay down beside Hallam. Down, for the dead.

Not up. Not the way it had gone up on the evening the old one came home.

Down.

Beside him on the shore, Aurveth had lifted her head. She'd walked up from the setters' ledge. All the way, on four legs, slowly, with the wing in its splints held out from her side and Kharrak walking beside her not touching, the whole long brown slope from the hold to the tarn. It had taken her half a day. She lay now on the shore with her one gold eye on the dying fire, and when the cry came down off the cliffs she didn't join it. She listened.

*I have heard that,* she said, *more times than there are stones on this shore.*

He put his hand on her neck.

*It does not get easier,* said Aurveth. *It only gets more familiar.*

---

Lisska said one thing that whole day.

She'd been beside him all night, on the shore, closer than she'd been since she was a hatchling, Ada said. Pressed against his legs. Not talking. In the grey morning, when the cry had gone down and the ledges were raking the pyre, she said it.

*Tor didn't lift the axe.*

"No."

*I saw. From the rock. I was half out on the water, but I saw.* She was quiet. *He had his hand out. To Skell. And Skell didn't know him.*

"I know."

*I'd have lifted it,* said Lisska. *If it had been you. If you didn't know me. I'd have lifted it.*

"I know you would."

She was quiet for a long time.

*I'm glad he didn't,* she said.

---

They poured on the hundred-and-second day, in the morning.

The ash went into two urns. Big grey plain ones, as tall as a child, like Haldor's. The ledges carried them down to the flat black stone that ran out into the tarn like a jetty, and set them down at the end of it, side by side, and went back to the shore.

Calder had stood here in the autumn. A winter and a war ago. He'd stood at the back of the crowd with Ada's mourning cloth over his arm and watched Arn step onto this stone and put his hands on his father's urn. And Ulric's voice coming across the water, slow and deep and calm. *The champion pours.*

And Teo, walking out onto the stone like it was his kitchen. *Pour with him. You're both right. Pour together. One hand each.*

The whole Clan was on the shore. Silent.

Arn walked out onto the stone.

He walked out alone, to the end, to the two urns, and stood there in his black with the black water round him on three sides and the cliffs going up behind with the dragons on them. He put his hand on the first urn.

Then he stopped.

He stood there a long time with his hand on it. Calder watched him. They all did. He stood at the end of the stone with his back to the Clan and his hand on the urn and didn't lift it.

Then he turned round.

He turned and looked back along the stone, to the shore, to where Ulric stood at the front of the grey with his arms folded and his face that gave away nothing, nothing at all.

"Uncle," said Arn.

The shore was so quiet Calder could hear the water on the stone.

"Pour with me," said Arn Varr. "One hand each."

---

Ulric didn't move for a moment.

Then he unfolded his arms, and walked out along the flat black stone, slowly, in his grey, and stopped at the end beside his nephew. He didn't look at him. He looked at the urns.

He put his hand on the first one. Beside Arn's.

They lifted it together. One hand each. Tor Brecc and Skell, and the three of the red thread and the two of the grey, in one urn and then the other. They tipped it over the edge of the stone together, and the ash went into Coldtarn in a long grey pour, like smoke going the wrong way.

It lay on the black water in a pale shape, spreading. Then, in the middle, it began to go down, and the whole shape drew in toward that point, as it had in the autumn, and went down, and the water closed over it, black, and was still.

They poured the second.

When it was done, they stood on the end of the stone side by side and looked at the water, and neither of them moved.

---

It was Arn who turned to the shore first.

"The man at the Fleece," he said. His voice went across the water to the Clan on the shore and up the slopes to the dragons on the cliffs. Not loud. It didn't need to be. "Vale. The one that's alive. And everyone who wears his ring."

He held up his hand. In it, the board. The plank from the Brack steading, with the boot-print in clay. He'd carried it out onto the stone under his arm. Calder hadn't seen.

"He killed my brother at the ford. With a hired knife, on a borrowed horse. He killed Hallam Brecc on the hill. He killed the Sallow girl and Sigrun Holm and Dag Rorke at the mill, with one bolt, bought, out of a hedge, that started it. He burned the Brack steading before midsummer and left our grey on its door so I'd ride on my uncle. He came for our dragons with smoke on midsummer night and killed Hallam's grandson on the Choosing Ledge." He put the board down at his feet on the black stone. "The Clan stands against him. Red thread and grey. All of it. From the black water."

On the shore nobody moved.

Ulric spoke beside him. Slow and deep and calm.

"The war between the red and the grey," he said, "is over. Here. At the black water. Whoever lit it, it's out." He looked at the Clan. "If any man of mine or my nephew's lifts a hand against the other side from this morning, he answers to me. And to him."

He didn't look at Arn. Arn didn't look at him.

But they were standing on the same stone.

---

"And the chair?" somebody called. From the back. An old man, red thread. "Who pours for the chair?"

Arn didn't answer at once.

"Nobody," he said.

The shore murmured.

"The chair stays empty." Arn looked down at the board at his feet. "It stays empty till we've buried my brother right." He looked up. "Till the men who paid for him are named. Every one. Not the knife. Not the horse. The silver. Whoever paid."

He looked at his uncle. For the first time, on the stone, he looked at him.

"Then we'll pour for the chair," said Arn. "Both halves. And not before."

Ulric looked back at him for a long moment. Then he nodded, once.

---

Ulric said the count.

He held up one of the binder's coins between two fingers so the light caught it. New silver. A ship, a wave. The six on the rim.

"He came for our dragons twice," he said. "Twice. At the mill, in the autumn, to break the war-pairs. Sigrun's great bronze, Dag's red, the Sallow girl's green. To kill the three who held them and let them loose, in a war, with nobody to hold them. And then smoke them and bind them." He turned the coin. "They didn't go loose. They went to a barley farmer in his own field." He looked at Calder on the shore. "Nobody paid for that."

A sound went through the crowd. Not a laugh. Nearly.

"And at the choosing," said Ulric. "On midsummer night. With smoke on the wind, for all forty of our young ones on the ledge, opened. He got four." He closed his fingers on the coin. "He's lost all he came for. Every time. All but four."

---

Then they gave Calder his errand.

He hadn't known it was coming. He was standing on the shore with the ledges behind him and the old one lying at his feet and the green dragon pressed against his legs, and Arn turned on the stone and looked straight at him across the water, and said his name.

"Calder Fenn."

He didn't move.

"Come out."

He went out along the stone. It was very long. He'd never been on it. The black water was so still on either side that he could see the cliffs in it, and the dragons on the cliffs, upside down. At the end, he stopped in front of the two Varrs.

"Bring them home," said Arn. "The four. The bronze and the black and the two reds. Wherever he's taken them. Bring them home to the ledges."

"And bring back the names," said Ulric. "Of whoever paid. For Teo. For all of it. The purse behind the knife."

Calder looked at them.

"Why me?" he said.

He didn't mean it the way it sounded. He meant: you've three hundred men at Skarrow and the red thread at Varrhold and the ledges. You've dragons. You've riders. You've the best warrior in the Clan standing on this stone and the chief's son. Why the barley man.

Ulric held up the coin again.

"Because the silver came up from the coast," he said. "And the coin was struck somewhere. And somebody paid for the die, and somebody paid for the silver, and somebody paid for the ship that brought it." He turned it so the six on the rim caught the light. "You've sold barley twenty years to Saltreach factors who cheated you every autumn. You know a factor's coin from a fair one. You know what a purse looks like when it's been to the coast and back." He put the coin in Calder's hand. "None of us does. We've never had to."

"You're the only one of us," said Arn, "who can follow a coin."

---

Calder stood on the end of the black stone with the coin in his hand.

He thought of the factors on Liss's ferry, every autumn, with their scales and their tally-sticks and their soft voices. He thought of the coin man at his gate. Of Delling at his table in the Plough with his spectacles on their cord. Of the book in his coat, in its oiled cloth, every bag of silver and what for and who to. *Up from the coast, three bags, the ring on every bag.*

He thought: the coast. Wendmouth. Gallows Quay. Where they'd sold the forcing.

He thought: I can write my own name, and the D's backwards.

He looked at the two Varrs on the stone.

On the shore behind him, he felt the ledges. He didn't turn round. He didn't need to. He felt them come down to the water's edge, Ivar and Hulda and Brede and the boy Leif, and Gunna on her litter carried by four of them, and stand there behind him, at the root of the stone, as they'd stood behind him at the gate. Not beside him. Behind.

"Yes," said Calder.

---

He met the Wyldmarch trader on the Scarfell road that afternoon.

He was riding back to the hold beside Orm Garrick on his grey hill-pony, with Kharrak overhead and Lisska walking beside his horse, close enough to touch, which she'd never done before. A horse-train came over the rise toward them going east. Eight horses, roped, loaded with salt and iron, and a man walking at the head in a coat of grey fur with red bars painted on his cheeks.

A different trader from the one at Skarrow, going home.

Orm talked with him, in their mix of tongues, with their hands. Calder waited. Then Orm turned in his saddle.

"He says he's going to the lake," said Orm. "The Moot. The clans have all gone there for the last week of midsummer, as they do. Every clan in the Wyldmarch." He listened to the trader again. "He says the great one's gone. Off the stones, in the deep forest, on midsummer night. Flown west. Nobody knows where."

Calder sat very still on his horse.

"And the lion-woman?"

Orm asked.

The trader said something, and then the short word, the two syllables, that the other trader had said at Skarrow's gate.

"She was at the stones with it, he says," said Orm. "Sitting by it." He listened again. "Nobody's heard since. If she's anywhere now, he says, she'll be at the lake with the clans." He shrugged. "That's his guess."

The trader touched his forehead to Calder, and went on east with his horses, over the rise and away.

Calder sat on the road a long time.

He thought of the lion lying at ten paces from the great one, in a ring of stones in the grey, as Lisska had told it. He thought of the smoke coming down the cliffs on midsummer night, and the stone of the Roosts humming toward the east in the morning. *Something has opened.*

He put his hand in his coat.

The book, in its oiled cloth. Rook's knife. The iron ring on its string. The folded bill. The tin charm. A new coin with six dots on the rim.

And the stone. Small, flat, grey, with the white ring running clean round it.

He took it out and held it in his palm. He didn't know what it was for. Nobody did.

He put it back, and rode on to the hold.
