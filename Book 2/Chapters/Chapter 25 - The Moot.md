---

Elder Aun gave the Moot's answer on the shingle, in the morning, with the lake behind her like a sheet of grey glass.

"Nobody will be told to go," she said.

Maren stood at the edge of the crowd with Orrun beside her and Hesper and Idony and Hush, and listened to Hesper say it after in her ear. The whole Moot was on the shore. Thousands. Standing on the shingle and the turf above it, among the upturned boats, in their families, with their beasts, in the grey light, and the old blind woman in front of them with the girl holding her hand.

"The lowlander has a glass," said Aun. "He says it tells him the ground is going to open. He walked two days to tell us." Her white eyes turned toward where Idony stood, and he went pink. "I believe he believes it. I don't know what his glass knows."

The crowd was very quiet.

"So. Whoever believes the glass, take to the water. Today. Now." Aun lifted her hand toward the lake. "Whoever doesn't, stay. Nobody will think less of you either way." She put her hand down. "The elders stay. Elders stay. That's what elders are for."

She turned round, on the girl's arm, and walked back up the shingle.

---

By noon half the clans were on the lake.

Maren watched them go out. The river clans first, because they'd come in boats and knew boats: the round hide boats, light as baskets, going out from the shingle three and four at a time, paddled, with whole families in them, children in the middle, grandmothers in the back. Then rafts. Poles lashed together with rawhide on the shore by the horse clans, who'd never been on water in their lives and didn't like it, and pushed out, wobbling, with ten and fifteen people standing on them holding each other's belts. Then anything that would float. Doors. Tent-poles bundled. A whole roundhouse roof, taken off its walls and turned upside down and pushed out onto the water like a great grass bowl, with forty people sitting in it.

They went out a bowshot from the shore and stopped there, on the still grey lake, in a long raft of boats and people that went along the whole front of the Moot. Waiting.

The other half stayed.

The old, who wouldn't. The sick, who couldn't. The stubborn, who didn't believe a lowlander with a box. The horse clans' riders, who wouldn't leave their elk, and the elk wouldn't go on water. The Keepers, who wouldn't leave their beasts. And the elders, sitting on the shingle in a row with their backs to the lake and their faces to the forest, because that was what elders were for.

---

The Moot lay on a long slope.

Maren walked it in the afternoon with Hesper to see the shape of it. The north shore of the lake went up from the shingle in a broad gentle rise of turf, half a league wide, to the edge of the Thornwood at the top. On the slope, the Moot: roundhouses and tents in long rows, running up from the water to the forest. And between the rows, lanes. Broad, as broad as a drove-road, trodden bare by a month of feet and hooves. Seven of them. All running the same way. North to south. Forest to lake.

"Anything that comes out of the forest," said Hesper, "toward the water, comes down those."

She said it as if she'd seen it before. Maren asked.

"A fire," said Hesper. "When I was a girl. In the dry year. The Thornwood burned in the north and everything in it came south and came down here to the water, down the lanes, through the Moot. Deer, boar, wolves. Bears. All at once. We stood in the lanes." She was quiet. "You stand in a lane so the beasts go down it and not through the houses. That's all you can do. You can't stop them. You make them go where the houses aren't."

---

They set the lanes at dusk.

The clans did it as they'd have done it for a fire. Without being told. Maren watched it happen all along the slope.

The horse clans brought their stonehorns down. Not wild ones: tame ones, the herd they'd brought to the Moot to trade, sixty of the great grey plated beasts, short-sighted, short-tempered, as big as haywains, driven down by riders on elk into the two western lanes and turned to face the forest. They stood shoulder to shoulder across the lanes, three deep, with their great horns forward, like a wall of grey rock with spikes in it.

In the middle lanes, the Keepers. With their beasts. Wolves, sitting in the lanes in rows with their tongues out, watching the trees. And the bears. Maren counted eleven. Brown bears and black, and one huge pale one she'd never seen the like of, standing in the middle of the third lane on its hind legs, taller than a roundhouse, looking north.

On the ridge of every roof along the slope, a duskcat. Black against the evening. Lamp-eyes. The night watch, Hesper said. A duskcat on every ridge, every night of the Moot, for as long as there'd been a Moot.

The children who'd stayed, and the old, went into the big roundhouses at the bottom of the slope by the water. The biggest and strongest, with stone footings.

Two lanes at the east end of the slope had nobody in them.

"Those," said Maren.

Hesper looked at her.

"The near one," said Maren. "And the one past it. We'll take those."

She didn't ask Orrun. She felt him, beside her, through the bond. He'd already chosen.

---

There was a seventh lane.

Past the two Maren had taken, at the very east end of the Moot, where the slope ran up against the forest's edge. Narrow. Half a drove-road's width, with a single row of roundhouses down one side of it and the trees close on the other. The first of the Thornwood would come out there before anywhere.

"That one's mine," said Hesper.

"Hesper..."

"It's the one nobody else wanted." Hesper shrugged. She had her spear over her shoulder and Hush at her heel and the red cords at her belt, and the red rings on her two fingers were going yellow at the edges. "Somebody has to stand in it. I've stood in a lane before. Hush has." She looked down at the duskcat. "She doesn't like it. She'll do it."

Hush looked up at Maren with her lamp-eyes and didn't blink.

"The asking," said Maren. "Your knots. In the surge. They take too hard."

"I know." Hesper looked north, at the dark line of the trees. "I'll ask them anyway. That's what we do. You ask. If they hear you, they go round." She looked at Maren. "If they don't, they don't."

She walked off up the seventh lane in the dusk, barefoot, with the duskcat behind her, toward the trees.

---

Idony didn't sleep.

He sat on a rock at the water's edge, at the bottom of the slope, with his four glasses on a flat stone in front of him and a stub of candle, and watched them climb. Maren went down to him in the middle of the night because she couldn't sleep either.

"Look," he said.

She looked. The quicksilver in the four glass throats was moving. At the sink it had crept a hair's breadth at a time. Now she could see it go. Up, steadily, as you'd see water rise in a ditch when the sluice is opened.

"Tonight," he said. "Or the morning." He didn't look up. "Soon."

"Where?"

He put his finger on the glass furthest north. Its quicksilver was nearly at the top.

"There," he said. "North. In the forest. A league. Perhaps less." He was very calm. "Not here. Not under the Moot. It's under the forest, and it's coming up."

She sat down on the rock beside him and they watched the glasses climb.

At dawn the birds went.

---

All at once. Out of the Thornwood, behind the Moot, from one end of the forest's edge to the other: every bird. She heard it before she saw it, a roar, a great rushing roar like wind in a gale, and looked up and the sky over the forest was black with them. Rooks. Pigeons. Finches. Owls. Hawks. Every bird in the northern Thornwood, in one cloud, going up off the trees together and pouring west over the Moot, over the lake, over the boats, so many that the dawn went dark under them and the sound of their wings was the only sound in the world.

They went over. West. And were gone.

The lake was absolutely still.

Idony picked up the first of the four glasses. The green one. The one that had come through the sink.

He didn't look north. He turned it south-west, as he always did, and held it up to the grey sky, and looked into it for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, to himself.

She didn't ask.

He put the glass into the crate, in its straw, and shut the lid.

A river-clan woman in a hide boat at the water's edge, the last boat still on the shingle, called to him sharply in her own tongue and held out her hand. He looked at it. Then he looked at Maren.

"Go," said Maren.

He picked up the crate and got into the boat, and the woman pushed off, and paddled out onto the still lake toward the long raft of boats a bowshot off, with the tide-reader sitting in the middle of her boat holding his crate on his knees like a child.

Maren went up the slope to her lanes.

---

The ground opened at the full of the morning.

Not here. North. A league into the forest, Idony had said, and it was. First a sound, low, under everything. As if the world had cleared its throat. She felt it in her feet, in the turf of the near lane where she stood beside Orrun, in her teeth.

Then the ground heaved.

The whole slope of the Moot lifted, and dropped, like a blanket shaken. The roundhouses swayed. Tents went down. On the ridge of every roof the duskcats crouched flat. The lake behind her slopped up over the shingle in one long grey wave and slid back, and out on the water the raft of boats lifted and fell, and somebody screamed, and stopped.

And over the Thornwood, to the north, something rose.

---

Antlers.

That was what she saw first. Over the black line of the trees, going up into the morning sky, a great crown of branches. Bare. Grey and brown. Branching and branching, like the bare crown of a winter wood seen from below, going up and up, higher than any tree, higher than the hill behind the Moot, until it stood against the sky like a forest standing on its head.

Then the head under it.

A stag's head. Long, grey-brown, with a great dark eye. The size of a hill. Rising out of the Thornwood a league off, slowly, with the trees falling away from it on every side like water off a rising seal. It lifted its head, and the antlers went up with it, and the morning light came through the branches of them.

Then it stood.

And the forest stood with it.

She'd seen the carving. Under the Ring, on the wall, in the grey glow: the stag lying down with its legs folded under it, and the forest lying down round it, leaning in, the trees' branches folded over. She'd thought the carver had meant it as you'd carve leaves round a door. To make it pretty.

He hadn't.

The trees round the great stag were getting up. All of them, as far as she could see. Pulling up out of the ground, roots and all, the roots coming out of the earth like the legs of something waking, and the trunks leaning, and straightening, and moving. Walking. A forest, walking. Round the stag, with it, as if they'd been lying down with it for a thousand years and now it was getting up and they were getting up too.

The sound came down to the Moot a moment after. A long, deep, tearing groan, of a thousand roots coming out of the ground at once.

On the shingle behind her, in their row, with their backs to the lake, the elders sat and looked at it and didn't move.

---

Then the beasts came.

Everything in the Thornwood that could run came out of it at once.

She saw them come out of the trees at the top of the slope. Not a few. Not tens. Everything. A brown flood, a grey flood, a black flood, pouring out from under the edge of the forest along its whole length, as if the forest had been tipped up and everything in it was sliding out. Deer. Elk, wild ones, with their antlers back. Boar, in a mass. Wolves, wild, running with their tails down. Bears. Foxes and martens and badgers and things so small she couldn't see what they were, a moving carpet in the grass. And a herd of wild stonehorns, a hundred of them, grey, plated, coming out of the trees at the west end at a run with their heads down and the ground shaking under them.

All running one way. South. Away from the stag. Toward the water.

Their eyes were rolling. She could see it from the lane. White all round. Mad. The surge was in them, the whole wild flood of the Deep coming up out of the ground, and it had gone into them, and they weren't beasts any more, they were fear with legs.

They came down the slope.

They came down the lanes.

---

The wild stonehorns hit the tame ones in the western lanes with a sound like two cliffs meeting.

She heard it. She didn't see; she was looking up her own lane. She heard the crash of a hundred plated beasts running full into sixty standing ones, horn on horn, plate on plate, and the bellowing, and then the horse clans' riders shouting, high and fierce, and the sound changed. Turned. The wild herd had broken on the wall of tame ones and gone round, sideways, east, and then down, and she heard them hit the water at the bottom of the slope, a hundred great bodies going into the lake at once like a landslide going into a river.

In the middle lanes, the bears stood.

She saw the great pale one, in the third lane. Standing on its hind legs, taller than the roundhouses, with its forelegs out and its head down, roaring. The flood came down the lane at it, elk and boar and deer, and split round it, like water round a rock, and went on down past it on either side, between it and the houses, into the lake.

On the roof-ridges, the duskcats. When a beast came off the lane, toward a door, a duskcat came off the ridge onto it. Black, silent, out of nowhere. Onto its back. And it went the other way.

And in her own lane, the near lane, the flood came down at Orrun.

---

He stood across it.

She stood beside him. There was nothing she could do. She stood at his shoulder in the near lane with Haverly's sword in her hand, dark, useless, and the flood came down the slope at them: deer and boar and a pack of wild wolves and an elk bull with its antlers back and its eyes white, all of it, mad, coming straight down the lane at the lion.

He roared.

She'd heard him roar on the island, on the night the collar came off. She'd heard him roar at Greyhold's gate. She'd never heard this. It came out of him and went up the lane into the flood like a wall, and the ground came with it.

The turf of the lane came up. In front of him. All along the lane's width. A long bank of earth, heaving up out of the ground like a wave rising, as high as her shoulder, with the grass still on it. Bending. Curving. Away from the houses, toward the next lane, toward the water.

The flood hit the bank and turned.

It went along it. Round it. The deer and the boar and the wild wolves and the elk bull, all of it, turned by the bank of earth, going sideways and down, past the lion, past her, between the houses and the bank, down to the lake. She felt them go past. She felt the ground shake with them. An elk went by so close its flank hit her shoulder and knocked her into Orrun's side.

He didn't move.

She felt him, through the bond. Holding the bank. The earth in him, going out of him into the ground, into the bank, and holding it up against the flood, as he'd held the slope in the sink and the stone that wanted to move. In the surge it came too easy. Too much. The ground wanted to heave. He held it, and it held.

---

Then the far lane broke.

She felt it before she saw it. Through him. He'd turned his head, and she turned hers, and looked across the row of roundhouses to the next lane east. The sixth.

There was nobody in it.

They'd meant to take it. Both of them. The near one and the far one. But there was only one of him, and the flood had come down the near lane first, and he'd stood in it. And the far lane was empty, and the flood was coming down it, and at the bottom of it, by the water, there was a big roundhouse with stone footings.

Full of children.

She'd seen them go in. At dusk. The children who'd stayed, whose families hadn't believed the glass, or couldn't go on the water, or wouldn't leave their elk. Thirty of them. Forty. In the big roundhouse at the bottom of the sixth lane, with two old women, and the door shut.

The flood was coming down the sixth lane at it.

She felt him decide.

---

He didn't tell her. He didn't ask. She felt it go out of him, all at once, as it had at the stakes on midsummer night, only more. A great pulling. A gathering. And then she was stretched.

Across the row of roundhouses, in the far lane, the turf heaved and something came up out of it.

A lion.

Grey. Enormous. The same size, the same weight, the same mane like iron-grey cloud, the same pale eyes. Coming up out of the earth of the sixth lane like a swimmer out of a lake, and shaking itself, and standing. Across the lane. In front of the flood. With the children's roundhouse at its back.

It roared.

She heard it twice. With her ears, from across the row of houses. And from inside, through the bond, the same roar, in the same moment, from the same throat. From both throats. Both of them. Him.

The turf of the far lane came up in a bank.

The flood hit it, and turned.

---

She was in two places.

She didn't know how else to say it, after. She tried, to Idony, and he wrote it down, and she read what he'd written and it was wrong, and she couldn't make it right. She stood in the near lane beside Orrun with the flood going past her, and she was there. And she stood in the far lane, too, in the turf, in the earth, in the bank, with the flood going past, and the children's roundhouse at her back, and she was there. Stretched between them like a skin over two drums, and both drums being beaten.

She was cold.

The way she'd been cold at the stakes, in the middle of her bones. Only it went on. It didn't stop after two minutes. It went on, and on, and the flood went on coming down the two lanes, deer and boar and wolves and bears, and the two banks held, and the cold went deeper, and she couldn't find her feet.

She looked down. They were there. Her boots, in the turf of the near lane. She couldn't feel them. She couldn't feel her legs. She couldn't tell which lane they were standing in.

She stood in two lanes at once and held on.

---

In the seventh lane, Hesper was singing.

Maren heard her through the far lion's ears. The seventh lane was the next one over from the sixth, the narrow one, by the forest's edge, and the far Orrun was close enough to hear. And through him, she heard.

Hesper was standing in the narrow lane with her spear in her hand and Hush beside her, low, flat to the ground, with her tail lashing. And the flood was coming down the seventh lane at them. Less than the others, because it was narrow. But the first of everything had come out of the trees there, nearest the forest's edge, and it was coming.

She was asking.

In the old tongue. The Keepers' way. Maren heard the words and didn't know them and knew what they were: an asking. *Go round. Go round. Not this way. Go round.* Not a charm put on a beast. A charm asked of one. The way the Keepers had always done it, mother to child, never on a beast without its leave.

In the surge, it took too hard.

They heard her. Every one. The whole front of the flood coming down the seventh lane heard the Keeper ask, and turned, all at once, as Hesper's knot had wound too tight round her fingers and her torch had flared to her wrist. They turned. Too hard. Too fast. A wild stonehorn bull at the front of them, mad, plated, as big as a haywain, heard her ask it to go round, and went round.

Into the side of the lane.

Into the roundhouse on the lane's edge. The wall of it. With all its weight.

---

The roundhouse came down.

Maren heard it. The far Orrun heard it. The crack of the roof-tree. The rush of thatch and wattle and turf coming down all at once into the lane, onto the narrow place where the Keeper stood with her spear and her duskcat.

Under it, as it came down, Hesper was singing.

Not the asking. The other thing. The little tune. Rising and falling, as you'd sing to something you were trying to rock. Maren heard it, through him, the last of it, very clear, in the clans' tongue and then, at the very end, as if she'd known somebody was listening who wouldn't understand, in Maren's own.

*Hush, now. Hush. Like the six who sleep.*

Then the roundhouse was down, and the stonehorn bull was through the wreck of it and gone down the lane to the water, and the rest of the flood behind it, turned, going round, as she'd asked them to.

And the singing stopped.

---

The great stag didn't come to the lake.

She saw it, at some point. She didn't know when. Through Orrun's eyes or her own. Over the forest to the north, the antlers, going away. Not south. North and east, into the deep Thornwood, slowly, with the forest walking round it. She could see the treetops moving, a great slow wave of moving trees going away into the forest with the bare crown of antlers above it. And with every step it took, the ground under the Moot heaved, and the lake slopped, and the boats out on the water rose and fell.

Slower. Fainter. Further off.

By noon the antlers were out of sight.

By noon the flood had thinned. Fewer beasts. Then a few. Then a deer, alone, coming down the near lane at a walk, with its sides heaving, looking round as if it didn't know where it was. It went past her and down to the water and drank.

The far Orrun went at noon.

She felt it go. Back into him. Out of the far lane, out of the bank, out of the earth, back into the one body in the near lane beside her, like water going down a drain. And all its cold came with it. Half an hour, Idony told her after. He'd counted from the boat. Half an hour, near enough. Longer than he'd ever held one.

All the cold came into her at once.

She went down in the near lane.

She didn't fall. Her legs weren't there. She was standing, and then she was sitting in the turf with her back against the bank he'd raised, with the sword across her lap, and she couldn't feel anything below her waist at all. She looked at her legs. They were there. She couldn't move them. She couldn't feel them. They were someone else's.

Orrun lay down beside her.

All at once, the way he had at the sink. As if the strings had been cut. He lay down in the turf of the near lane with his head on his paws, and she felt him through the bond, and he was so tired that it frightened her. Emptied. Hollowed. As if he'd poured himself out into two bodies and only half of it had come back.

She put her hand on his neck. It was all she could do.

They lay there in the lane together while the last of the deer went down to the water.

---

The clans came in off the lake at dusk.

The boats first, then the rafts, then the upside-down roof with its forty people, paddled in to the shingle with poles. They came up the slope in the dusk, looking for their own, calling names.

Forty-odd of those who'd stayed on the shore were dead. Trampled in the lanes, or crushed in the roundhouses at the edges, or gone into the lake with the flood and not come up. Two of the horse clans' riders, between the stonehorns. A Keeper in the fourth lane, and his wolves. The great pale bear in the third lane was lying down, alive, with its sides going in and out, and a Keeper with a broken arm sitting against it, singing to it.

Of the children in the roundhouses by the water, none.

Not one. Maren heard it said, at the fires, over and over, by people who couldn't stop saying it. The children in the big roundhouses at the bottom of the lanes, where the lanes had held. None of them. Not in the western lanes, where the stonehorns stood. Not in the middle, where the bears stood. Not at the bottom of the sixth lane, where a second lion had come up out of the ground and stood across it for half an hour.

None.

---

They found Hesper and Hush at dusk.

Under the roundhouse in the seventh lane. Maren couldn't go; she couldn't stand. She heard it. Somebody came down the slope and said it at the fire where she was lying against Orrun, with Idony beside her with his crate, and she heard it, and didn't say anything.

Together. Under the roof-tree. The duskcat lying across the Keeper, as if she'd got there first.

Elder Aun's girl led her up the slope to them in the dark. Maren didn't see that either. Idony went, and told her after. The old blind woman knelt down in the wreck of the roundhouse in the seventh lane, on the girl's arm, and felt for Hesper's face with her hands, and found it, and kept her hands there.

Then she said it. The lullaby. In the clans' tongue. Very low, very dry, in her cracked old voice, to the Keeper and the duskcat lying together under the thatch.

*Hush, now. Hush. Like the six who sleep.*

---

She couldn't feel her legs until dawn.

She lay by the fire against Orrun's side with Idony's coat over her, and the clans' fires going all along the slope of the Moot, and the lake behind her black and still again, and the stars. Every so often she tried to move her feet. Nothing. Then, a long time after midnight, a prickling. Like pins. Starting at her hips and going down, slowly, so slowly, until near dawn she could feel her toes in her boots, cold, and move them.

Orrun didn't eat.

Somebody brought him meat at the fire. An elk, one of the dead ones from the lanes, a haunch of it. He didn't look at it. Somebody brought him water and he drank a little. He lay with his head on his paws and his eyes half shut, and through the bond she felt him, hollow, and so tired that he couldn't be anything else.

*Hesper,* he said, once, in the night.

"I know."

*She called me Grandfather.*

"I know."

He didn't say anything else.

---

The word went round the fires that night.

She heard it. She couldn't help hearing it. All along the slope, at every fire, in the clans' tongue, people saying the same thing to each other, over and over, as people do when they've seen something and need to make sure the others saw it too.

Two lions.

Two lions, in two lanes, at once. The grey-maned, and another one, the same, come up out of the ground. In the near lane and the far. Both of them. For half an hour. Somebody had seen it from the water. Somebody had seen it from a roof. Somebody's child had seen it from the door of the big roundhouse at the bottom of the sixth lane, before the old women pulled her back in.

Magic, somebody said.

The lion-woman's gift, somebody else said. The one who sits with it. She can be in two places at once. She stood in the near lane and she stood in the far lane, and the grey-maned stood in both with her.

She lay against the lion with the feeling coming back into her legs like pins, and the lullaby going round and round in her head in a dead woman's voice, and let them say it.

Idony sat beside her by the fire with his crate on his knees and his ledger open and wrote it down. All of it. What they said, and what he'd counted from the boat, and what he'd seen. Once he looked up at her, and she saw him want to ask, and not.
