The old one wouldn't come.

He'd known she wouldn't. He asked anyway, on the setters' ledge on the morning of the hundred-and-fifteenth day, with Kharrak waiting on the high ledge above with his wings half open and Lisska on the rock behind, fidgeting.

*I have flown a quarter of a league,* said Aurveth. *Crooked. Once.* She had her head on her forelegs and her one gold eye on him. *Wendmouth is a hundred.*

"I know."

*Go. Take the red one and the little one. Do the thing.* A pause. *I'll be here. I am not going to fall off a ledge now, after all this.*

He put his hand on her jaw.

*Bring it back,* she said. *Whatever it is. And bring yourself back with it.*

"You said that last time."

*It was true last time,* said Aurveth. *It is still true.*

---

They came over Wendmouth at dusk on the second day.

He'd seen it twice. Once from the drove road in the autumn, walking, with nothing in his head but a lion and a name. Once from the Wendwood's edge, with Lisska bringing him bread. He'd never seen it from above.

It lay at the mouth of the Wend where the river went out into the sea through a mile of mudflats, brown and shining in the last light, with channels running through them like veins. On the east bank, the city. Warehouses on stilts over the mud, painted, row on row. The four houses' colours: blue for Marrow, white for Quill, green for Strand. And red and gold for Halloran, more of it than any of the others, all along Gallows Quay and up the hill behind.

The floating market: boats lashed together in the harbour, a hundred of them, with lanterns coming on. The Tidesteps going up from the Strand in a great flight of stone. At the top of the steps, on the iron rail where Vale had stood, papers.

Lisska went close enough to read them. She couldn't read. She could see.

*Your face,* she said, coming back. *The old one, from the autumn. All grey with rain.* A pause. *And a new one over it. Bigger. With more numbers.*

"How many?"

*A five,* said Lisska. *And the circles.*

Five hundred.

---

Kharrak lay up in the saltings north of the city, in a creek among reeds taller than a man, with the tide going out round him and the mud steaming in the evening.

*I'll lie still,* he said. *I'll not light anything.* A pause. *Unless.*

"Unless what?"

*Unless,* said Kharrak, and shut his eyes.

Calder walked in at dawn as a drover. He'd walked into the Cross like that, and into Wendmouth in the autumn: with his hood up and a borrowed crook and his head down, among the carters on the north road, past the toll-house, into the city. Nobody looked at him. A man with a long face and a hat, the paper said. He'd left the hat at Varrhold.

---

Lisska found Brask at noon, in the Crane.

The tavern on Gallows Quay where a beast-seller had grumbled in the autumn about knights freeing other people's property. Low, dark, smoky, smelling of fish and tar and old beer, with a crane's head carved over the door. Calder came in out of the bright quay and stood blinking, and a voice from the corner said, "Farmer," and it was Brask.

He was thinner. He had a cup in front of him and a heel of bread and a black eye going yellow. He didn't get up.

"You're the most wanted man in Saltreach," he said. "Did you know? Five hundred silver. It's on the Tidesteps. And you've walked into the Crane on Gallows Quay at noon." He shook his head. "You don't learn."

Calder sat down.

"You got home," he said.

"I got home." Brask looked at his cup. "Over your moor. All of us. Not a hen taken. The old woman on the black-and-grey dragon sat on a crag over us for three days and never said a word. Then down Skarrow's side to the coast, and along it, and home. The forty-fifth day." He drank. "Delling paid us off at the fish-docks with what was left in the chest. Two silver a man. The company's broken." He looked at Calder. "I owe you my legs, and now I owe you my road. I'm running out of things to owe you."

"Tell me about the silver."

Brask looked at him.

"The new silver," said Calder. "With the six on the rim."

---

Brask told him, low, with his head down over the cup.

It was struck here. In Wendmouth. Everybody on the quays knew that; nobody said it. The Tide Council gave a licence to strike the ship-and-wave to one house at a time, and the house that held it now was Halloran. They struck it in a cellar under the Halloran counting-house on Mint Lane, behind Gallows Quay. Ordinary coin, mostly. Saltreach silver. But some of it went out with the six on the rim.

"Rusk," said Calder.

"Rusk." Brask nodded. "The factor. The beast-office at the end of the quay. Plump little man, red coat. He runs the mint for the house. He's always writing in a little book." He drank. "And there's a big book. In the mint. That's the one the house keeps. Every chest that goes out, what's in it, who for. Rusk writes that one too."

"How do you know?"

"My cousin carts the blanks." Brask turned the cup. "The silver comes up from the coast in bars, and they cut the blanks in a yard down the fish-docks, and my cousin carts them up Mint Lane in a covered cart every tenth day and backs it up to a grating in the street and they go down a chute. And every new moon, at night, chests come up the chute and go down to the river on the same cart, onto a barge." He looked at Calder. "Going up the Wend."

"Up the Wend where?"

"My cousin doesn't ask," said Brask. "That's why he's still got a cousin."

---

Lisska went out that night without being sent.

He felt her go. He was lying in the loft of a net-maker's shed on the fish-docks that Brask had found him, under a heap of old nets that smelled of the sea, and he felt her go up off the roof where she'd been sitting and away across the harbour in the dark. A few streets. And stop.

She didn't come back till dawn.

He knew where she'd been. He didn't know how he knew. In the morning she came down on the net-maker's roof and folded her wings and sat there, and he climbed up through the hatch and sat beside her on the wet slates in the grey light, and didn't ask.

She sat a long while before she spoke.

*There's a house by the fish-docks,* she said at last. *A lodging-house. With yellow cloth on the door. Old. Torn.* A pause. *The yellow tower.*

He kept still.

*They're all there. Delling's. What's left. They come and go. They drink.* She was looking at the harbour, not at him. *I sat on the roof all night. And looked down. In the yard. At the ones who came and went.*

"Lisska."

*I didn't do anything,* she said.

"I know."

*I didn't do anything. I sat on the roof and looked.* She was quiet. *I could see him. I think. I don't know. I don't know his face. Imra knew his face, maybe, at the end, in the dark. I don't.* A long pause. *But I sat there, and I knew he was in that yard or in that house, and I could have come down.*

"But you didn't."

*No.*

He waited.

*I don't know why,* said Lisska. *I wanted to. All night. And I didn't.* She turned her head and looked at him with her pale green eyes. *Is that what you did? On the crag? With the red one under you?*

"Yes," said Calder.

She looked at him a while longer. Then she looked back at the harbour.

*It's horrible,* she said.

"Yes."

He sat with her on the wet slates until the sun came up over the mudflats, and she didn't say anything else, and he was more afraid of what she'd chosen not to do than of anything she could have done.

---

They watched Mint Lane for three days.

It ran up behind Gallows Quay from the water, narrow, cobbled, with the backs of the Halloran warehouses on one side and their counting-house on the other. Three storeys of red brick with red and gold shutters, and guards on the door, two, with cudgels and Halloran sashes. A grand front door with a brass knocker in the shape of a ship. And at the foot of the wall, at street level, a grating.

Iron. Barred. The size of a cart's tail. Smoke came out of it, sometimes, black, and heat; you could feel it in the street. Calder walked past it twice a day with his crook and his hood up and didn't look down.

Lisska watched from the roofs. She could see, through the bars, a chute going down, and a red glow at the bottom of it. A furnace. And men, in leather aprons, going back and forth.

On the third day Brask's cousin backed his covered cart up to the grating and the blanks went down the chute in a long silver rush, like water. Calder stood in a doorway across the lane with Brask and watched them go.

"New moon's in nine days," said Brask. "That's when the chests come up."

"We're not waiting nine days."

Brask looked at him.

"I'm not here for the chests," said Calder. "I'm here for the book."

---

They went in on the night of the hundred-and-twentieth day.

Lisska on the roof of the warehouse opposite, watching. The guards on the counting-house door changed at midnight; she saw them go in, and the new ones come out, and stand, and after an hour sit down on the step. Kharrak in the saltings, a league off, a warm red weight at the back of Calder's head, near enough to call.

Brask had a crowbar. A ship's bar, iron, as long as his leg. He'd got it off a chandler. Calder had used one like it on these same quays in the autumn, on the cage bolts, the night he took Mabbin home.

They came down Mint Lane in the dark from the top end, slowly, close against the warehouse wall. The grating was warm. The furnace below was banked for the night: a red glow, faint, at the bottom of the chute.

Brask put the bar under the grating's edge and leaned on it.

It didn't move.

He leaned harder. The veins stood out on his neck. Calder put his hands on the bar beside Brask's and leaned too.

The grating came up out of its frame with a shriek of iron that went down the lane and off the warehouse walls like a cat in a trap.

They froze.

*Nothing,* said Lisska, from the roof. *They're on the step. One of them's asleep. The other one's looking at the moon.* A pause. *He didn't hear. Go.*

Calder went down the chute.

---

It was hot at the bottom. A vaulted cellar, brick, low, with the furnace at one end banked and glowing, and the heat coming off it like a wall. He stood up in the red dark and let his eyes get used to it.

Presses. Three of them, iron, with long levers, like the cider press at the Fenn farm only bigger, and a die set in each, the upper and lower, for striking. Trays of blanks. Trays of struck coin, cooling. Ship, wave. Ship, wave.

On a rack on the wall, the dies.

Pairs of them, iron, in a row. Each with a ship cut in one face and a wave in the other. And at the end of the rack, by itself, not a pair. A ring. A collar of iron, a hand across, that fitted round a blank while it was struck, to make the rim. He took it down and turned it to the furnace glow.

Round the inside of the collar, cut in, six dots.

He stood there holding it.

He thought of a soft-spoken man in a good dark coat at his gate in the autumn, holding out a coin. He thought of twelve coins on a trough in Varrhold's yard, with the burr still on their edges. He thought of Ulric holding one up on the black stone, and the six on the rim catching the light. *In the die,* Calder had said. *Somebody's minting it.*

Here. In a cellar on Mint Lane.

He put the collar inside his coat.

---

The book was on a lectern by the far wall.

Big. Bigger than Rook's. Bound in red leather, with gold on the spine, open on the lectern under a shuttered lamp, like the great ledgers in the factors' houses at the Cross. He pulled the shutter on the lamp back a finger's width and looked at the open page.

Rows. Columns. A neat small hand, very even, the same all the way down. Numbers.

He could read numbers.

He could read some of the words now. Not many. He'd been at it with his mother and Wren for sixty days, with a burnt stick on the cave floor and a board at Fisk's. He knew his own name. He knew the letters, all of them, though some of them still went the wrong way when he wrote. He could make out short words if he went slowly and said each letter in his head.

He turned the pages back. Slowly. With a wet thumb, as Ulric had.

He didn't know what he was looking for. He looked anyway. Rows and rows. Numbers. Words he couldn't make out. Words he could. *Chest. Silver. Barge.* A name he didn't know. A name he didn't know.

Then three letters, on their own, at the start of a line.

T. E. O.

He stood in the red dark of the mint with his thumb on the page and looked at them.

He couldn't read the rest of the line. The words after it were long, and the hand was small, and his eyes wouldn't go. But he could read that. Three letters. A name. He'd stood in the beck to his knees with that name in his arms, with its head going back over his arm, and he'd never once seen it written down.

He couldn't move.

"Farmer," said Brask, from the top of the chute, in a whisper. "What are you doing?"

---

Brask came down. He read it.

He read it slowly, by the shuttered lamp, with his finger, like Arn, like Ada.

"*The Varr boy at the ford,*" said Brask. "*Three hundred. For the account of the House. At the instruction of the Council's member.*"

He stopped.

"The House," he said. "That's Halloran. They always call it that in their books. *The House.*" He looked at the page. "And the Council's member. That's Halloran too. The Halloran house holds a seat on the Tide Council. Has done for a hundred years." He looked up at Calder in the red glow. "A Council house paid for your Varr boy, farmer. Three hundred silver. At the instruction of its own seat."

Calder didn't say anything.

"There's more," said Brask. He turned the pages. "Look. *The companies, the muster, the Ram's Head.* Chest after chest. *Wages for the drover at Varrhold.* That's your man, isn't it? Rook? Paid from here." He turned another page. "*The Halloran woman, lent, for the gate.*" Another. "It's all here. The whole war. The whole winter. Paid from this cellar. The House paid for the whole thing."

---

There were two more pages Calder made him read twice.

The first was near the end. Written since the spring, the ink still dark.

"*The four,*" Brask read. "*Bound. North. For a library. The hunters' wages: a year's.*" He frowned. "North for a library. What library?"

Calder didn't know. He thought of the four young dragons going up off the Choosing Ledge into the smoke with binders on their necks, empty-eyed. The big bronze. The black. The two reds.

North.

The second was on the last written page.

"*The companies at Ashford,*" read Brask. "*A chest, up the Wend, at every new moon.*" He looked up. "Ashford. That's in Hearthmere. Over the river."

"The army," said Calder. "The one from the Cross. They went over."

Brask nodded slowly. "They went over in the summer. The silver came at last, and they went over the Wend into dead Hearthmere and took Ashford. That's what the quays say." He put his finger on the page. "And here's where the silver came from. Every new moon. A chest up the river on a barge from Mint Lane." He looked at Calder. "The House is paying for the war in Hearthmere. Still. Now."

Calder closed the book.

---

The clerk was behind the presses.

They'd missed him. A young man asleep on a pallet in the warm corner behind the third press, under a blanket, with his boots off. He'd slept through the grating. He woke now, at the sound of the book closing, and sat up, and saw them.

Brask had the crowbar up before Calder could move.

"No," said Calder.

"He's seen us."

"No."

The clerk sat on his pallet with his blanket clutched to his chest and his face white in the furnace glow. Nineteen, perhaps. Thin, with ink on his fingers and a clerk's soft hands and a Halloran sash hung on a nail over his head.

"Please," he said.

"What's your name?"

"Abbot. Piers Abbot. I'm Master Rusk's under-clerk. I keep the..." He swallowed. "I keep the counts. In the mint. I sleep here so nobody comes in at night." He looked at the grating, at the open chute. "Somebody came in."

"Where's Rusk?"

"Corsa." The clerk was babbling now. Calder had seen it before, in men who think talking will keep them alive. "At the Council. The Tide Council. He sits for the house when the master's away, he's always at the Council. Please. I only keep the counts."

"What counts?"

"Everything. The silver. The chests. The..." He stopped. Then, faster: "The lamps. The lamps are mine, mostly, I keep those."

"What lamps?"

---

"For Vael," said Piers Abbot.

He couldn't stop now. He sat on his pallet and it came out of him like water out of a cracked jug.

"The colleges. At Vael. They're buying binding-lamps. By the cartload. Tall iron ones with crystal in them, the crystal comes from Kessmoor, out of the Iron Fells, cut, and the colleges cut the words in it themselves, the old words, and the house buys the crystal in Kessmoor and ships it up the Wend and over the pass, and takes a tenth." He swallowed. "Cartloads. Every month since the spring. Since the thing flew. They can't get enough. They want a hundred. They want more than a hundred." He looked at Calder. "I keep the count. That's all. That's all I do. Please."

Calder looked at him.

He didn't know what a binding-lamp was. He knew what a binding was.

"Tie him," he said to Brask.

---

They came up the chute into the grey of the dawn, with the book.

And the lane was full.

Not full. Six of them. Rusk's guards, with cudgels and Halloran sashes, and the two off the door, awake now. Somebody had heard something. Somebody had gone for them. They were in the lane at the bottom end, toward the quay, in a line across it.

And behind them, coming up from the quay, a man in a plain coat.

Lean. Grey. Nobody you'd look at twice. Walking slowly up the cobbles of Mint Lane with his hands at his sides, and the guards stood aside for him as a dog gets off a chair.

Calder knew what he was before he lifted his hands.

The Halloran house's drawer. Not the woman from the winter, the plain grey-haired woman Calder had seen at the Ram's Head and at Greyhold's gate, carried off white-haired. A new one. The house kept one, the way it kept a lawyer.

He stopped twenty paces off, in the middle of the lane, and put his feet apart on the cobbles, and lifted his hands.

*Calder,* said Lisska, from the roof. *Calder, he's going to...*

The lane went white.

---

Not fire. Weight. Calder felt it come: a great flat push of nothing, off the man's palms, down the lane, like a wave coming up a beach, and it hit him and Brask and the book and threw them back against the counting-house wall so hard the breath went out of him and the bricks cracked behind his head.

He slid down the wall.

The drawer lifted his hands again.

And Kharrak came down out of the morning.

---

He'd come from the saltings. Calder felt him come: a red tide, coming up the back of his head from a league off, getting nearer, louder, hotter, faster than he'd ever felt the red dragon fly. Over the mudflats. Over the floating market, where every boat's lantern swung. Over Gallows Quay, low, the first time in broad day, the whole length of the quay, with the beast-sellers diving into doorways and the gulls going up in a white roar.

Into Mint Lane.

He didn't land. There wasn't room. He came over the roofs and down into the narrow lane with his wings half closed, like a hawk into a hedge, and his tail came round.

It took the drawer across the chest.

The man went up. Off his feet, off the cobbles, up and over the guards' heads and out of the bottom of the lane and across Gallows Quay in a long curve, arms out, and into the harbour with a splash Calder heard from the top of the lane.

The guards ran.

Kharrak landed on the quay at the bottom of the lane with a crash that cracked the cobbles and stood there, red, enormous, with fire in his throat and the morning sun on him, looking up the lane at Calder.

*Unless,* he said.

---

Calder got up.

He had the book. Brask had the crowbar. They went down Mint Lane past the place where the drawer had stood, onto Gallows Quay, past the red dragon. The quay was empty. Everybody had run. The beast-sellers, the buyers, the porters, the gulls. Only the cages were left. Along the whole length of the quay, in rows, as in the autumn. Hounds and martens and falcons and a sea-otter and a young wyvern, hooded, wild-caught on the coast, and goats and a white ox and things he didn't have names for.

He took the crowbar from Brask.

He went down the cages, one after another, the way he'd done in the autumn, and broke the bolts, and let them out. Hounds went past him down the quay. The martens went up the warehouse walls. The wyvern threw off its hood and went up into the sky over the harbour screaming. The white ox stood in its open pen looking at him until Brask hit it on the rump with his hat.

At the end of the quay, the Halloran beast-office. With the shutter on the side, where in the autumn he'd looked through and seen a boy with an iron ring kneeling in honey-smoke beside a hound with empty eyes. And in the front window, on its velvet cushion, in its glass case, the phoenix feather. Red-gold. Still warm. He could feel it through the glass from a pace away.

He left it.

He didn't take what wasn't his.

He came back up the quay to Kharrak, and stood by the red dragon's shoulder, and looked at the warehouse beside the beast-office. The big one. Red and gold. The one the drawer had come out of in the autumn, and the man in grey, and the smoke.

It was empty. He'd let everything out.

"That one," he said.

---

Kharrak burned it to the waterline.

He did it slowly, because Calder asked him to, so nobody would be in it. He walked round it first, on the quay, and roared, once, at each door, and waited. Nobody came out. There was nobody in it. Then he put his head down to the stilts it stood on, over the mud, and opened his mouth.

The fire went in under it.

It went up as a hayrick goes up. The tarred stilts first, then the floor, then the walls, then the great red and gold roof, all at once, with a roar like the sea and black smoke going up over Gallows Quay into the morning so thick it hid the sun. The heat came off it like a forge. The Halloran colours on the wall went black and curled and fell. The windows burst. The roof came in. The stilts went, and the whole burning thing went down into the mud with a hiss that went on and on, until there was nothing at the end of the quay but black timbers standing in the tide and the smoke going up.

Calder stood on the quay in the smoke with the book under his arm.

Up on the warehouse roofs, on the Tidesteps, on the boats of the floating market, people were watching. Hundreds of them. He could see them through the smoke, standing still, looking at the farmer on the quay with the red dragon beside him and the Halloran warehouse burning to the waterline.

He didn't hide his face.

---

They went north up the coast into the saltings.

Brask came. He said he wasn't staying in Wendmouth with his face seen on Gallows Quay next to a dragon, not for a week or two, and anyway somebody had to know which way north was. They walked out through the reed-beds behind Kharrak, and the red dragon flew low ahead of them, and Lisska over, and nobody followed.

Five days up the coast there was a fishing-hamlet. Twelve huts on a shingle bank, and drying-racks, and boats drawn up, and people who looked at a red dragon on their shingle and a green one on their racks and two men coming up the beach with a red book, and asked nothing.

The new reward was on the Tidesteps by the hundred-and-twenty-fourth day, a fisherman said. He'd heard it from a boat that came up the coast. A thousand silver. For the farmer. The Tide Council's own mark on it, the wave and the coin.

"A thousand," said Brask, by the fire. "You've doubled your price twice in a year. There's not a factor on the coast can say that."

---

Calder read the mint-book by the fire for five nights.

A page a night. The way his mother had read Rook's. With his finger under the line, slowly, saying each letter in his head, and Brask beside him reading the long words when he couldn't. He made himself do it. He made himself read every line he could, and only let Brask read the ones he couldn't, and some nights that was most of them.

But it was his. That was the thing. Some of the words were his. He'd read them himself, with his own eyes, on a page.

*TEO.*

*VARR.*

*FORD.*

*THREE HUNDRED.*

He read the page with Teo's name on it every night. He didn't need to. He knew what it said. He read it anyway.

---

On the hundred-and-twenty-seventh day Brask said he was staying.

"Somebody's got to," he said. "If you don't come back from wherever you're going. North, for a library." He poked the fire. "Somebody's got to tell the Varrs where the silver's struck. And who struck it. And for whom." He looked at the red book on Calder's knee. "I can read. I'll walk to the Scarfell by the coast road. Down Skarrow's side. I know the way. I've walked it."

Calder looked at him for a long time.

Then he took the coin out of his coat. The one Ulric had given him on the black stone. New silver, a ship, a wave, and the six on the rim.

He held it out.

"Give them that," he said. "With the word. So they know it's from me."

Brask took it. He turned it so the six caught the firelight.

"You're a strange man, farmer," he said. "I said that before. I'll say it again."

---

He went north on the hundred-and-thirtieth day.

On Kharrak, with Lisska. Up the coast first, then inland, over the scrub and the Ashlings and the low hills, toward a line of mountains in the north that Brask said were the Iron Fells, white along the top even in summer. And beyond them, Vael. The colleges. A library.

He had the red book inside his coat. And Rook's book, in its oiled cloth. And the knife, and the iron ring, and the folded bill, and the tin charm, and an iron collar with six dots cut round the inside. And a stone with a white ring round it.

*North for a library,* said Lisska, behind him in the air.

"North for a library."

*What's in a library?*

He thought about it.

"Books," he said. "Everybody's books. All the ones they wrote down."

Lisska flew in silence for a while.

*You can read now,* she said. *A bit.*

"A bit."

*Then you can read them,* said the green dragon. *When we get there.*

They went north.
