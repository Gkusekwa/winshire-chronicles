He found her on the hundred-and-thirty-sixth day, at the end of the old fish-quay, where the lion lay on the shingle at the water's edge.

He'd known where she'd be. Everybody in the lower city knew. A lion the size of a dray-horse doesn't walk through Vael without the whole of Ferry Street knowing which way it went. It had gone down through the lower city on the evening of the hundred-and-thirty-fifth day, past the brown lamps, past the fish-market and Mother Talley's dark corner, to the shore. To the end of the old fish-quay that nobody used any more because the stones were sinking. And lain down on the shingle beyond it, at the very edge of the water, with its forepaws almost in the lake.

Facing the island. A mile out.

As close as dry land would let it.

---

She was sitting beside it on the shingle with her knees up and her coat round her against the cold that came off the water.

He came down the old quay slowly. The stones rocked under his boots. He stopped twenty paces off, as he'd stopped at the Ring, and waited, and she looked round.

"Reader Vael," she said.

"Idony."

She looked at him a moment. Then she nodded.

He came the rest of the way and sat down on the shingle on her other side, not too close to the lion. Orrun didn't look at him. He lay with his chin on his paws and his eyes half shut, facing the island, and Idony could see the band of pale at the roots of his mane, wider than it had been at the Ring, and the ribs under the grey coat.

The island lay on the water a mile out, in the late afternoon, grey, breathing.

"He's lying at ten paces," said Idony.

Maren looked at him.

"At the Ring. He lay at ten paces from its head." He looked at the mile of flat water between the shingle and the island. "This is as close as ten paces gets, here."

"Yes," said Maren.

---

He took out the field-glass after a while, because he couldn't not.

He set it on the shingle a pace from his own knee. The quicksilver lay in the bottom. Lower city; the drain. He moved it along the shingle, toward the lion. A pace. Two. Into the stones where Orrun's flank lay against them.

It lifted.

A finger's breadth. Off the bottom of the throat. And stayed there, in the brown dead shingle of the lower city's shore, where nothing else on his glasses had lifted for twenty days.

Maren watched him look at it.

"It does that," she said. "Wherever he lies."

"I know." He put it back in its case. "I wrote it down. At the Ring."

They sat on the shingle and watched the island.

---

"The bird wants to see you," he said, when the light was going.

She didn't turn her head.

"The Old Reader. In the Library. It said, when you come, bring her to me. Before you came. Eight days ago." He looked at his hands. "It walked to the window. Yesterday. For the first time in anyone's memory. To see you go past."

"I know. I heard it."

"What did it say?"

"My name."

He'd known that. He'd known it at the stall at the bottom of Ferry Street with his spoon in his hand.

"It wants you to come up," he said. "To the far end. Where it sits."

Maren looked at the lion beside her. At the island.

"When he'll leave the water," she said.

"When will that be?"

"Not yet."

He didn't argue. He'd learned that much, at the Ring, and at the Moot. He sat with them on the shingle until it was dark and the lower city's lamps came on brown behind them and the cold came up off the lake, and then he got up and went home.

---

The talk in the Library's great hall on the hundred-and-thirty-seventh evening was all about her.

He heard it because he'd gone there to work, at the long tables at the door end of the Long Library, where the readers sat in the evenings with lamps because the lamps up here on the hill burned white and bright and you could read till midnight without straining. A dozen of them. Second rings and Third rings, students and readers and a few old Fourths who'd never gone further. He'd sat among them for eleven years. They'd never talked to him much.

They were talking now. Not to him. Round him.

"At the lake gate," a Third ring from the Scribes' College was saying, a young man with a beard he was proud of. "Two days ago. My cousin's a clerk on the register. Wouldn't write the beast in. Said it wasn't a gift. Said it wasn't hers." He laughed. "As if that's how it works."

"What did the Wardens do?"

"Nothing." The young Scribe looked round the table, pleased to have something. "That's the thing. Nothing. Old Brannagh was on the gate, the big one, and he looked at the lion, and the lion looked at him, and he said *pass*."

"He can't do that."

"He did."

"It's a scandal," said an old Fourth ring at the end of the table, a woman with ink to her elbows who'd been working on the same commentary for twenty years. "Every bond-gift is wild power. Every one. It's the law of the colleges. If she won't register it, the Blue College should have her up before the Council."

"Who's going to fetch her?" said somebody else. "You?"

The table laughed.

Idony bent over his ledger and didn't say anything. He didn't look up. He wrote the day's readings in his small hand, and under them, very small, so small nobody reading over his shoulder could have made it out: *She won't. And they didn't.*

---

The bird sent for him on the night of the hundred-and-thirty-eighth day.

He was in the attic, at the window, with the glasses on the sill and the lamps of the city below him going brown and white. The line between them was at Candle Row now. He'd measured it at dusk. And somebody knocked on the attic door at the second bell after dark, which nobody ever did, and he opened it and there was a Library under-clerk on the stair, a boy of fifteen in a grey gown with a candle, out of breath.

"The Old Reader," said the boy. "Asks for Reader Vael. Now, it said." He swallowed. "It said please."

Idony looked at him.

The bird had never said please in his hearing, in eleven years, to anybody.

He got his coat.

---

He went up through the city in the dark.

Up Ferry Street from the water, past the stall with its shutters down, past the brown lamps. Up the steps to Rope Street. Up to Candle Row, where the line was: he felt it. He didn't need a glass. He stepped up the last step onto Candle Row and the lamp on the corner over his head was brown, and he took one more step and the lamp on the next corner was white. And under his boots, through the cobbles, the hum came up, faint, steady, like a bowl that's been struck.

Up the hill. Up past the College of Tides, with its lamps white instead of their own soft blue. Past the Hall of Rings. To the Library.

Not the great door. The small one, at the side, that the librarians used, that the under-clerk had left on the latch. Into the dark of the first hall, and then the light. Every lamp in the Long Library burning white. Half a league of it, under the vaults, between the shelves, empty, with the white light lying on the long tables and on the floor in bars from the tall windows, and the hum coming up through the stone, louder and louder as he went.

He walked the whole half-league.

It took him a long time. He didn't hurry. He thought he'd never walk it again quite like this, at night, alone, with every lamp white, and he wanted to remember it.

At the far end, where the hall went into the hill and the air was cold and smelled of stone, the perch of black oak. And on it, the Old Reader.

"Ferryman," it said.

---

It got down off the perch.

He'd heard it had. He'd never seen it. It came down off the black oak slowly, carefully, with its wings half open for balance, the grey-black feathers going stiff and ragged at the edges, and landed on the stone at the foot of the perch with a small heavy sound, and stood there. On its own feet. It was smaller standing. He'd only ever seen it high up.

It went round to the back of the perch's foot, where the black oak went into the floor, and put its beak to the wood.

There was a panel there. Carved. He'd never noticed it. Eleven years of sitting at the foot of that perch with his charts and his questions, and he'd never once looked at the back of it. A panel of the same black oak, carved with a pattern of hooked marks so worn they were only shadows, the size of a book.

The Old Reader put its beak in a crack at the panel's edge, and pulled, and the panel came open.

Inside, in a niche cut into the foot of the perch, a case.

---

"Take it out," said the Old Reader. "My beak is old."

He knelt on the stone and took it out.

A case of dark wood, as long as his forearm, round, bound at the ends with silver gone black with age. On the side, burned into the wood, a mark he knew. He'd seen it every day of his life: over the College of Tides' door, on the Gallery's shelves, on the seal of every letter the College sent. A wave, and under it a glass. The old mark. Older than the one they used now.

"I have kept it," said the Old Reader, "three hundred and four years."

He knelt on the floor with the case in his hands.

"Nobody has asked for it," said the bird. "In three hundred and four years. Not once."

"Nobody knew to."

"No," said the Old Reader. "That was the point."

---

He opened it on the reading-desk at the foot of the perch, where he'd unrolled his charts on the morning of the breaking.

Vellum. Old. So old it had gone the colour of tea and soft as cloth, and he unrolled it very slowly, a hand's width at a time, holding the edges down with the bird's dish and two books and his own palm. Written all over in the hooked hand. Not the Second ring's hand, the hand he was ranked in and read in public. Not the Fourth's or the Fifth's. The Sixth's. The full script, the old one, the one only two or three people living were supposed to be able to read.

He read it like a Sixth. He always had.

At the top, in larger letters:

*Between the College of Tides at Vael, by its Master, Aldwyn Sarre, Keeper of the Script, of the one part; and the lord of the river hill, in the country by the Wend, of the other.*

He stopped.

The river hill. The country by the Wend.

He'd charted a hole there for eleven years.

---

He read it all.

It took him half the night. The bird sat on the floor beside the desk the whole time, on its own feet, with its head on one side, and didn't hurry him. The white lamps burned. The hum came up through the stone.

Clause by clause. In the old College form, every clause numbered in the old count. The College's undertakings, and then the lord's.

*First: the College shall measure the sleeper beneath the river hill, its breath and its depth and the great draw of its waking, and render the measure to the lord.*

There was a table after it. A reading-table. Columns and figures, in a notation he knew: older and rougher than his own ledgers', but the same. The ancestor of it. He looked at it and saw the first tide-reader who'd ever sat with a glass over a sleeping thing and written down what it did, and the columns were his columns. The figures were his figures. A Reader of the College of Tides, three hundred years and more ago, had measured the draw under the river hill, and laid it out on vellum as Idony laid out the Hearthmere ledgers, and sold it.

*Second: the College shall design and cut the forms for six chains, to be forged by the lord's smiths in the cavern beneath the hill, every link to bear upon the inner curve the rune of holding; which rune shall feed upon that which it holds, so that the harder the held thing strains, the faster it is held.*

He read that twice.

*Third: the College shall give the lord a common charm for many voices, to be said daily at the noon bell by every sworn man of the lord's watch, facing the place. In which charm the words* Keep the watch below *shall feed the holding; and the words* and do not ask *shall be said at its end, so that the voices shall not doubt; doubt being the solvent of all many-voiced working.*

He stopped reading.

He sat at the reading-desk at the foot of the perch with his palm flat on the vellum and didn't move for a long time.

---

*Keep the watch below, and do not ask.*

He'd never heard it said. It was a Hearthmere thing, a knights' thing. He'd read it in a letter once, years ago, from a Hearthmere archivist called Hale, who'd written to the College asking about old oaths, and the College had filed the letter and told Idony not to bother answering. *Keep the watch below, and do not ask.* Their oath. Said at noon by every knight in Hearthmere for three hundred years.

The words were older. The charm was his college's.

Written here. On this vellum. In this hand. Sold, with the chains and the measure, to the lord of the river hill.

*And do not ask.* So the voices wouldn't doubt.

He thought of a roll on a black oak table in Quell's rooms with the shutters closed. Brown vellum, rust-coloured ink, the College of Tides' mark. The form of the great binding. A hundred voices on the Library hill. And a line at the end of it, that the voices said together, that wasn't part of the binding at all. A line that told them not to think about what they were doing.

He'd asked her where it came from. *From the College. It's ours.*

It was the noon oath.

The great binding was the noon oath again. The same form. The same line at the end. A charm for many voices that held something by feeding on it, and kept the voices from asking what they were holding. The College had made it once for the lord of the river hill, three hundred years ago, and sold it, and the lord had used it to hold a sleeping thing in chains under his hill and bleed it for his lamps.

And now the College was going to say it again. A hundred voices. On its own hill. Over its own lake.

---

He read the rest.

*Fourth: the price.* So much in silver, a sum he couldn't imagine. And then: *and a tenth of the light, for so long as the light shall last.* A tenth of the light. A tenth of what the chains bled out of the sleeper, every year, for three hundred years, coming back to the College along the Deep's own lines. He thought of the College's lamps. The soft blue of the College of Tides. That had always burned so steadily, so cheaply, that the other colleges envied it.

*Fifth: the College shall keep this matter outside study, in perpetuity.*

*Outside study.*

*The Hearthmere draw is not a subject for study.*

He thought of Quell at the head of the black oak table in the Hall of Rings, small and white-haired, saying it in her dry-leaf voice as if she were reciting something written down a long time ago. Because she was. Because it was written here. Clause five. Every Master of Tides for three hundred years had known it, and kept it, and told the Readers who came to them with charts of a hole under Hearthmere to stop writing about it.

He'd charted his own college's work for eleven years.

He'd charted it, and they'd told him to stop, and he hadn't, and he'd thought it was because they were small and timid and didn't want to know.

They'd known. They'd always known. They'd made it.

---

"Ferryman," said the Old Reader.

He looked up. He found his face was wet. He didn't remember starting to cry.

"There's more," said the bird. "At the end. Below the seals. Read that."

He looked.

Below the clauses, below the price, below the two seals in faded wax, the College's and the lord's, there was a last piece: an appendix, outside the contract, written in the same Sixth-ring hand, very small, very exact. The working itself.

The rune of holding. Drawn out, stroke by stroke, the way you'd draw it for a Scribe to cut. And beside it, the words. The working-words. The ones you'd say over the rune to wake it, if it slept. The ones that told it what to hold, and how hard.

He read them.

They went into him like a hook going into a fish. He felt them go in. He read them a second time, slowly, every stroke of every word, and they went in further, and he knew, kneeling at the reading-desk at the far end of the Long Library in the white lamplight, that he'd never get them out again.

He didn't write them down.

He didn't need to. He'd read like a Sixth his whole life. He had them. In his head, where nobody could impound them or burn them or take them away.

---

"What will you do with them?" said the Old Reader.

He didn't answer. He didn't know.

The bird looked at him with its one good eye.

"The chain-ends," it said.

"What?"

"The chain-ends. On its legs. You've seen them. You told me. At the Ring, and on the lake. The broken ends of the six chains, still on its forelegs, hanging from the cuffs."

"Yes."

"Every link of them," said the Old Reader, "bears the rune of holding on its inner curve. As the roll says. You've just read it." It shifted on its feet. "The chains broke. The runes didn't. A rune lasts as long as the thing it's cut into. And it feeds on what it holds." A pause. "They are still holding, ferryman. A little. The ends. On its legs. They still say *hold*. And they still feed on what they hold, and it is very large, and it has never stopped straining."

He went cold.

He hadn't thought of that. He'd watched the chain-ends on the forelegs at the Ring for thirty days, through the grey trees, iron, hanging, with the links as thick as his body lying in the grass. He'd thought they were broken. He'd thought they were nothing. Iron.

"Then anyone who can speak to them," he said slowly.

"Has a hand on it," said the Old Reader. "Yes."

He knelt on the cold stone with his palm on the vellum and the working-words in his head and understood what he'd just been given, and why the bird had kept it under its perch for three hundred and four years where nobody would think to look.

---

"Why have you shown me?" he said.

The Old Reader didn't answer for a long time.

When it spoke its voice had changed. Not the dry exact voice, like a quill on good paper. The other one. The one he'd heard once before, on the morning of the breaking, when he'd said *Hearthmere* and it had gone silent and then said, very quietly, *Then the door is open.* A voice like an old man's in the dark.

"Because the door is open," it said. "Because the door-keeper has come, and walked past my window, and lies on the shore of my lake." It looked at him. "And because I am old, ferryman. I am very old. Older than this roll. Older than this hall." It shut its good eye. "I have kept a great many things in a great many places, and I have never once been asked for any of them. And I find I am tired of keeping them by myself."

He didn't know what to say.

"Bring the knight," said the Old Reader. "In two days. Whether the lion will leave the water or not." It opened its eye. "Tell her I asked. Tell her I said please."

---

He put the roll back in its case, and the case back in the niche, and closed the carved panel over it.

The Old Reader got back up on its perch. Slowly. With its beak and its claws, as it had climbed onto the window-sill, and settled there, and shook its ragged feathers once, and was still.

He walked home at the fourth bell.

Down the half-league of the Long Library with every lamp white. Out of the small door. Down the hill in the dark, past the Hall of Rings, past the College of Tides with its lamps burning white instead of their own soft blue. The soft blue that had always been so steady, so cheap, a tenth of the light, for so long as the light should last. He stopped outside the College's door and looked up at it.

Then he went on down.

At Candle Row he stepped off the last white step onto the first brown one, and the lamp over his head went brown, and the hum went out of the cobbles under his boots, and he was in the drain again, where Nessa lived, and Mother Talley's lamp was dark, and a lion lay on the shingle at the end of the old fish-quay facing the thing on the lake.

He went down to the shore before he went home.

He didn't know why. He stood at the top of the old fish-quay in the dark and looked along it to the end, and he could see them. The shape of the lion on the shingle at the water's edge, grey in the starlight. And beside it, small, sitting with her knees up, the knight.

She was awake. He could tell. She was looking at the island.

He didn't go down to her. He stood at the top of the quay with the working-words in his head and the cold coming up off the lake, and watched her watch it, until the sky began to go grey in the east.
