---

He came to the shingle with the bird's message on the hundred-and-thirty-ninth day, and stood twenty paces off as he always did, and said it.

"It said please."

Maren looked up at him.

She'd been sitting on the shingle at the end of the old fish-quay for four days. By day and by night. With Orrun lying beside her at the water's edge, facing the island a mile out, and the cold coming up off the lake, and the lower city's brown lamps behind her. People came to the top of the quay to look at them. Children. Fishermen. Once, a Warden, who stood there an hour and went away. Nobody came down.

The tide-reader came down every day.

"Please," she said.

"I've never heard it say please." He was very pale. He looked, she thought, as if he hadn't slept for a long time, and as if he'd read something that had gone into him and wouldn't come out. "Not in eleven years. Not to anyone. It said tell her I asked, and tell her I said please."

She looked at Orrun.

---

He didn't move that day.

She told him. She didn't need to; he'd heard. He lay with his chin on his paws and his pale eyes on the island, and through the bond she felt him hear it and put it somewhere and not answer.

In the night he got up.

She was asleep, against his side, with her coat over her. She woke because the warmth went. She sat up on the cold shingle in the dark and he was standing, a little way off along the shore, with his back to the lake. Facing the city. Facing the hill, where the Library's windows blazed white at the top of everything.

He stood there a long time.

In the morning, when it was light, he came back and lay down beside her and said:

*I will go up.* A pause. *It asked.*

---

They went up through the city on the hundred-and-fortieth day.

Through the lower city first, in the morning, with the brown lamps still burning in the doorways because nobody had bothered to put them out; they gave so little light it didn't matter. Up Ferry Street, past the stall, past the fish-market, past a dark lamp on a corner that a woman was standing under and looking up at as if it had died. People came out of doors to look. They pulled children back in. A man dropped a basket of fish.

Orrun walked up the middle of the street and didn't look at any of them.

Up the steps to Rope Street. At the top of Rope Street a Warden patrol stopped them. Three Wardens, in grey and blue, with snow lynx at heel. They stood at the head of the street and looked at the lion coming up it, and the lynx flattened their ears, and nobody said anything. Then the Wardens stood aside.

Up to Candle Row.

She felt the line before she saw it.

Through him. He came up the last step onto Candle Row, and the ground under his paws was thin, brown, the lower city's ground, drained, as it had been since the shore. He took one step more.

And the ground was full.

All at once. In one step. Like walking off a cold flagstone onto a hearthstone. Like stepping out of a cold river onto warm sand. It came up through his paws into the bond and into her, full, brimming, humming, and she stopped in the street, and so did he.

*It is full,* said Orrun. *All of it. The whole hill.*

She looked up at the hill. At the Library at the top of it, with its windows blazing.

*It is full,* he said, *and it is drinking.*

---

The Library's great door was high enough.

She hadn't been sure it would be. It was. A great arched doorway in the long grey front of the hall cut into the hill, high and wide, with doors of black oak standing open on iron pins. Built for carts, the tide-reader said. Carts of books, in the old days, when they brought whole libraries up from the other kingdoms to be copied. A lion could walk through it with his head up.

He walked through it with his head down.

Inside, the light. White. Cold. Every lamp burning, at midday, in the long hall, and the light from the tall windows lying across the floor in bars, and the hum coming up through the stone under her boots so loud she could feel it in her teeth.

And the readers.

At the long tables at the door end. A dozen of them. Young and old, in gowns, with books open in front of them and their feet up on the rungs of their chairs. They looked up when the light from the door went dark. They saw what had come in. They stood up, all of them, scraping their chairs, and backed away from the tables toward the shelves. One of them, a young man with a beard, fell over a stool.

Only one didn't move.

An old man at the end of the nearest table, with white hair and ink to his elbows and a pen in his hand. He looked up at the lion. He looked at it for a long moment. Then he looked down at his page again, and dipped his pen, and went on writing.

Maren liked him very much.

---

It was half a league.

She'd known it was half a league. Knowing a number and walking it are different things; the tide-reader had said that to her once, at the Ring, and she'd thought it was the kind of thing a tide-reader would say. It was true. She walked beside the lion down the middle of the Long Library between the shelves, under the vaults, with Idony a pace behind, for half a league, and it went on and on.

Books. More books than she'd known there were in the world. On shelves, floor to vault, on both sides, row after row after row, with ladders on rails, and every so often a long table with lamps, and every so often a reader at it who stood up and backed away.

The hum got louder.

With every step. Coming up through the floor. She felt it through her boots and through him, and she felt him walk slower as it got louder. Slower. His head lower. As if he were walking into a wind.

*It is under here,* he said.

"What is?"

He didn't answer.

At the far end, where the hall went into the hill, there was a perch.

---

The Old Reader sat on it and looked at them come.

She'd seen it from the street, at the window, a dark shape. She saw it now. Black, going grey at the edges, ragged. The size of a big dog. A beak like a cobbler's awl. One eye dark and bright. One milky, like a pearl.

Orrun stopped twenty paces from the perch.

The bird looked at him.

It didn't say anything for a long time. It looked at the lion with its one good eye, and its feathers lifted all down its back, slowly, and stayed lifted. The hum came up through the floor. The white lamps burned. Idony stood very still behind Maren.

Then the Old Reader said:

"Door-keeper."

---

Orrun stopped breathing.

She felt it. Through the bond. Not the stillness of the ground under snow. Not the stillness before he went past her at the beck. Another stillness. As if the word had gone into him and found something, a long way down, that nothing had touched in three hundred years.

*It knows,* he said.

"How?"

He didn't answer. He stood twenty paces from the perch with his head low and his eyes on the bird.

"I know your face," said the Old Reader. Its voice was very dry, very exact, like a quill on good paper. But there was something under it. "Not from life. I'm old, but I'm not as old as that. I know it from a carving on a door that isn't there any more. And from a page that a frightened man cut out of a book." It shifted on its perch. "Come closer. I can't see as well as I used to. Neither can you, I think."

Orrun came closer.

He came to ten paces from the perch, slowly, and stopped, and lay down. Front legs first. Chin on his paws. The way he'd lain at ten paces from his kin on the Ring, and at the water's edge on the shingle.

The Old Reader looked down at him.

"You kept it a long time," it said.

*Not long enough,* said Orrun.

Maren said it aloud. For the bird, and for Idony. She found her voice was strange. "He says: not long enough."

The Old Reader was quiet.

"Nobody keeps anything long enough," it said at last. "That's the trouble with keeping."

---

It asked for the black book.

Maren hadn't told it she had it. She hadn't told Idony. It had been inside her coat since the second day after the river, since she'd written her name in it under Hale's, in plain letters, and taken it out of the archive under the north tower. She'd carried it across the Marches and the Scarfell and the Plains and the Thornwood and the grey. She'd never shown it to anyone.

"The book," said the Old Reader. "The archivist's. You have it in your coat. I can smell the old vellum from here." Its good eye was on her. "May I see it?"

She took it out.

Black, worn, the size of a hymnal, with the corners gone soft. She held it a moment. Then she went forward and put it on the reading-desk at the foot of the perch.

The Old Reader came down.

She'd heard it had. She'd heard the clerk at the gate say it, and the readers at the tables. It came down off the perch onto the desk, slowly, heavily, with its wings half open, and stood over the black book on its own ragged feet, and put its beak to the cover, and opened it.

---

It turned the pages with its beak.

Carefully. One at a time. The list first: *From the closing.* Three hundred and eleven names, each with a door-mark beside it, in a hundred different hands, going back and back. And at the end of the list, the last name, and under it, in plain letters, the name she'd written herself.

The bird stopped at that page. It looked at her name a long time.

*MAREN ASHDOWN.*

It turned the page.

The charter. *Given into the keeping of the crown.* Back. Further back. Into the oldest pages, near the front, where the vellum was brown and soft as cloth and the hands were strange and cramped and old. Hale had shown her these, in the archive, on the night she asked him what was below. Pages about the closing. Then pages that weren't there.

Stubs.

She'd seen them. A row of them, near the front, where pages had been cut out close to the spine with a sharp knife, so that only a finger's width of each was left, a narrow strip of vellum standing up from the binding like the stumps of a hedge after it's been cut. Seven of them. A few letters left at the edge of each, where the writing had come close to the spine. Not enough to read.

On the fourth stub, a drawing. The edge of one. Hale had held a candle to it for her. A great forefoot, four toes, the claws drawn in. And beside it, the end of a word. *…kin.*

The Old Reader looked at the stubs.

Then it laughed.

---

A dry, short sound. Like a page torn.

She'd heard it laugh once already, she thought, without hearing it: the tide-reader had told her, at the sink, that on the morning of the breaking it had laughed for the first time in his memory. She heard it now. It stood on the desk over the black book with its beak an inch from the fourth stub and laughed, once, and then again, and its ragged feathers shook.

"He cut out the cat," it said.

Maren stared at it.

"Who?"

"The first archivist. Your Hale's predecessor, ten times removed." The bird put its beak to the fourth stub, very gently, the way you'd touch a scar. "He cut out the door-keeper. Seven pages. All of them. Every word about who was at the door before the knights came, and what it was, and what was done to it." Its good eye came round to Orrun, lying at ten paces. "Because the crown didn't want anyone to know there had been somebody there first. Somebody at the door, keeping it, before the knights came with their fire and their chains and their oath and called themselves its keepers." It looked back at the stub. "He was frightened. You can tell. Look at the cuts. He did it in a hurry, and he did it badly, and he left a foot."

It laughed again. Quieter.

"He left a foot," it said. "Three hundred years they've been reading that book in the dark under the north tower, and swearing on it, and nobody once asked whose foot."

---

"Do you know what was on them?" said Idony.

He'd come up beside the desk. He was looking at the stubs with his face very close.

"Yes," said the Old Reader.

"How?"

"Because the man who wrote them wrote them twice." It shut the black book's front pages with its beak and turned back toward the middle. "Once in this book. And once in a letter, to a college that had sold his lord a set of chains and wanted to know how they were holding." It looked at Idony. "Your college keeps its letters, ferryman. Even the ones it doesn't want."

Idony went white.

"I'll tell you," said the Old Reader. "Both of you. Not today." It looked at Maren. "When I tell you, write it down. For yourself. And for the farmer."

Maren went still.

She hadn't said anything about the farmer. Not to the bird. Not to the tide-reader, except that once, at the sink, when he'd held the green glass up and said *three together*, and she'd known and not told him.

"How do you know about the farmer?"

The Old Reader looked at her with its good eye.

"Because the door is open," it said, "and three dragons chose one man in a barley field, and I have been waiting for both those things for longer than you'd believe." It turned another page. "Now. There's something at the back of this book that you put there yourself. In charcoal. Show me."

---

She'd copied it on the second night after the river.

Under Greyhold. Down past the black door, past the ledge over the cavern that was half full of still black water now, with the six iron rings in its walls and the stumps of chain hanging from them, and Callis on a door in irons, and Cassian's body on a plank. Along the passage with its walls carved floor to roof in the old letters, dark now, only cuts in stone. To the lock chamber. And the disc in the middle of its floor, the great round of carved stone with the iron boss at its centre, turned a quarter out of true, with a long scar in the floor where it had ground round under a dead man's hand.

Everything else down there had been dark. Every rune, every mark, every lamp. Dead ground.

The lock's sentence had been burning.

White. In the stone. The rings of hooked letters that had lined up from rim to boss when the disc turned, lit from inside, steady, as they'd been lit at midnight. Still burning, two days after, on dead ground, when nothing else in the kingdom would. She'd sat cross-legged on the cold floor beside it with the black book on her knee and copied it, stroke by stroke, with a stub of charcoal Ada had given her, without knowing what any of it said.

She'd asked Orrun. He couldn't read it either. But he'd looked at it a long time and said: *Something is shut there. Like a breath held.*

She turned to the back of the book and laid it open on the desk in front of the bird.

---

The Old Reader read it.

She watched it read. It stood over the page with its head on one side and its good eye very close to the charcoal marks, her marks, clumsy, smudged, copied by someone who couldn't read a word of them. It read the first line. And the second.

It stopped laughing.

It had stopped already. Now it went still. All of it. Its feathers lifted down its back, slowly, all of them, until it stood on the desk in the white lamplight with every ragged feather on end, like a bird in a frost. And they didn't settle. It read the third line, and the fourth, and they didn't settle, and it went on reading.

Idony leaned in beside it.

She saw him read it too. He could read the script; she knew that. He read it, and his face changed. Not the way it had changed at the green roots by Orrun's flank, the slow light coming up from underneath. The other way. Puzzled. Then less puzzled. Then something else.

"It's a binding," he said, slowly. "Of two. Two... somethings. Two hands, or two wills, or two..." He stopped. "It's in a form I've never seen. Not in the College. Not in any commentary. Not even in the..." He stopped again, and looked at the bird, and didn't finish.

The Old Reader didn't look at him. It was looking at the page.

"What does it say?" said Maren.

The bird didn't answer.

"What does it say?"

---

The Old Reader lifted its head from the page and looked at her.

For a moment she thought it was afraid. She didn't know why she thought that. It was a bird, and old, and it had laughed at a dead archivist's knife-work a moment ago. But something was in its face, round the beak, round the good eye, that she'd seen in faces before. In Hale's, in the archive, on the night he told her what was below. In Pell's, in the gateway, at the end.

"One word," it said.

"What?"

"I'll give you one word of it. That's all of it you can bear yet." It looked down at the charcoal marks. "That's all of it anyone can bear yet. Including me."

She waited.

"*Together,*" said the Old Reader.

---

She stood at the desk with her hand flat beside the black book.

"Together," she said.

"Yes."

"What does that mean?"

"It means what it says." The bird's feathers were still up. "The lock was made for two hands. Two keys. Two locks, one under the school and one under the bridge, turned in the same breath. So no one person could ever open it alone." It looked at her. "You know that. You were there when one of them turned."

"Under a dead man's hand."

"Yes. Made for two." The bird's good eye didn't leave her. "So was the other thing."

"What other thing?"

It didn't answer. It looked at her a long time with its good eye, and then it shut the black book with its beak, very gently, as you'd close a door on someone sleeping, and pushed it across the desk toward her.

"Keep it," it said. "Keep it in your coat. Don't show anyone else what's in the back." It turned round on the desk. "And don't let anyone read it aloud. Not even him." It meant Idony. "Not even me."

---

It got back up on its perch. Slowly, with its beak and its claws. It settled there. Its feathers came down at last, one by one, and lay flat.

Orrun hadn't moved.

He lay at ten paces from the perch with his chin on his paws, and through the bond she felt him, enormous and still, thinking: a slow turning, like stones in a riverbed. *Door-keeper.* He hadn't heard it in three hundred years. She didn't know if he'd ever heard it. She didn't know if anyone had ever called him anything.

*It knows what was at the door,* he said.

"Yes."

*Before the fire.* A pause. *I don't remember. It was taken out of me. Like the names at the Barrows.* Another pause. *It knows.*

"It'll tell you," said Maren. "It said."

*Yes,* said Orrun. *It said not today.*

---

She went to the window at dusk.

There was a window at the hill end of the hall, by the perch, the last of the tall narrow ones cut in the hillside. It looked south-east, across the city, over the white-lit towers of the hill and the brown-lit ones below, across the lake with the island lying on it, to the mountains.

The Iron Fells. Running away south-east along the edge of the sky, grey and steep, with snow along their tops even in high summer. White, in the last of the light. Going pink.

She stood at the window and looked at them because there was nothing else to look at, and her eyes were tired of white lamps.

Something moved against the snow.

Far off. Very small. A shape, against the white of the Fells' tops, moving. A bird, she thought. An eagle. It was too far to see. It moved north along the line of the ridge, steady, fast, and as it turned a little the last of the sun caught it, and it was green.

She put her hand on the stone of the window.

It went north along the Fells. Small. Green. A long way off. A shape she'd seen over the Fellwood, crying with joy. Over the Plains, coming down out of the noon. Over the dead trees at the Ring, afraid.

Then it wasn't there.

Gone behind a ridge. Or down, into a valley. Or just too far. She didn't know. She stood at the window and looked at the place on the snow where it had been until the pink went out of the Fells and they were only grey.

Idony had come up beside her.

"Was that...?" he said.

"Yes," said Maren.

He didn't ask anything else. He stood beside her at the window in the white lamplight, with the bird asleep on its perch behind them and the lion lying at ten paces and the hum coming up through the floor, and they both looked at the Iron Fells going dark, where something green had gone north, and then not.
