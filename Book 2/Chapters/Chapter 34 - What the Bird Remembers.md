---

Orrun went back to the shingle after the first day, and lay there, and she went up to the Library every second day alone.

The bird talked to her. Not about the thing. About everything else. About the shelves, and which kingdom's books were on which, and how the Sarovel books smelled of the oil they used on their bindings and the Kessmoor books of iron filings. About the readers at the long tables, and which of them were frauds. About Idony's citations, which it said were the best in the College and the worst-written, as if he'd learned to spell from a ferry timetable. It asked her about the river watch. It asked her about Greyhold's yard, and the straw men, and what Pell had taught. It asked her what the black book weighed.

It didn't tell her anything.

She didn't push it. She'd learned that from the lion.

On the hundred-and-fifty-first day it said: "Tomorrow. Bring the door-keeper. Bring the ferryman. Bring something to write on." It looked at her with its good eye. "I'm going to tell it once, and I'm old, and I won't tell it twice."

That night, on the shingle, she told Orrun.

*Yes,* he said. *I will come up.*

---

Idony brought paper. A whole quire of it, thick, cream-coloured, the good College paper he said he wasn't supposed to have, and a pencil. A stick of grey stuff in a cedar case, sharpened to a point.

"Kessmoor graphite," he said. "From the Fells. Charcoal smudges." He gave it to her as if it were a gift, which she supposed it was. "You'll want it not to smudge."

She sat at the reading-desk at the foot of the perch with the quire in front of her and the pencil in her hand. Orrun lay at ten paces. Idony sat on the floor with his back against the shelves and his knees up, the way Wren sat.

The white lamps burned. The hum came up through the floor.

The Old Reader sat on its perch and looked at the three of them.

"I'll tell it plainly," it said. "I'm telling it from a letter. The College's copy. Written by the first archivist of the knights' house, three hundred and six years ago, after he'd cut it out of his book." It shifted its feet. "He wrote like a man trying not to be frightened. All flourishes. I'll leave them out. You can imagine them."

It shut its good eye for a moment.

Then it began.

---

"There was no kingdom," it said. "That's the first thing. Three hundred and six years ago, where your Hearthmere is, there was march-land. Border country. A river, and hills, and lords on the hills who fought each other over the fords. No king. No crown. No knights.

"One of the lords held a hill above a bend in the river. A good hill. Good stone in it. He had quarrymen cutting it, for his walls.

"One spring the quarrymen cut into the hill and the hill opened.

"Not much. A crack. A chamber behind the face they were cutting, with a floor of dressed stone and walls carved with marks they couldn't read. A shrine, the archivist calls it, because he didn't know what else to call it. And in the floor of it, steps. Going down."

---

Maren wrote.

She wrote in the plain square letters of the river watch, the ones you painted on a boat's bow. *No kingdom. March-land. A lord on a hill. Quarrymen. A shrine. Steps going down.* She wasn't fast. She'd never had to be. She wrote what she could, and when she couldn't keep up the bird waited, as if it knew.

---

"The lord went down," said the Old Reader. "Himself, with torches and two men. Down the steps, a long way, under the hill, under the river. The archivist went with him. He was young then. He was the lord's clerk.

"At the bottom there was a cavern. Long. Under the river, from one bank to the other. Dry, then. The river hadn't found its way in.

"And in the cavern, lying down, asleep, a beast the size of a hill."

The hum came up through the floor.

"Grey," said the Old Reader. "Lion-kin. With wings folded along its sides. With its head on its paws. Breathing. So slowly that the archivist stood beside it for the time it takes to say a prayer before it breathed once." It paused. "He says it was the most beautiful thing he'd ever seen. He says it in the letter, in the middle of all the flourishes, very plainly, as if it had got out before he could stop it. *It was the most beautiful thing I have ever seen.*"

Maren's pencil had stopped.

She thought of the carving on the wall under the Ring. The lion lying down with its head on its forepaws and its wings folded and its eyes shut. Asleep. With a tall shape of light standing over it with its hand held out.

*That is how it was,* Orrun had said. *Asleep.*

She wrote it. *It was the most beautiful thing I have ever seen.*

---

"In front of it," said the Old Reader, "there was a plinth. A drum of stone, like the one you found under the Ring. And on the plinth, two things.

"An egg. Grey stone. Smooth. The size of a cart-wheel's hub. Warm to the touch, the archivist says, warm as a loaf.

"And a ring. Of the same stone. As wide as a man's two hands. With six hollows cut in it, round its face, like the sockets of a crown that's lost its jewels."

Idony, on the floor, had gone very still.

"The lord didn't know what they were," said the bird. "Nobody did. He didn't know what the beast was. But he could feel it. Everybody who went down could. The archivist says the air down there was so thick with it you could hardly breathe. Like standing in a bread oven. Like standing in the sun. The lord could feel that he was standing on the heart of something. And he was a border lord with a hill and a quarry and not enough men, and he could feel it, and he wanted it."

---

"So he wrote to the College of Tides," said the Old Reader.

Idony made a small sound.

"Because the College measured the Deep. Everyone knew that, even then. And the lord could feel that this was the Deep, or the heart of it, lying under his hill. He wanted to know what it was, and how much, and whether it could be had." The bird's good eye went to Idony. "And the College came. A Master, and two Readers. They went down. They measured it. You've seen the roll, ferryman. You've seen the table."

"Yes," said Idony. His voice was very quiet.

"And they told the lord what it was," said the Old Reader. "A sleeper. One of the old great ones, put to sleep at the end of the one realm by the ones who were older still. And they told him what the egg was."

It stopped.

"What was it?" said Maren.

The Old Reader looked at her.

"The sleep," it said. "The sleep was in the egg. The College's Master told the lord so, and the archivist wrote it down. The sleep is held in it, he said, as a flame is held in a lantern. While the lantern's whole, the flame burns, and the beast sleeps. Break the lantern, and the flame goes out." The bird was quiet. "And the beast wakes."

---

She had stopped writing.

She sat at the desk with the pencil in her hand and looked at the Old Reader and didn't write anything.

*A stone. Grey. Round. The size of a cart-wheel's hub.* In the thing's jaws, at the Ring, as it lifted its head out of the opened ground to the stars. And a hollow in a plinth, egg-shaped, smooth, warm as a bed when someone's just got up.

And then the stag.

"At the Ring," said Idony, from the floor.

His voice had gone strange.

"The thing it took," he said. "Out of the plinth. Under the stones. That was..."

"Yes," said the Old Reader.

"And then the stag woke."

"Yes."

"Because it took the..." He stopped. "Because it broke the lantern."

"It didn't break it," said the bird. "It took it. It took it away. Which comes to the same thing, for the one asleep beside it." It shifted on its perch. "A lantern carried away is no use to the room it was lit for."

---

"Then it's not taking them by accident," said Maren.

She heard herself say it. Her voice was quite steady.

The Old Reader looked at her for a long time with its one good eye.

"No," it said. "It isn't."

"It knows."

"It knows exactly what it's taking," said the Old Reader. "It lay asleep under that river with one of them on a plinth in front of its face for a thousand years before a border lord broke it. It knows what they are better than any of us. It knows what's in them." It paused. "You saw it at the Ring. It didn't wander. It flew straight there, the morning after it came up out of your river, and lay down on the stones, and waited till they were full, and went down for it."

"And now it's here," said Idony. "On the lake. On the hill."

"Yes."

"And there's one under the hill."

The bird didn't answer that. It didn't need to.

The hum came up through the floor.

Nobody said the next thing. Maren felt it in the room, sitting at the reading-desk with the pencil in her hand. The question. *Why?* Why would a thing that had been asleep, and woken, and chained, and bled, and freed, go from kingdom to kingdom taking the lanterns away, one after another, so that the ones still asleep would wake?

Nobody asked it. She didn't. Idony didn't. Orrun, at ten paces, lay with his chin on his paws, and through the bond she felt him not ask it either. As if all three of them had come to the edge of something in the dark and stopped, and none of them wanted to be the one to put a foot out.

The Old Reader didn't offer.

---

"He broke it," said the bird, after a while, and its voice was dry again. "The lord. On the College's advice. In the cavern, with a hammer, with the archivist holding the torch.

"And it woke.

"And before it knew what it was, or where, or who had done it, the chains were on it."

Maren wrote. She made herself.

"The College had sent the forms," said the Old Reader. "You've read them, ferryman. Six chains. Forged in the cavern by the lord's smiths over a whole winter, while the beast slept and the egg sat whole on the plinth in front of it. Every link with the rune on its inner curve. Staked to the old stone at both ends of the cavern. Waiting." It shut its good eye. "The archivist says it took a long time to wake. Days. He says it lifted its head, after the egg broke, very slowly, the way you lift your head from a pillow when you don't know where you are. And looked round. And the chains were already on it."

"It didn't fight," said Idony.

"Not then. It didn't know there was anything to fight." The bird opened its eye. "By the time it did, the runes had had a day to drink, and every time it pulled, they drank more."

---

"The ring," said Idony. "The other thing on the plinth. The ring with six hollows."

"Yes."

"What happened to it?"

The Old Reader looked at him for a long moment.

"The archivist doesn't say," it said. "He says it was there on the plinth when the lord broke the egg. He says that after, when the chains were on, they looked for it, and it wasn't." A pause. "Nobody knows where it went. Not the lord. Not the College. Not I."

Idony looked at the bird a long time. Maren thought he didn't believe it. She didn't know if she did.

"Write that down too," said the Old Reader to her. "Exactly. *Nobody knows where it went.*"

She wrote it.

---

"There were words," said the Old Reader. "Over the arch. At the bridge."

Maren looked up.

"Not here. At the bridge across the river, the old one, the one the old realm built, where the white tower stood on the island. Over the arch of the bridge, carved, in the old script, there was a verse. The old ones' charge to whoever kept the door." It looked at Orrun. "You know it, door-keeper. You lay under it for three hundred years."

*Yes,* said Orrun.

Maren said it aloud for Idony. "He says yes."

She knew the end of it. Everyone at Greyhold knew it; she'd said it at the noon bell every day for two years, facing the inner wall with the rest of the yard. *Keep the watch below, and do not ask.* And the farmer had told her, on Liss's floor, the first night, where the words had come from: an arch on an island, and an old dragon who'd read them there when she could still read, and given him the whole verse from memory.

"The lord's men took the words," said the Old Reader. "For their oath. *Keep the watch below.* It was the old ones' charge to the door-keeper. A guardian's charge. They made it a jailer's." It looked at Idony. "And your College took the last line too. It was a warning, on the arch. *Do not ask.* The College made it a muzzle."

"So the voices wouldn't doubt," said Idony.

"So the voices wouldn't doubt."

---

"There was a cat at the door," said the Old Reader.

It said it quite plainly. Not looking at the lion. Looking at the paper in front of Maren.

"At the bridge. At the white tower, on the island, where the door was. A great grey cat. Bigger than any cat in any book. It had lain on the tower steps for longer than any song anybody could sing. People who crossed the bridge bowed to it as they went by. The archivist had done it himself, as a boy." The bird paused. "He says in the letter that he bowed to it and it looked at him, and he was so frightened he couldn't sleep for a week, and so happy he didn't want to."

Orrun stood up.

---

Maren felt him go. Through the bond. Not into the dark, not down the well. Up. Out of the place where he'd been lying for three hundred years, at ten paces, at the edge of a lake, by a door. He got up off the floor of the Long Library, slowly, and stood, with his head low, and the hum came up through the stone round him.

"When the lord's men came," said the Old Reader, "with the chains, to take them down the steps through the door, the cat on the tower steps stood up."

Orrun was very still.

"It fought them," said the bird. "The archivist says. It fought them on the steps, and on the bridge, and at the door. The stones of the bridge came up under the men's feet. The tower shook. He says a man was crushed under a step that rose up out of the bridge like a wave." It paused. "They drove it off with fire. Pitch, on poles. Torches. A hundred men with fire on poles, on the bridge, driving it back along the bridge and off the end of it. And it went." The bird looked at Orrun at last. "And it didn't come back for a long time. And when it did, the door was shut, and the knights were on the other side of it, and the words over the arch had become an oath."

---

She felt it.

Not memory. He didn't have it; she knew that. It had been taken out of him, like the names at the Barrows, one stone at a time from a wall, by three hundred years of waiting. Not words. Not pictures.

Heat.

It came up through the bond into her, all at once, so hard she put her hand flat on the desk. Heat. On stone. Fire on steps. A smell, thick, black, choking: pitch. Men. A great many men, and poles, and the ends of the poles burning. And pain.

Along his flank. The left side. Low, under the ribs.

She knew it. She'd seen it every day since the island. An old scar, long, ragged, where the fur grew wrong, under the iron-grey of his left flank. She'd thought it was from the hunters on the island, from the stake on the spit where they'd chained him, from the collar. She'd never asked.

It wasn't from the hunters.

*Fire,* said Orrun.

His voice in her head was very slow and very deep, like stones turning a long way down in a riverbed.

*On the steps.* A long pause. *I remember the fire.*

Then nothing more. She felt him reach for more and not find it. A wall with the stones taken out. He stood in the Long Library with his head low and his flank against the old scar, and the heat went out of the bond slowly, like a coal going grey, and he lay down again.

Maren wrote it.

Her hand wasn't steady. The graphite went crooked across the paper. She wrote it anyway. *A cat on the tower steps. It fought them. They drove it off with fire. Orrun remembers the fire.*

---

"There was a man at the door too," said the Old Reader. "A warden of the bridge. The archivist names him."

Maren looked up.

"Ennis," said the bird.

The pencil stopped.

She knew the name. She didn't know how she knew it. Then she did: the farmer. On Liss's floor. Talking about the old dragon, about what she'd remembered under the arch, about a man whose face had come back to her after three hundred years. *Ennis. A warden of the bridge. She said his name and then she was quiet.*

"He stood at the door," said the Old Reader, "with his dragon. A bronze. Old even then. And when the cat had been driven off with fire, he stood in the door with the bronze behind him and wouldn't let them by." It paused. "He was one man and one dragon against a hundred men and a lord. He knew what was down the steps. He'd been keeping it with the cat. He wouldn't let them by."

"What did they do?"

"They smoked her," said the Old Reader.

Maren went cold.

"The bronze," said the bird. "Three days. With pots. The lord had binders, even then. Hearthmere's first knights were binders before they were knights; nobody tells you that. They smoked her three days on the bridge until she was opened. And then they did a thing the archivist had never seen done and never wanted to see again." It looked at Idony. "He says they took the witness out of the beast."

"What does that mean?" said Maren.

"It means," said Idony slowly, from the floor, "that they cut the man out of her. Out of her bond. While he was still alive." He was white. "So she'd never be able to tell anyone. What she'd seen. So she'd forget him."

"Yes," said the Old Reader. "And then they killed him. On the bridge. At the door." It was quiet a long time. "And the bronze went north, the archivist says, opened and empty and not knowing why, and nobody saw her again."

---

"Somebody did," said the Old Reader.

It had shifted on its perch. Its voice had changed: drier, and quicker, as when it talked about citations.

"Not the archivist. Others. Later. The Library has the Clan's songs, the ones the Clan's skalds sang at the Roosts and a College reader once took down in a winter there. And it has the College's letters from the Clan, which aren't many." It looked at Maren. "There was a bronze. An old one. Sitting on a cliff above the Clan's hold, alone, unbonded, older than anyone could remember, when the Clan's first chief came to the Scarfell. A hundred years after the bridge. Sitting there as if she'd been waiting."

Maren didn't breathe.

"She chose a man of the Clan, Ragnald Holm, and was his for the rest of his life. And he died, in a rockfall. And she lay down on the shore of the Clan's black tarn and didn't eat for a year." The bird's good eye was on her. "And didn't follow him."

"What does that mean?"

"A dragon whose partner dies goes with him," said the Old Reader. "Almost always. Down the bond, the way water goes down a weir. A breath. Less. You've seen it. Or the farmer has."

She thought of the farmer in a barley field in the dark, with three bonds tearing round him. She hadn't seen it. She'd felt Orrun's grief under the river, once, and known what it would be to have nothing at the other end of it.

"She didn't follow," said the bird. "Because there'd been a place in her, once, where a man was cut out. And it had scarred. And a scar holds harder than skin." It paused. "She chose Sigrun Holm. Ragnald's great-niece. Fifty-nine years. And Sigrun died, in a barley field, in a war. And the bronze didn't follow her either."

It shut its eye.

"Three partners, outlived," said the Old Reader. "Ennis, and Ragnald, and Sigrun. A dragon almost never survives one."

---

"And now," said the bird, "a fourth."

It opened its eye and looked at Maren.

"Shared three ways. With two others. A red, and a green. In a barley farmer, in his own field, on the night her third partner died." It shifted its feet on the black oak. "Do you know how many times in the Clan's songs three dragons have chosen one person? Or two? Or a dragon who'd lost her partner that same hour has chosen anyone at all?"

"No."

"Neither do I," said the Old Reader. "Because it isn't in them. Not once."

The hum came up through the floor.

"Write this," it said. "Exactly. For the farmer."

She held the pencil.

"*Ask your farmer whether he has wondered why her,*" said the Old Reader. "*Why him.*"

She wrote it. Every word. In plain square letters, very carefully, so he'd be able to read it, if he'd learned. She thought he'd learned. The green dragon had said, at the Ring: his own name, and one of the letters backwards.

She looked at what she'd written for a long time.

*Why her. Why him.*

---

Idony showed the bird his drawings at the end.

He'd brought them. The roll of oiled silk from under the Ring, with the carvings copied on it in charcoal and ink: the six sleepers, each with its line of marks under it, *sleep, sleep, sleep*. And the tall shapes over them. Standing. Faceless. Taller than the beasts, going up the curve of the wall, with one hand held out over each sleeping head and the other going up toward the middle, toward the thing in the dome that had fallen.

He unrolled it on the reading-desk next to Maren's paper and stood back.

The Old Reader came down off its perch to look.

It stood on the desk over the silk a long time. It looked at the sleepers first: the stag, the serpent, the plated one, the horned one, the great hump, the lion. It touched the lion with its beak, very gently, as it had touched the stub in the black book.

Then it looked at the tall shapes.

It didn't say anything for so long that Idony shifted his feet.

"What are they?" he said.

The Old Reader looked at the faceless shapes of light with their hands held out over the six sleepers.

"The Unbound," it said.

---

Idony went white.

Maren saw it. She didn't know why. She didn't know the word. She'd heard it once, in the farmer's verse: *where the old ones walked unbound.* She looked at the tide-reader and he'd gone the colour of the paper.

"That's the seventh ring," he said.

The bird didn't answer.

"The College's seventh ring." He was talking to Maren now, quickly, as if he had to say it to somebody. "There are seven rings. Hand, Reader, Scribe, Fellow, Master, Keeper of the Script. And a seventh. In the grammar. On the last page. It's called the Unbound. Nobody's ever held it. Nobody ever will. It's for..." He stopped. "It's for working the Deep with nothing between. Old magic. Without runes or words or anything. The grammar says it belongs to..." He stopped again.

"To what?" said Maren.

"It doesn't say." He looked at the bird. "It just says nobody's ever worn a seventh notch. It's always been a joke. *When you get your seventh notch.* It's what they say to first-years who think too much of themselves."

The Old Reader looked at him with its good eye.

"Yes," it said. "It's a joke."

It got back up on its perch. It didn't say anything else about it. Idony asked it twice, and it looked at him, and didn't answer, and after the second time he stopped asking.

---

Maren wrote it down anyway. *The Unbound.* At the bottom of the last page, by itself.

She'd filled eleven pages. Both sides. In plain square letters, in Kessmoor graphite, that didn't smudge. No kingdom. A lord on a hill. Quarrymen. Steps going down. The most beautiful thing I have ever seen. An egg and a ring. The sleep in the egg, like a flame in a lantern. Break the lantern. The chains already on it. Nobody knows where the ring went. A cat on the tower steps. Fire. Orrun remembers the fire. Ennis. They took the witness out of the beast. Three partners, outlived. A fourth, shared three ways. *Ask your farmer whether he has wondered why her. Why him.*

She folded them, all eleven, and put them inside her coat, with the black book.

For the farmer. To give him when their roads crossed.

She didn't know when that would be. She didn't know if it would be. She put them in her coat anyway.

---

Idony had gone to the window.

The hill-end window, by the perch. It was nearly dark, and the lamps had come up white along the hall behind him. He'd gone there out of habit, she thought, as he did everything, with a green glass in his hand, and he was standing with it held up to the last of the light.

He turned round.

"There are dragons coming over the Fells," he said.

Maren got up.

"Two," said Idony. He looked out of the window again, into the dark, and then down at the glass in his hand. He frowned at it as he'd frowned at the top of the forty steps under the Ring. "Two. On the glass. Coming over, from the south-east, toward the peaks. Close together."

She went to the window and stood beside him. She couldn't see anything. The dark, and the line of the Fells a little darker under it.

"The glass says three," said Idony slowly. "It's always said three. Three together, since the spring." He turned it in his fingers. "Two of them are coming over the Fells. And the third one..." He tilted the glass. "The third is a long way south. Still. Very still."

He looked at her.

"What is it?" he said. "You know. You've known since the Ring. I saw your face."

She looked out at the dark.

Two dragons coming over. A red, and a green. And a third, a long way south, on a ledge, with a crooked wing, very still.

"A barley farmer," said Maren.
