---

He climbed the six hundred steps on the hundred-and-fifty-third day, and counted them.

He hadn't meant to count. He'd started at the bottom, at dawn, walking past the guardhouse at the foot of the peak with his hood up and his bandaged hands in his sleeves, a pilgrim, one of the people who climbed Starwatch to see the stars from the top. And by the twentieth step he'd found he was counting, under his breath, the way he counted sheep through a gate. He didn't stop.

The steps were cut in the rock. Up the south face of the peak, the highest in this end of the Iron Fells, in a long zigzag, each step a hand deep and a stride wide and worn hollow in the middle by six hundred years of feet. An iron rail on the outside, cold, rusted red where the hands had worn it. Below the rail, nothing. Air, and then a long way down, the screes and the snow and the corries.

Kharrak and Lisska were in one of the corries, on the far side of the peak. Out of sight. He'd asked them to be.

*I could carry you up,* Kharrak had said. *In a breath.*

"Then they'd know what I was."

*They'll know anyway.*

"Not yet," Calder had said, and started walking.

---

At the four-hundredth step he could see Glasswater.

He stopped. He held the rail with his bandaged hand and stood on the step with the wind coming up the face of the peak into his face and looked north-west, down the long valleys between the mountains, a day off.

A lake. Long, narrow, blue, lying in a fold of the mountains with snow round it. A city on its north shore, climbing a hill; he could see the shape of it, the towers, small as pins. The hill. He'd seen it lit white at night from the ridge over the bowl. By day it was grey, like the rest. But even by day, from here, he thought the windows at the top of the hill were bright. Too bright.

And on the lake, lying on the water, grey, the size of an island, with its wings folded along its sides, the thing that had come up out of the river under the Old Ruin on the morning after the locks and gone into the sunrise.

He hadn't seen it since.

He stood on the four-hundredth step and looked at it for a long time.

He wasn't here for it. He was here for four young dragons in a bowl of smoke. He was here for a library and a bird and a paper. But he stood on the step and looked at it lying on the lake a day off, and thought of the morning after the locks, standing in the reeds below Redwatch with the old one dying beside him and the knights in a line on the bank and the lion-woman beside the lion. All of them, looking up. The thing going into the sunrise.

*It's lying down,* said Lisska, from the corrie, very small at the back of his head.

"Yes."

*It's lying on the water like a duck.*

He nearly laughed. He went on up.

---

The tower was on the very top.

Squat. Round. Black stone, the same as the peak, as if it had grown out of it. With a dome on it of iron plates, riveted, rusted, and a slot down the middle of the dome where the plates could open. A door in the side. A bell on a bracket by the door, and a rope.

He was out of breath. Six hundred and four steps; the last four were up to the door. He stood in front of it with his hands on his knees and the wind trying to take him off the mountain, and rang the bell.

A girl opened it.

Sixteen, perhaps. Thin, in a long grey woollen gown with ink on the cuffs and her hair tied up in a cloth, and a sharp face, and eyes that went over him once from his boots to his hood, quickly, as Wren's did.

"Pilgrim?" she said.

"Yes."

"You're early. Nobody comes up at this hour. They come at dusk, for the stars." She looked at his hands in their strips of shirt. "What happened to your hands?"

"I burned them."

"On what?"

"A boy," said Calder.

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she stood aside.

---

Her name was Mira, and her grandfather was the Master of Starwatch, and he'd been looking at the sky for sixty years.

Master Halvard Esk. Fifth ring, the girl said, as if that meant something, and Calder nodded as if it did. Small, old, with a white beard down to his belt that he tucked into the belt so it wouldn't get in the way, and a squint in his right eye so bad that he had to turn his head sideways to look at you with his left. Sixty years at the eyepiece, Mira said. The right eye was the one he looked through.

He was in the dome.

Calder had never seen anything like it. The whole of the inside of the tower was one room, round, going up to the iron dome, and in the middle of it, on an iron cradle as big as a hay-wain, there was a tube. Brass. Green with age. As long as a barn. It pointed up at the slot in the dome, which was shut, at an angle, and at the bottom end of it there was a little brass cup you put your eye to, and at the top end, Mira said, a glass. A great round glass, the size of a cartwheel, ground by Kessmoor glassmen two hundred years ago, the only one like it in the world.

"The great lens," said Master Esk, without turning round. He was standing at the bottom end of the tube with a cloth, polishing the brass cup. "It sees what eyes can't. That's all. No magic in it. Kessmoor glass and Kessmoor brass and a great deal of patience." He turned his head sideways to look at Calder past the squint. "You've come up for the stars."

"Yes."

"You've come up at dawn."

"Yes."

"Hm," said the old man, and went back to his polishing.

---

He stayed that day, and the next.

They let him. Pilgrims stayed sometimes, Mira said; there was a room for them, a cell in the tower's wall with a cot and a blanket and a window the size of a book. People came up to see the stars and stayed a night or two because the stars were worth it. He slept in the cell. He ate their bread and their goat's cheese, which they had from the shielings below the same as he'd had from Tasso's mother. There was a third one who lived there, a silent man in a grey gown with a Third ring on his thumb who did the arithmetic, Mira said, and never spoke, and Calder never heard him say a word.

He didn't know why he stayed. He'd come up to see the city. To see Vael, and the hill, and the library on it, and the valley the hunters would come down when the hill burned all through. He'd seen it. From the four-hundredth step. He could have gone.

He stayed.

On the second evening, the hundred-and-fifty-fourth, Mira saw the marks.

---

He'd been washing. In a basin in the pilgrim's cell, with the window open, in the evening, with his shirt off and his collar open, because the wind had dropped and the air was warm for once. And Mira had come to the door of the cell with a candle to say the stars were out and her grandfather was opening the dome, and stopped in the doorway, and looked at his chest.

Three marks.

He'd had them since the barley field. He didn't think about them any more. One under the other, on the left side of his chest, over his heart: bronze, rust, and green. Not tattoos. Not scars. Under the skin, the way a bruise is, only they never faded. The old one's bronze at the top. Kharrak's rust in the middle. Lisska's green at the bottom.

Mira stood in the doorway with the candle and looked at them.

She didn't say anything. She went very white. Then she turned round and went away down the stair fast, and he heard her calling her grandfather, and her grandfather's voice answering, cross, and then not cross.

When Master Esk came to the door of the cell, he didn't look at Calder's face. He looked at his chest. With his head turned sideways, very close.

"Three," he said.

"Yes."

"Three dragons."

"Yes."

The old man straightened up. He stood in the doorway of the cell, small, with his beard tucked into his belt, and looked at Calder at last.

"Come with me," he said. "Not up. Down."

---

There was a room under the tower.

Cut into the peak, under the dome, down a stair inside the wall. A long low room, with a vaulted roof of black stone, lit by lamps. Ordinary lamps. Not white. Calder noticed that. They burned the ordinary yellow of oil-lamps, the way lamps burned anywhere.

And books.

Not the way the bird's library was full of books, the way he'd imagined it: shelves and shelves going on for ever. Here there were three walls of shelves, and on them, in rows, the same book, over and over. Tall, thin, bound in grey, with numbers on their spines. Three hundred of them, Mira said. More.

"The tables," said Master Esk. "The star-tables. Every night's sky, from this tower, for six hundred years." He put his hand on the shelf. "Where every star was. Where the wanderers were. What the moon did. And before that," he went to the end of the room, where there was a smaller shelf of older books, in darker bindings, "copies. Of copies. Of tables from before there was a tower. From before there were colleges."

He took one down. Brown. Soft. So old the binding was coming away in flakes.

"From before," he said, "anybody knows."

---

He opened it on a table under a lamp and turned the pages, with his head sideways.

Calder stood beside him.

The pages were columns. Rows and rows of them, in the hooked script, tiny, faded. Numbers, he thought. He could see they were numbers, though they weren't the numbers he knew. Rows of figures down the pages, and at the top of each column a mark, and every so often in the margin a little drawing: a star, a moon, a ring.

The old man stopped.

He put his finger on a line. Not on the page; just above it, not touching, as you'd point at something you were afraid would come off on your hand.

"There," he said.

---

It was a line in the table like any other line. Calder couldn't read it.

"The Ferrymen," said Master Esk. "Three wandering stars. We call them that, in Isvaren. One red, one green, one pale gold, like a coin. They wander across the sky on their own roads, each at its own pace, and you can see them most nights, one or two. But all three, together, in the same place, standing in the Hunter's belt," he moved his finger along the line, "once in about a thousand years."

He moved his finger to the margin.

There was a drawing there.

Small. In ink gone brown and faint. A figure: a man, standing, drawn as a child draws a man, a round head and a body and arms and legs. And on the man's chest, on the left side, one under the other, three small marks.

Under the drawing, a line of the hooked script.

Calder looked at it.

He couldn't read the hooked script. Nobody could but the scholars. But he looked at it and something went through him, cold, from the top of his head to his feet, like the cold off Coldtarn's black water in the dead of winter.

"What does it say?" he said.

The old man read it, slowly, with his head sideways and his nose nearly on the page.

"*One comes with three,*" said Master Esk.

---

Mira wrote it for him.

On a slip of the tower's paper, in the common letters, under a copy of the hooked line that she drew very carefully, stroke by stroke. She gave it to him at the table, and he held it under the lamp and looked at it.

He could read three of the words himself.

He did. Slowly. With his bandaged finger under the line, the way his mother did, the way Wren did, the way he'd seen Arn do by the east fire with his brother's name under his finger. Saying each letter in his head.

O. N. E.

W. I. T. H.

T. H. R. E. E.

The other one he had to ask. *Comes.* Mira said it, and he looked at it, and said the letters after her.

*ONE COMES WITH THREE.*

He looked at the drawing in the old book. At the man drawn like a child draws a man, with three marks on his chest. It didn't look like him. It didn't look like anybody. It was a round head and a body and three marks.

It was nobody.

---

"When was it?" he said.

Master Esk looked at him sideways.

"The conjunction. The three together. When does it come?"

The old man turned the page. The next column. He put his finger above it.

"Here," he said. "The last time. Before ours." He moved his finger down the column. "The end of the one realm. When the old ones went into the light. That's what this copy says; the copy before it said the same; the one before that said the same." He turned back. "And before that, here, as far back as this table goes. Another thousand years. And another."

"Before ours," said Calder.

The old man didn't answer for a moment.

"I worked it out," he said. "Forty years ago. From the tables. Every wanderer's road, every night, six hundred years of them, and the old copies for further back. I worked out when the three would stand together in the Hunter's belt again." He looked at the drawing. "Last autumn."

Calder didn't move.

"I watched it," said Master Esk. "From the dome. With the plates open and the great lens on it. All three. Red and green and pale gold, standing together in the Hunter's belt, so close you could have covered them with your thumb." His voice had gone strange. "I've looked at the sky for sixty years. I've never seen anything so beautiful. Mira was with me. She cried."

"I did not," said Mira.

"What night?" said Calder.

The old man told him.

The night of the harvest's last full moon. A night in the autumn. He gave the date in the College's count, and Calder didn't know the College's count, and Mira worked it out for him on the back of the slip with a pencil, and said the day in the Marches' count.

It was the night of the mill.

---

He sat down.

He didn't decide to. There was a stool at the table and he sat on it, with the slip of paper in his bandaged hands.

The night of the mill.

The barley field. In the dark, after the harvest. The Clan's war-pairs fighting over the Fenn field, Sigrun and Dag and Imra, three dragons and three riders in the air over his own barley, and the crossbow in the hedge at the bottom of the field. *Thock.* He'd heard it lying in the stubble with his ribs stove in. Dag going off Kharrak's back. Sigrun dying. Imra dying. Three bonds tearing, all at once, in the dark.

And three dragons coming down out of the sky into the barley, one after another, round the man lying in it. The bronze. The red. The green.

He hadn't looked up. He'd been lying on his back in the stubble. He might have been looking up. He couldn't remember. He'd been looking at the dragons.

Above them, in the Hunter's belt, three wandering stars. Red and green and pale gold. Standing together for the first time in a thousand years.

---

"What does it mean?" he said.

Master Esk shook his head.

"I don't know." He closed the old book, carefully, so the flakes of binding wouldn't come off. "Nobody knows. It's a line in a table. A copy of a copy. Somebody a thousand years ago, or two thousand, or more, sat at the eyepiece of a lens much worse than mine and saw the three stand together, and drew a man in the margin with three marks on his chest, and wrote *one comes with three.*" He put his hand flat on the cover. "That's all. We don't know who drew it. We don't know what they meant. We copy it every time we copy the tables, because it's there, and the rule is you copy everything." He looked at Calder sideways. "I've wondered about it for forty years."

"So have I," said Calder.

He didn't know he was going to say it. The old man looked at him.

"Not about that," said Calder. "Not about stars. About why." He looked at the slip of paper. "Why me. Why three. Why a barley farmer in his own field. Why her, the old one, who's had three partners and outlived them all and then came down to me. Why any of it." He stopped. "Nobody ever told me. I asked. Nobody knew."

"No," said Master Esk.

"And it's a drawing in a book."

"Yes."

Mira was standing on the other side of the table, very straight, with her ink-stained cuffs and her sharp face.

"But somebody knew it was coming," she said.

They both looked at her.

"A thousand years ago," said Mira. "Somebody looked up and saw the three and knew. Not what it meant. That it was coming. That when the three stood together, one would come with three." She looked at Calder's chest, where his shirt was still open. "They didn't know who. They didn't know why. But they knew it would happen. And it did."

Nobody said anything.

"That's not nothing," said Mira.

---

The Wardens came up the steps on the morning of the hundred-and-fifty-fifth day.

Two of them, and the sabrecat. From the guardhouse at the foot of the peak, where they kept the road. Calder heard the bell ring and went to the top of the stair and looked down into the dome, and Mira had opened the door, and there they were. A man and a woman in grey and blue with their sleeve-wards burning steadily on their left arms, and between them, on a short chain, a cat.

He'd seen sabrecats on the Fells. From the air, on ledges. He'd never seen one this close. It stood in the door of the dome on its four great legs, heavier than a bear, grey and tawny, with its short tail lashing and its two fangs hanging down past its jaw, longer than his forearm. It looked up the stair at him with yellow eyes.

"The bonded man," said the woman Warden. She had a paper in her hand. "With three dragons. Reported at Starwatch by the pass-watch on the hundred-and-fifty-second day." She read from the paper. "By order of the Blue College, under the law of the colleges: to be brought down to Vael for study, and registered as wild power." She looked up at him. "You'll come with us."

"No," said Calder.

---

The woman looked at him.

"It's the law," she said. "Every bond-gift is wild power. Every bonded person in Isvaren's borders must be registered. You've three bonds. Three. The College has never had one bonded to three." She held out the paper as if he could read it. "They want to study you."

"No."

"We have orders."

"You can have them," said Calder. "I'm not coming."

The man Warden put his hand on the sabrecat's chain. The cat's tail stopped lashing.

"Enough," said Master Esk.

He'd come out from behind the great lens. Small, old, with his beard tucked into his belt and his head turned sideways to look at the Wardens. He stood in the middle of the dome between them and the stair.

"This tower is the colleges'," he said. "These steps are the colleges'. That lens is the colleges'. I've kept them for the colleges for sixty years." He didn't raise his voice. "And I'll not have the colleges' business done on my roof by two Wardens and a cat." He pointed at the door. "If the Blue College wants him, it can send a Master to ask. Politely. Up six hundred steps."

The woman Warden looked at the old man.

Then she folded the paper and put it in her coat.

"We'll send for more," she said.

"Send," said Master Esk.

They went down. The sabrecat went last, and turned on the top step, and looked up at Calder once more with its yellow eyes, and went.

---

*I could take the roof off,* said Kharrak.

He'd felt it all. From the corrie, through the bond. The paper. The cat. *We'll send for more.*

"Don't hurt anyone."

*I didn't say hurt.* A pause. *I said the roof.*

Calder stood at the slot in the dome, where the iron plates had been opened a crack for the morning air, and looked down the face of the peak at the guardhouse at the bottom. A stone hut with a slate roof, small from up here, at the foot of the six hundred steps, with a thread of smoke coming out of its chimney. Two Wardens going down the steps toward it, and a cat. Halfway.

He thought of more. More Wardens. A Master. A paper with *study* on it. A register, with his name in it, and three dragons in a column.

He thought of four young dragons in a trench of smoke.

"Wait till they're in it," he said. "Then wait till they're out of it. Then the roof. Only the roof."

*Only the roof,* said Kharrak. He sounded delighted.

---

He did it at noon.

Calder watched from the slot in the dome with Mira beside him and her grandfather behind, at the eyepiece, which he'd turned down the mountain specially, and which he said was a terrible waste of a lens.

The red dragon came over the peak from the corrie on the far side. Low. Fast. With the sun on him. Down the south face, past the six hundred steps, past the tower, over the screes, so close to the rock that his shadow went down it like water. And came down on the guardhouse.

On it. On the roof. With his forefeet. He landed on the slate roof of the stone hut and stood there, enormous, red, his wings half open, and roared.

The door of the guardhouse burst open. The two Wardens came out of it at a run. The sabrecat came out after them, faster, and went past them, and kept going, up the screes, its tail straight up.

Kharrak waited until they were thirty paces off.

Then he took the roof off.

In four blows. Calder counted. With his forefeet and his tail. The first blow took the ridge. The second took the slates off the near side, all at once, in a long grey slide that went down the mountain like a scree. The third took the other side. The fourth, with his tail, took the rafters, and threw them, all of them, in a bundle, out over the edge of the screes and down, and they went end over end down the face of the peak and were gone.

He stood on the walls of the roofless guardhouse with the smoke still coming out of the chimney, which he'd left, and looked up the mountain at the tower.

*Not one hurt,* he said. *Not one. Not even the cat.*

"You did well," said Calder.

*I know,* said Kharrak.

He waited.

Calder didn't say it again. He was laughing too hard. Mira was laughing beside him, with her hands over her mouth. Master Esk, at the eyepiece, wasn't laughing, but his beard had come out of his belt.

*Say it twice,* said Kharrak.

Calder couldn't. He was holding on to the iron plate of the dome.

The red dragon sulked all afternoon.

---

Master Esk let him look through the lens at dusk, because Mira asked him to.

"Once," said the old man. "Since they'll come back with a Master tomorrow and you'll be gone. Once." He turned the great brass tube on its iron cradle, slowly, with a crank, so that it swung round and down, away from the sky, toward the north-west. Toward Vael. "This lens is for stars. Not for cities. It's a waste of it." He squinted along the tube. "There. Put your eye to it. Don't touch the brass."

Calder put his eye to the little brass cup.

---

He nearly stepped back.

It was there. All of it. So close he could have reached out and touched it. A city, on a hill. Towers of grey-blue slate. Bridges between them, roofed. Streets. People in the streets, small, moving. And at the top of the hill, a long grey front of stone cut into the hillside, with a row of tall narrow windows, every one of them blazing white in the dusk.

And a great door. Arched. Open.

In the door, coming out of it into the evening, a lion.

Grey. Enormous. With a mane like an iron-grey cloud. Walking out of the door of the library at the top of the hill slowly, with its head low. And beside it, at its shoulder, small, in a dark coat, with her hand on its neck, a woman.

He didn't breathe.

He watched them come out of the door and stand at the top of the hill in the dusk, for a moment, looking out. South-east. Toward the mountains. Toward him, though she couldn't know. The lion lifted its head. The woman stood with her hand on its neck and looked at the Fells going pink along their tops.

Then they turned, and went down the hill, out of the lens.

---

He stood with his eye to the brass cup for a long time after they'd gone.

"Well?" said Master Esk.

He took his eye away.

"The library," he said. "The big one. At the top of the hill."

"The Long Library. Yes. The oldest in Winshire." The old man cranked the tube back up toward the sky. "With an old bird in it, they say, that talks. I've never been down. Sixty years, and I've never been down."

"There's a lion at its door," said Calder.

Master Esk stopped cranking.

He looked at Calder sideways for a long moment. Then he went back to the crank.

"Then you'd better be quick," he said, "when you go down. And go before the Master comes up."

---

He left on the hundred-and-fifty-sixth day, at dawn, by air.

Mira gave him the slip of paper at the door of the tower. The one she'd written: the hooked line, and under it, in the common letters, ONE COMES WITH THREE. And on the back, the night it had happened in the Marches' count, in her small sharp hand. And under that, the drawing. She'd copied it. The round head and the body and the three marks.

"So you can look at it," she said.

He put it in his coat. With the red book and Rook's book. With the knife and the iron ring and the folded bill and the tin charm and the iron collar with six dots round the inside.

And the stone. Small, flat, grey, with a white ring round it.

He touched it in his coat. He'd started doing that.

Kharrak came over the top of the peak from the corrie and landed on the bare rock by the tower door so heavily that the iron plates of the dome rang, and stood there, red, enormous, in the dawn, and Mira looked up at him with her mouth open and didn't say anything at all.

Master Esk came to the door behind her. He looked at the red dragon with his head sideways, very still.

"Hm," he said.

Then he went back in to his lens.

Calder climbed onto Kharrak's neck. Lisska came round the peak from the corrie and fell in behind, and they went off the top of Starwatch in one great drop, and the six hundred steps went by under them in a breath, and the roofless guardhouse at the bottom, with two Wardens standing in it looking up.

He looked north-west once, toward the lake and the hill and the library on it, and the lion at its door.

Then he turned the red dragon east, back along the Fells, to the ridge over the bowl, to lie in the snow and watch the smoke, and wait for the hill to burn all through.
