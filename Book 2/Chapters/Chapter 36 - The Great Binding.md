The line came down to the water in the second week, and the lower city went dark.

He watched it from the attic every night. Candle Row on the hundred-and-thirty-first day; Lamp Street on the hundred-and-forty-fifth; the fish-market on the hundred-and-fifty-ninth. A street a week and then faster, two streets, three, as if the hill were filling faster the fuller it got. On the hundred-and-sixty-fifth night he looked out of the attic window and the line was at the water's edge. There was no line any more. There was the hill, white, from the top to the bottom of it, every lamp burning so cold and bright that the towers threw shadows in the night. And below it, along the shore, the lower city.

Dark.

Dark. Every lamp in the lower city out, or so near out that it was a red ember in a glass. Mother Talley's corner. The Dyers' College. The stall at the bottom of Ferry Street. The Tide-hall annex with its soft blue. All of it. People had candles in their windows, tallow, as they had in Hearthmere now, and in the Wyldmarch. He could see them from the attic, small and yellow and ordinary, along the shore.

He wrote it down. *Day 165. The cup is the whole hill. The drain is the whole shore.*

---

The ice came on the hundred-and-sixty-sixth day.

At dawn. In late summer. He was at the ferry steps, because he went to the ferry steps every morning now to look at the island, and there was a skin on the lake.

A mile out. Round the island. Thin as a window pane, grey-white, lying on the water in a great ring round the thing that breathed. He could see it catch the first light. A ring of ice, on Glasswater, in the last week of summer, with the air warm on his face and the bees going in the window-boxes behind him.

By noon it had gone. By the next dawn it was back, wider.

By the hundred-and-seventieth day it reached the ferry steps.

He stood on the bottom step and put his boot on it. It held. He pressed. It cracked, with a sound like a cup breaking, and under it the water was black and so cold it hurt to look at.

The ferries broke it with poles to cross. He watched Nessa do it, standing in the prow of the *Heron* with a boathook, breaking the ice ahead of the boat in a long grey channel, cursing, and Tib at the sheet, and the lantern owl in its cage staring at the island. She threw the salt over the prow before she broke the ice. Every crossing.

It wasn't the Titan's cold. He knew that. It came up from under it. From the deep glass's place. From whatever was down there, under the water, under the island, breathing in.

---

He found the Undercroft on the hundred-and-sixtieth day, by measuring.

He'd been measuring the hill for a month. With the field-glass, on every floor of every college he was allowed into and some he wasn't. The Hall of Rings. The College of Tides. The Library's halls, all of them, from the great door to the bird's perch. Every one full. Every glass he set down ran up to the top of its throat and pressed there, trembling.

All but one place.

The lowest hall. Under the hill end of the Library, below the bird's perch, down a stair behind the last shelves that the librarians kept locked and he'd never once been down. He got the key from an under-librarian who owed him a citation. A long low hall cut in the rock, empty except for dust, with a floor of old stone. Not the College's stone. Older. Dressed so close you couldn't see the joins. The kind under the Ring.

And in the middle of the floor, a door.

Lying flat. A great round of carved stone, set into the floor, with hooked marks cut round its edge in rings. The librarians called it the Undercroft door. Nobody had opened it in living memory. Nobody knew if it opened at all.

He set the field-glass on it.

The quicksilver went up the throat so fast he didn't see it go. It hit the top. And the glass cracked. A fine white line, up the throat from the bulb, and a bead of quicksilver came out of the crack and ran down the outside of the glass and onto the carved stone and lay there, trembling, like a drop of water on a hot stove.

He sat on the floor of the lowest hall in the dark for a long time.

It was under here. Whatever the Titan had come to Glasswater for. The thing on the plinth. The lantern with the flame in it. Under the Undercroft door, under the Library, under the hill.

It was drinking the hill to reach what was under it.

---

He came up the stair with the cracked glass in his hand.

The lamps in the hall were white and there was nobody under them. The readers had gone a week ago, when the hum in the lowest halls got into their teeth. Chairs pushed in. Inkwells capped. At the hill end the perch of black oak, and on it, the Old Reader, with its head under its wing.

It took the head out when he was still twenty paces off.

"Ferryman," it said. "You have been under my floor."

"Yes."

He put the glass on the reading-desk. The crack ran up its throat from the bulb like a hair in an egg. As he set it down, a second bead came out of the crack and lay on the wood and trembled, and then it rolled, slowly, toward the rock.

The bird watched it go with its good eye.

"Did you know?" he said. "What's under you."

"I have sat on it a long time."

"That isn't an answer."

"It is half of one. Sitting on a thing is not knowing it." The feathers lifted along its back and settled. "It is, however, a start. Most of this hall never got so far."

He sat down at the desk. His desk. The scratches on its lid were his, eleven years of a dividers' point. The bird's dish stood at the corner. Dry. A crust in it gone hard as slate.

He looked at it.

Nobody had come up with scraps. Not since the readers went. The juniors had brought them twice a day for as long as anyone remembered, and nobody had thought about who'd bring them when the juniors didn't.

He had smoked whitefish in his coat. Nessa had pushed it at him that morning at the steps, because he hadn't eaten, she said, since the ice. He unwrapped it and put it in the dish. All of it.

The Old Reader looked at the dish. It didn't come down.

"Seven days," it said. "Footnote: the hum makes junior readers remember they have mothers in the country. I don't blame them. I make a note of it."

"You make a note of everything."

"I make a note of everyone. That is different." It shifted its feet on the black oak. "Every reader who sat at that desk. What they read. What they got wrong. Which is most of what remembering is, ferryman, if you do it honestly. A list of corrections."

He laughed. He hadn't meant to. The sound went off down the empty hall between the shelves.

"What did I get wrong?" he said.

The bird thought about it. It thought about everything, slowly, as if every question were a book it had to send down to the stacks for.

"Your Hearthmere ledgers," it said. "Eleven years. Read low every night. The commentaries said the glasses were faulty, and the Masters said so, and nobody above the Third ring opened a page." It looked at him. "I opened them."

He sat very still.

"Every page," said the Old Reader. "Your sevens are untidy. You correct in the margin and not on the line, as a ferryman's boy would, so the next one can see where the shallows were. I approve of that. I approve of very little." Its head went on one side. "You got nothing wrong, as it turns out. You only wrote it down for eleven years with nobody to read it."

"You read it."

"I read everything, ferryman. It's no compliment." It closed its good eye. "Keeping a thing is easy. You put it somewhere dark and you sit on it. Reading it is the work."

The quicksilver had reached the edge of the desk. It hung there, a bead of it, against the lip of the wood.

"You could go," he said. "When it comes. Out of the window. You still could."

"I could." The eye opened. "Read me today's numbers. My eye is old and your hand is worse than it was in spring."

He took the silk out of his coat and read them. The field-glass on every floor, the lean, the height up the throat, all of it, in order, slowly, as his father had called the depths off the ferry's bow, coming in to the steps in fog. Three fathom. Two. Two and a half. Ground.

The bird didn't correct one.

When he'd finished it said, "Go home and sleep." And then, after a pause, "Leave the dish."

He left it. He went down the hall with the lamps white over the empty tables. At the twentieth shelf he heard, behind him, the black oak creak as something leaned down from it, and the dish move on the wood.

He didn't look round. Nobody had ever seen it eat.

---

He wrote it in the silk on the hundred-and-sixty-eighth day.

His third reading. The Ring had shown him the stir; the Moot had shown him what came after it; and now this. He wrote it the way he'd written *a waking is a stir, a flood, and then a famine*: in the smallest hand he had, first, before anything else.

*When the cup is fullest, the beast goes down for what is under it. Then the flood. Then, out of the flood, the sleeper.*

He looked at it. He did the arithmetic beside it.

At the Ring, the stir had run ninety days, and the relic had gone at the peak, on midsummer night, and the surge had run seven days, and the stag had stood up out of the forest.

Here the slope was steeper. The cup was filling faster. The glasses would peak, he thought, on the hundred-and-seventy-second day. Near noon. And then the beast would go down for what was under the Undercroft door. And then the surge. And then, out of the surge, whatever was under the lake, breathing in.

Five days, he wrote. Perhaps fewer.

He took it to the Council.

---

They set the working for noon on the hundred-and-seventy-second day.

Quell set it. In the Hall of Rings, with the light coming down onto the black oak table like water into a well. He gave her his reading and she listened, and when he'd finished she said, in her dry-leaf voice:

"Noon, then."

"Near noon, Master. The glasses can't..."

"Noon," said Quell. "The form was made for the noon bell. It'll be said at the noon bell."

He looked at her.

The noon bell. *Every voice at the noon bell is a hand on the chain.* He'd read it in Hale's letter, years ago, the one the College had filed. A knights' saying. The noon oath. Said every day for three hundred years at the noon bell, facing the inner wall, by men who didn't know what they were saying or who'd written it for them.

And now the College was going to say it again, on its own hill, at the noon bell.

He thought somebody at the table would see it. Dane, perhaps. The masters of the Blue College and the Scribes. He waited for somebody to see it and laugh.

Nobody laughed.

---

He went to Nessa on the morning of the hundred-and-seventy-second day.

He went before anything else, before the hill, before the ring. Down through the dark lower city in the grey light, down Ferry Street past the shuttered stall, past Mother Talley's dead lamp, to the steps.

The ice was on the lake from the island to the steps. Grey-white. With the ferries' channels broken through it, black, freezing over again at the edges. And the island lying in the middle, a mile out, breathing.

Nessa was in the *Heron* at the foot of the steps, chipping ice off the gunwale with a hammer.

"Get off the water," he said.

She looked up.

"Today. Now. All of you. The ferry-folk and the fish-market and the dyers and everyone on the shore." He heard his own voice, and it sounded like somebody else's, somebody who said things to people's faces. "Get them up the hill. Above Candle Row, at least. By dusk. And keep them there."

"How long?"

"Five days."

She put the hammer down.

"Don't cross," he said. "Don't take the boat out. Don't sleep on the water, don't sleep on the shore, don't let anybody go down to the steps to look." He looked at the ice. At the island. "It's going to take what it came for. Today. At noon. And then the lake's going to do something."

"What?"

"I don't know," said Idony. "Something under it. In five days. Perhaps fewer."

Nessa looked at the ice. At the channels the ferries had broken through it, black. At the island. At the lantern owl in its cage on the prow, which had turned on its perch and was staring at the island, as it had stared for fifty-six days, and hadn't slept.

"Five days," she said.

"Five. Perhaps fewer."

She didn't argue. She'd never argued with him about the lake. She'd argued with him about colleges, and rings, and eating, and his coat, and the whole of their lives. Never about the lake.

She got out of the boat and climbed the steps and turned round to where Tib was sitting on a bollard with a line, and said:

"Start with Mother Talley. She'll fight you. Carry her if you have to."

---

The ring was on the terrace in front of the Hall of Rings.

The crown of the hill. A great paved space between the Hall of Rings and the Library's upper door, where the colleges held their processions, two hundred paces across. Old stone, under the College's paving: the Undercroft's stone, the same as the floor in the lowest hall. The College had paved over it two hundred years ago and nobody had thought about it since. It hummed. He could feel it through his boots from the edge of the terrace.

A hundred magisters stood on it in a ring.

All seven colleges. Grey gowns with their colours at the collar: the Blue College's blue, the Scribes' black, the Tides' soft green, the violet of the Measures, the red of the Healers, the gold of the Sky-watchers, the white of the Keepers of the Script. Fifth rings and Fourth rings, and the best of the Thirds. Old men and old women mostly. Some young. A hundred of them, standing in a ring two hundred paces across on the old stone of the terrace, an arm's length apart.

Barefoot.

Dane's order. The commentaries said a drawer must stand on the stone itself, flesh to stone, for a many-voice working fed from it. So they stood there in the noon light with their gowns to their ankles and their bare feet on the humming paving, and he saw an old Master of the Healers at the west point shift from foot to foot as if the stone were hot.

Quell at the north point. Small, white, with her five-and-a-half-notch ring on her hand.

Dane at the south. Bandaged to the wrists still. Standing very straight.

The very old Keeper of the Script from the Sixth-ring house in a chair at the east point, because he couldn't stand, with his student beside him holding his hand.

And in the middle of the ring, on the old stone, alone, the reader.

---

He had four glasses on a stand. The three green ones that had come through the sink, and one he'd cut for the hill. And the College's great sand-glass, an hour-glass as tall as his arm in a brass frame, to keep the voices in time.

He stood in the centre of a hundred magisters with his bare feet on the humming stone and watched the quicksilver climb.

Every lamp on the hill was white. At noon, in the sun. He could see them in every window of every tower round the terrace, burning so bright they paled the daylight. The Library's tall windows. The Hall of Rings' ring of windows under its dome. All of them white.

Below the hill, the lower city, dark. Above Candle Row, crowds. He'd seen them coming up the streets all morning: the ferry-folk, the fish-wives, the dyers, carrying bundles, carrying children. Mother Talley in a chair on two poles with Tib at one end. Nessa had done it. He'd known she would.

On the lake, the island, lying in the ice. Breathing.

He watched the glasses.

---

They stopped.

Stopped. All four, at the same moment. The quicksilver in the throats, that had been climbing for fifty-six days, stopped at the very top, and trembled there, and didn't move.

The peak.

He'd been told to call it. He'd agreed to. He'd stood in Quell's rooms with the shutters closed and been told: *you'll stand at its centre and call the hour.* It was the Council's use for him. Until the Council judged him.

He looked at the glasses. He looked at the island.

"Now," said Idony Vael.

---

A hundred voices began.

All at once. On the word. A hundred magisters on the old stone saying the same words at the same moment in the old tongue, low, steady, and the sound of it went up off the terrace into the noon like smoke going up from a hundred chimneys on a still day. He'd never heard a many-voice working. Nobody living had. It was like a choir. It was like the sea. It was like a hundred people breathing in.

He knew the words. He'd read them once on Quell's roll and twice on the bird's. *Keep the watch below.* In the old tongue, over and over, round and round, and through it, woven, the hold-words. The ones the bird had shown him in the appendix of the contract, that had gone into him like a hook into a fish. He heard a hundred people say them, and felt them go into the stone.

The stone answered.

Under his bare feet. The hum. It rose. Not louder: tighter. As if it were being drawn up, out of the paving, into the voices, into the ring. He felt it go past him, up through the soles of his feet, out of the old stone, into a hundred people standing on it.

Every lamp on the hill went whiter.

---

He felt it go out over the lake.

A pressure. Going out from the ring on the hill over the dark lower city, over the shore, over the ice, toward the island. Like a held breath. Like the weight in a room before a storm. He felt it in his ears, a pressing, and in his chest, and the four glasses on their stand in front of him didn't move.

On the lake, the island stopped breathing.

He was counting. He always counted. Eleven in. A pause. Nineteen out. Fifty-six days of it. He counted the in-breath, and it came, eleven, and then the pause, and then the out-breath didn't come.

The island lay on the ice with its head on its paws.

He couldn't see its eyes from here. He knew what they'd look like. He'd seen it at the Ring, under the lamps' white net, in the grey: blank. Nobody behind them. Held.

One breath.

---

The voices came to the end of the form.

He'd known they would. He'd read it. The last line. The one that wasn't part of the binding at all. The one that told the voices not to think about what they were doing.

A hundred magisters said it together, in the old tongue, on the hill of the College that had written it three hundred years ago and sold it to a border lord.

*And do not ask.*

He heard it. A hundred mouths. The noon oath's last line, said at the noon bell. He stood in the middle of the ring with his bare feet on the old stone and heard every one of them say it, and believe it, and not ask.

He stepped out of the ring.

---

He didn't decide to. Afterwards he thought he'd decided it a long time before, on the floor of the lowest hall with a cracked glass in his hand, or on the shingle with the lion, or at the reading-desk at the foot of the perch with his palm on the vellum. But he didn't feel himself decide. He felt his feet move. Off the stand. Across the old stone. Away from the centre.

Toward the edge.

Between two Fourth rings at the south-east of the ring. An old woman of the Measures in violet and a young man of the Scribes in black, saying the words with their eyes shut. He walked between them. They didn't break off. They didn't open their eyes.

Somebody shouted. A proctor, at the edge of the terrace. "Reader Vael!"

He kept walking.

He didn't run until he was past the Hall of Rings. Then he ran.

---

He was on Rope Street when the voices broke.

He heard it behind him, up the hill. The sound changed. The steady low many-voiced breath of a hundred people saying the same words went ragged, and then it wasn't words. It was screaming.

He stopped. He turned round.

He couldn't see the terrace from Rope Street. He could see the hill. And every lamp on it, every white lamp in every window of every tower from the crown of the hill to the shore, went out.

All at once. Like a hand closing.

He knew what it was before he understood it. He felt it in his feet. Through the cobbles. The hum, that had been under everything in Vael for fifty-six days, under the Library and the Hall of Rings and the College of Tides and the terrace, the full cup, brimming: gone.

Not faded. Gone. In one draw.

The island on the lake had breathed in.

---

He understood it later. Sitting on the steps of Candle Row in the dark with the cobbles still trembling under him, he worked it out as he worked everything out, slowly, a step at a time, like a man crossing ice.

The binding held it for one breath. One. The hundred voices drawing on the full stone of the hill and pressing it out over the lake, and the island held in it, not breathing. One breath.

And then it breathed in.

And it breathed in the hill. All of it. The whole cup, the whole brim-full hill that it had been filling for fifty-six days, in one draw, like water at the end of a long day. It took the Deep out of the stone of the hill in one breath. Out of the terrace. Out from under the bare feet of a hundred magisters who were drawing on that stone at that moment, as hard as they could, with all their voices.

And the stone was empty.

And they were still drawing.

On themselves.

---

Then the ice broke.

He heard it from Rope Street. A crack. Not like a cup breaking. Like the whole world's ice-house breaking at once, a sound so huge that it went through him from the soles of his feet to the top of his head and the windows on Rope Street shivered in their frames. Out on the lake, from the island outward, the ice went. Cracking in long lines, spreading, the whole grey-white skin of Glasswater from the island to the steps splitting into plates and lifting and turning over.

The island rose.

Out of the ice. Its wings opening, wider than the lake, grey, stone and leather, with sheets of ice sliding off them and falling back into the black water. Its head lifting off its paws. Its legs coming up under it, and the broken chain-ends on its forelegs swinging, each link as thick as his body. It stood on the lake. It stood on the water as a hill would stand, if a hill could, with the ice breaking round it.

Then it came across.

---

Three wingbeats. He counted.

A mile of water. Three. The wings went down, and the air came across the lake in a wave that he felt on Rope Street a quarter of a league off, and the wings went down again, and it was over the shore. Over the dark lower city, the dark towers, the dark streets, so low that its shadow went over the whole of the lower city at once. And the wings went down a third time.

It came down on the hill.

On the Blue College's side. The east side of the crown, where the Blue tower stood, tall, slender, grey-blue slate with its covered bridge to the Hall of Rings, the tallest tower in Vael.

He saw the forefoot come down.

On the tower. Not on purpose, he thought. It was there. It was where the forefoot came down. The Blue tower went under it as a reed goes under a boot. Down. All of it. The slate and the stone and the covered bridge, folding, falling, with a sound like a cliff coming down into the sea, and a cloud of grey dust going up off the crown of the hill into the noon.

Then it put its head down.

Into the hill. Between its forepaws. Into the crown of the hill where the terrace was, and the Library's upper door, the way it had put its head down into the ring of stones in the Thornwood on midsummer night. And he felt, through the cobbles of Rope Street, the old stone under the hill answer it.

The hill opened.

---

He was on Ferry Street when it came up again.

He'd run. Down. Away from it. Toward the shore, toward the steps, toward the ferry-folk and Nessa, because that was where he'd been going when he left the ring and his feet were still going there. He came out at the bottom of Ferry Street by the shuttered stall, and turned, and looked up.

It was lifting its head out of the hill.

Out of the crown, out of the dust, out of the broken place where the terrace had been and the Library's upper door. Slowly. The way it had at the Ring. And in its jaws, small against it, grey, round, a stone.

He couldn't see it well. He didn't need to. He'd seen the hollow in the plinth under the Ring. He knew the size of it. A cart-wheel's hub.

It held it up to the noon sky.

Then it turned, on the hill, slowly, with the dust going off its back in sheets and the broken chain-ends swinging, and spread its wings, and went back out over the lower city, over the shore, over the broken ice, the way it had come. Three wingbeats.

It lay down on the lake again. In the middle, where it had lain. In the broken ice. With the stone in its jaws.

And put its head down on its paws.

---

The ferry-folk were moving.

Nessa had done it. The shore was empty. The fish-market empty, the dyers' yards empty, the steps empty. The ferries were up on the slips, all of them, the whole Glasswater fleet, drawn up the stone ramps on rollers above the tideline and turned over, keel-up, like a shore full of turtles. The *Heron* among them, black, upturned, with the lantern owl's cage lashed to her keel.

Nessa was at the top of the slips with a rope in her hand, shouting at somebody.

He went up to her. She looked at him.

"You left," she said.

He didn't know how she knew. She always knew.

"Yes."

"Good," said Nessa, and gave him the other end of the rope.

---

He helped carry Mother Talley up Rope Street.

She'd come back down. She'd been taken up the hill in the morning in her chair on two poles, and when the Blue tower fell she'd got out of the chair and walked down Rope Street to see to her lamp, because she'd had it forty years and her father before her, and Tib had gone after her, and now she was sitting on the cobbles at the corner of the fish-market under her dark lamp and wouldn't move.

"It's out," she said, when they found her. "It went out last week. It's out."

"I know," said Idony.

"It'll not come back."

"Not for a long time."

She looked up at him. A very old woman, with a face like a walnut and her hands in her lap.

"Your mother threw salt," she said. "Every crossing. I used to laugh at her."

He and Tib and a fish-wife carried her up Rope Street in the chair, and she sat in it with her hands in her lap and didn't say anything else.

---

The surge came up through the hill at dusk.

He was on Candle Row, on the steps, with the crowds from the lower city round him, sitting on their bundles, waiting, in the dark. No lamps anywhere. The hill above them dark and broken, with dust still going up from where the Blue tower had been. The lower city below them dark. The lake beyond, dark, with the broken ice on it and the island lying in the middle.

Then every lamp in Vael lit at once.

All of them. Not just the hill. The lower city too. Every lamp that had been dead for weeks, every lamp in every window and over every door, Mother Talley's at the corner of the fish-market, the Dyers' College's green, the Tide-hall annex's soft blue, the lamps on the hill that had gone out at noon: all at once, white. So bright the crowd on Candle Row cried out and covered their eyes. So bright that the whole city stood up out of the dark round the lake like a hand of cards held up to a fire.

The ground heaved.

Under Candle Row. Under him. The steps lifted and dropped. A child screamed. Somebody's bundle went bouncing down the steps.

He'd brought the deep glass from the attic in his coat. He took it out. In the white glare he could see it. The quicksilver in its throat went up so fast it hit the top with a crack he heard, and the glass broke in his hand, and the quicksilver ran out over his fingers, cold, colder than the lake, and dripped on the step.

And the cold came off the lake.

Not a haze any more. A wind. A great cold wind coming off Glasswater up the hill through the lower city, through the streets, up Rope Street, over Candle Row, so cold it took his breath, so cold the white lamps frosted over in their glasses. He felt it on his face. It smelled of deep water and ice and something very old.

It came from under the island. From under the lake. From the place the deep glass had been cut for, where something had been breathing in for fifty-six days, and holding it.

The surge, he thought. And under the surge, the sleeper.

Five days. Perhaps fewer.

---

He looked down to the shore.

He looked past the crowds on the steps, down over the white-lit lower city, to the shore. To the old fish-quay, sinking, with its stones rocking. To the shingle beyond the end of it, at the water's edge, where a lion had lain for thirty-six days facing the island.

The lion was standing.

In the white glare of every lamp in Vael. At the edge of the lake, on the shingle, with the cold wind coming off the water into its mane. Standing, with its head down. Low. To the stones. The way he'd seen it stand at the Ring, at ten paces from the head, before it lay down.

And the shingle round it was opening.

He could see it from Candle Row. The stones of the shore cracking outward from where the lion stood. In lines. Spreading. Like ice round a stone thrown onto a frozen pond, the cracks going out from it in every direction, along the shore, up toward the old fish-quay, out toward the water. The ground opening round it.

Beside it, small, in a dark coat, a woman. Standing with her hand on its neck.

He watched them from the steps of Candle Row in the white glare, with the cold wind in his face and the quicksilver drying on his fingers and the island lying on the broken lake beyond them with a stone in its jaws, until Nessa came and put her hand on his arm and said his name twice before he heard her.
