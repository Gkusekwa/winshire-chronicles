The cracks ran out from him in every direction, and he didn't move.

Maren stood with her hand on his neck and felt them go. Through him. Through his feet, into the shingle, through the stones under the shingle, down. A listening, not the pushing she felt when he opened stone. He had his head down so low that his chin was on the stones, and his whole body was still in a way she'd only felt once before, on the night at the lock, with the rock humming and the thing below breathing out.

He was listening down. Under the shore. Under the lake.

In the white glare of every lamp in Vael, with the cold wind off the water in her face, she listened with him.

She felt it. Very far down. Something larger than a breath. A turning. Like a weight moving in a deep place. Slow. So slow that it might have been going on for a hundred years and she was only now close enough to feel it.

"It is turning over," said Orrun, in her. "In its sleep. Like a child before morning."

"What is it?"

He was quiet for a long time. The cracks in the shingle stopped spreading.

"Cold," he said. "Long. Very old. It is the one on the wall with frost for scales."

She remembered the wall. The round room under the Ring with the glow coming out of the stone. Six sleepers carved round it. The stag with the forest lying down with it. And beside it, coiled on itself, a serpent with scales cut like frost on a window.

"The stag got up," she said.

"Yes."

"And this one's getting up."

"Not yet," said Orrun. "It is turning over."

Behind her, up on the steps of Candle Row, somebody was calling a child's name over and over. The lamps burned white. On the lake, the island lay in the broken ice with its head on its paws.

---

It went at dawn.

She was asleep against Orrun's flank when he lifted his head, and she woke with it. Grey light. The lamps of Vael still white, paled by the morning. Frost on her coat, on his mane, on the shingle, thick as salt.

On the lake the island was standing.

It stood in the broken ice with the frost-fog round its legs. It turned its head and looked at the city. At the hill, with the hole in its crown where the terrace had been and the grey scar where the Blue tower had stood. At the shore. At them. She didn't know whether it saw them. Ten paces was as close as it had ever let him come, and it was a mile out.

Then it opened its wings.

It went up off the lake slowly, as if it were tired. Ice fell off it. Water fell off it. It went up and turned west, low over the far shore, and went away along the snow-line under the High Crown, with the sun coming up behind it and its shadow running ahead of it over the snow.

She looked for the stone in its jaws. She couldn't see it. Its jaws were shut, and empty, as far as she could tell. It was too far to tell.

Orrun didn't get up.

She waited for him to. He'd followed it for a hundred days. Over the Plains, over the High Crown. He'd lain on the shingle facing it for thirty-six. She waited for him to stand up and shake the frost off his mane and go after it, west, along the snow-line, and for her legs to go with him.

He lay with his chin on the stones.

"Not yet," he said. "Here first."

---

The frost came every night after that.

Hard frost. Winter frost, at the end of summer. She woke to it on her coat every morning, on her hair, on Orrun's back in a white crust that cracked when he moved. It lay on the shingle and the old fish-quay and the steps and the roofs of the empty lower city, grey-white, and it didn't go when the sun came up. It went by noon, mostly. By the third day it didn't go at all in the shadows.

The ice came back on the lake.

Every dawn, thicker. A skin and then a pane and then a floor. By the hundred-and-seventy-fifth day it didn't go by noon. She stood at the water's edge and put her heel on it and it held her. Grey-white, all the way out to where the island had lain, with the old broken plates frozen into it at angles like gravestones.

It wasn't coming from the sky. She knew that. It was coming up. Through the water. Through the stone under the shore. She felt it in her feet when she stood on the shingle, through Orrun, a cold that was nothing to do with the air. Rising.

On the second day snow came down on the pass.

She saw it from the shore. The Vael Pass above the city, where the road went up past the lake gate and the toll-house to the pass-stone, white. Snow on the summer road. Snow at the end of summer, on the pass the herd wouldn't come down in summer.

Orrun got up when he saw it.

He went up through the empty lower city. Up Ferry Street, up Rope Street, through the crowds on Candle Row, who drew back from him into doorways with their bundles and their children, up past the broken hill and out of the city on the pass road, to the lake gate. She went with him. The Wardens at the gate stood back. He went past them and lay down in the road on the far side of the iron-rail fence, with his chin on the frozen ruts, and was still.

She felt him call.

Not a sound. Down. Into the road. Into the stone under it, and along it, up the pass. The rumble she'd felt in her chest in the high bowl under the snowline, when the old cow met him at ten paces and the herd stood round with their calves in the middle. That, but long. Going up the road into the snow.

He lay there a whole day. She sat beside him. At dusk he got up and went back down to the shore.

---

Idony came on the night of the hundred-and-seventy-fifth day.

She heard him coming over the shingle. She knew his walk by now: careful, as if every stone might turn. He came out of the dark at the end of the old fish-quay with no lantern and a scarf over his face against the cold and sat down on the other side of Orrun's head.

"They're looking for you," she said.

"Yes."

"Nessa?"

"She's got me in the boat-shed. Under a ferry." He pulled the scarf down. In the white light from the city his face was thin and grey and looked older than it had a month ago. "Quell's sent for me three times. I'm to come to the Hall of Rings and answer for leaving the working."

"Will you?"

"After," said Idony.

He sat for a while. Then he said:

"I went into the hill."

She waited.

"Yesterday. Through the Library. The lower halls are open now. The floor of the lowest one's gone, where the door was, and there's a stair under it. Forty steps." He looked at the lake. "It's the same as the Ring. A round room with a dome. The plinth in the middle with the hollow in it, empty, warm. The six on the wall. The stag. The boar. The one with the horn. The island with the fin. The lion." He didn't look at Orrun. "But here the serpent's bigger than the others. Twice as big. It goes all the way round behind the plinth. And it's got a word under it. Its own. Not *sleep*, like at the Ring. Two words."

"What do they say?"

"*The pale one that coils.*" He said it slowly, as he said anything he'd had to work for. "The College would write it *the Pale Coil*. That's what I've written."

She thought of the frost on the shingle. The ice on the lake, every morning, thicker.

"Its scales are cut like frost," said Idony. "All of them. Every scale. Whoever cut it spent a year on the scales."

"When?"

"The fifth day." He took his glass out of his coat, the green one, and set it on a flat stone, and they both looked at it. The quicksilver stood at the top of the throat and trembled. It had stood there since the night the hill opened. "Perhaps sooner. I can't read it any closer. It's full. It's been full for three days. Everything's full."

He put it away.

"Keep them off the shore," said Maren.

"I've told them."

"Tell them again."

---

The dyers went back down on the hundred-and-seventy-sixth day.

She watched them come. Down Rope Street in the morning, in a long line, with their bundles and their handcarts and their children. Four nights on the steps of Candle Row in the frost, with no fires because there was nothing to burn and no lamps but the white ones that gave no heat. Their houses were down there. Their vats. Their stock, the bolts of cloth and the bags of madder and woad they'd left in the yards. And nothing had happened. Four days, and the lake was ice, and the island was gone. It was over.

A Warden at the bottom of Rope Street tried to stop them. A man in a dyer's apron with his arms blue to the elbow pushed past him. The rest followed.

They went along behind the fish-market, down Dye Street, the low street that ran along behind the market to the dyers' yards at the water's end of the lower city. She heard their doors open, and shutters. A child laughing. Smoke from a chimney: somebody had found something to burn.

The fish-market folk came after them. Then some of the people from the lanes behind Ferry Street.

The ferry-folk didn't come.

She saw Nessa at the top of Rope Street, standing in the middle of the road with her arms folded, and the ferry-folk behind her. A big man with a boathook argued with her. She didn't move. She said something Maren couldn't hear, and the man stood a moment, and then went back up the street.

Afterwards Tib told her what Nessa had said.

"He said five."

---

The herd came down the pass road at noon, out of the snow.

She felt them before she saw them. Through Orrun, through the ground. A slow heavy rumble, far up, coming down. Orrun lifted his head off the shingle.

Then they came out of the snow at the lake gate, thirty of them, red-brown, white with snow along their backs. The old cow first, grey-faced, with her one broken tusk and her one long loop. The calves in the middle. They came down the road to the iron-rail fence and the toll-house and stopped.

The Wardens on the gate stood with their lynx at their heels and looked at them.

The big one came out of the toll-house. The short-haired one who'd let her through, on the first day, without writing Orrun down. He looked at the old cow. She looked at him, over the fence, with her small wet eyes, and the snow melting on her back.

He didn't ask for the register. He took the bar off the gate himself and swung it open and stood beside it.

They came down through the city. Down the pass road, past the broken hill, down Rope Street. The crowds on Candle Row stood up on the steps to watch them go by. Down Ferry Street. They came out at the bottom into the empty fish-market and stood in it, steaming, among the dead stalls and the frozen gutters, and the old cow came over the shingle to where Orrun lay at the water's edge and put her trunk on his head.

He didn't get up. He leaned his head into it.

---

Haverly's edge woke on the morning of the fifth day.

She felt it before she knew what it was. A warmth at her hip, through the scabbard, through her coat. She thought for a moment she'd lain on a stone the sun had been on, except that there was no sun yet, and the stones were white with frost.

She drew it.

The runes were lit.

All along the blade, from the hilt to a hand's breadth from the point. The old letters that Haverly's family had cut there before anybody's grandfather was born. They'd been dark since the stair. Since the morning she'd stood in the river-fog with the dead on the hill and Grieve had put the sword into her hands, dark and cold, and she'd sharpened it every night on the jetty and watched the letters stay dark under the whetstone, and stopped expecting anything else. She'd carried it since the first day. Through Hearthmere and the Ring and the Moot and the Plains and the High Crown. Steel. A good edge. Nothing.

They were white: a thin white light along each stroke, like frost catching the first sun, steady, and when she turned the blade the light didn't move with the light of the sky. It was in the steel.

She held it up. Orrun turned his head and looked at it.

"It's awake," she said.

"Everything is awake," said Orrun. "Today."

---

The lake rose in the middle of the morning.

She was standing at the water's edge with the sword in her hand and the old cow beside her and Orrun lying at her feet, and the ice moved.

The whole floor of it, out in the middle of the lake where the island had lain, lifted. Slowly. A great low hump coming up in the grey-white, as wide as the lower city, with cracks running out from it in rings. She heard it before she saw the cracks. A groan. So deep that it was more in her feet than her ears.

Then the ice broke, and something came up through it.

Pale. That was the first thing. Pale as the ice. White and blue at once, the colour of the deepest part of a glacier, and so cold that a fog came off it the moment it met the air, a white fog boiling up off it in sheets, so that for a long moment she saw only fog and the pale thing moving inside it.

Then a coil.

A coil of it, breaking up through the ice like the back of a fish breaking the surface of a pond, except that the fish was as broad across the back as a ferry was long. Scaled. Every scale as big as a door, and every scale white at the edge and crazed across like frost on a window-pane. It rose out of the ice and went over and went down again into the black water, and another coil came up behind it, further out, and another, and another, so many and so long that she couldn't see where it began or ended.

The head came up last.

Long. Blunt. Flat across the top like a serpent's, and as pale as the rest. She couldn't see eyes. If it had them they were shut, or white, or under the frost that crusted its face. It lifted out of the fog on a neck as thick as the Blue tower had been, high, higher, until it was above the city's towers, above the hill, and hung there, swaying a little, with water and broken ice sheeting off it.

It opened its mouth. No sound came out. Only cold.

She felt it. A wave of cold coming off it across the lake like the wind off the Mere in a black winter. It went past her and up the shore and up the streets of the lower city and the frost thickened on everything as it passed. On her coat. On the sword. On the old cow's back.

Orrun stood up.

"The pale one," said Maren.

"Yes," said Orrun. "It is awake."

And under its coils, the lake rose.

---

She saw it come.

The whole lake lifted. As if the thing under it had taken all the room there was, and the water had nowhere to go but up. It rose round the coils in a great slow swell, and the broken ice rode up on it, and it came toward the shore.

It didn't need to be fast. A long grey wall of water and ice, the height of a house, the length of the shore, coming in over the shingle.

"Up," she said, to nobody, to everybody. "Up."

Orrun was already moving.

Along the shingle behind the old fish-quay, past the fish-market, toward the low end of the lower city where Dye Street ran behind the market to the dyers' yards. Where the smoke was coming out of a chimney. Where she'd heard a child laughing.

She ran with him.

---

He stopped at the mouth of Dye Street, where it came out on the shore between the last of the market stalls and the first of the dyers' sheds. A narrow cobbled street, with houses three storeys high on the landward side and the yards and their vats on the lake side. People in the upper windows. People at the doors, looking out at the lake. A woman with a child on her hip.

He put his head down.

She felt him go into the stone under the cobbles. The old stone. The stone the College had paved over, and the city had built on, and nobody had thought about for two hundred years: the same old-realm stone that ran under the hill and the terrace and the Library and the Undercroft, running down through the city under the streets to the water. He went into it. She went with him.

It answered.

The street came up.

Not the cobbles. The stone under them. It came up out of the ground at the mouth of Dye Street in a long grey wall, the cobbles sliding off it and rattling down, dressed so close you couldn't see the joins, up past her knees, her waist, her head, up to the height of the first-floor windows. Across the street, from the last market stall on the left to the first dyers' shed on the right. Twenty paces. Thirty.

It wasn't enough. She felt it wasn't enough. The street went on past the dyers' sheds along the shore, open, and the water would come round.

Then at the far end of the wall, fifty paces off, the cobbles heaved, and broke, and something came up out of them.

Orrun.

Another Orrun. As big as him. Grey, with the broken-rock mane and the plates along his back, standing up out of the street at the far end of the wall with the cobbles falling off his shoulders, and putting his head down. And the wall came up under him too, from his end, toward the middle, until the two ends met.

Fifty paces of old stone, the height of a house, across the mouth of Dye Street and along the shore in front of the dyers' sheds.

She was in both of them. She felt it. Both ends of the wall, both of him, holding. She felt her legs go and she put her hand on the wall to stay up.

She felt him grow.

Under her hand, through the stone. He was pouring himself into the wall and the wall was pouring itself back into him, the old stone, the Deep in it, the surge, more than anything had ever been in it, and it went into him. She felt his shoulders broaden. His legs set deeper. The plates along his back thicken. Like a tree putting on a ring in a single morning.

The water hit the wall.

---

She didn't see it hit. She was behind the wall. She heard it. A sound like the sea hitting a cliff. The wall shook. She felt it in her hand, in her teeth, in both of him at once. Water came over the top in a sheet and fell on the cobbles behind and ran down Dye Street round her ankles, so cold her feet went dead.

The wall held.

On either side of it, the water came round. Past the market. Past the far end where the other Orrun stood. Into the lower city. She heard it going up Ferry Street, into the fish-market, into the lanes behind. Up to the doors. Up to the windows. Screaming somewhere. A crash of something going down.

But not into Dye Street. Dye Street stood behind the wall with its doors and its windows and its people in them, and the water came over the top in sheets and ran down past her ankles and out at the landward end, and that was all.

Then the wall cracked.

---

In the middle. A block at the height of her chest. She saw the crack before she heard it: a black line, thin as a hair, running across the grey stone from one joint toward the other. Then she heard it. A tick. Like ice on a pond in the night.

She put her hand on it. She didn't know why. She put her hand flat on the stone over the crack and felt it go. Through her hand. Through Orrun, at both ends. The weight of the whole lake leaning on one block. The block giving. A little. A little more.

He couldn't hold it. She felt him trying. He was holding the whole wall, at both ends, and growing, and the water was coming, and the block in the middle was giving, and he couldn't be in the middle too.

She didn't hear Idony come.

He was beside her. Out of breath, his scarf gone, his coat soaked to the waist. He'd come down the fish-market steps through the water. He had his green glass in his hand, broken. Its throat was snapped off.

"It broke at the top of Rope Street," he said. "I knew."

He looked at the crack.

"Hold," he said.

"What?"

"The rune. *Hold*." He was looking at the crack as if it were a page. "The one inside every link of the chains. The bird showed it to me in the contract. Four strokes and the hooks." His voice was very fast. "I can read it. I can't cut it. Writing a rune that answers is Third-ring work. I'm Second. A wrong stroke does nothing." He put his hand on the stone beside hers. "But in a surge... it might answer anyway. Everything's answering."

"You haven't got a chisel."

He looked at the sword in her other hand. At its white-lit runes.

She gave it to him.

---

He held it like a pen.

He held it high on the grip, close to the guard, not like a sword, with the point down against the stone, as he held his pen at the desk under the bird's perch when he wrote in the smallest hand he had. His hand was shaking. She saw that. She saw him see it and stop, and breathe, and the shaking stop.

"Keep your hand there," he said. "Don't move it."

She kept it there. Flat on the stone, beside the crack, with the cold of the lake coming through the wall into her palm.

He cut.

The edge went into the stone like a knife into cheese. She'd never seen anything do that. He drew it down, one stroke, a hand's length, square and spare, and the stone parted under it in a clean grey line, and the white light from the runes on the blade went into the cut and stayed there.

She felt it go through her.

Not from the sword. From Orrun. His earth. The thing that came into her when he opened stone, the weight and the slowness and the long patience of rock. It came up through her feet from the street and through her body and down her arm and out of her hand into the stone beside the cut. Into the cut. She felt it go. She couldn't have stopped it if she'd tried.

He cut the second stroke. Across. The third. Down. The fourth.

The hooks. Four of them, small, at the ends of the strokes. He cut them very slowly, with the tip of the blade, his tongue between his teeth.

The rune lit.

White. All four strokes and all four hooks, lit from inside the stone, a thin steady white like the runes on the blade. And the crack stopped. She felt it stop. Under her hand, the block that had been giving, a little, a little more, stopped giving. It stood.

It held.

---

The cold came after.

She took her hand off the stone and it didn't feel like her hand. It was white. Not the white of the frost. The white of something that had been under snow all night. She couldn't feel it. She couldn't feel her arm to the shoulder. And then the cold went on, into her chest, into her stomach, down her legs, until she was shaking so hard her teeth knocked together and she had to sit down in the water at the foot of the wall.

Idony sat down beside her. He'd let go of the sword. It lay on the cobbles in the water with its runes still lit.

His hands were white to the wrist. His nose was bleeding. He looked at his hands as if they belonged to somebody else.

"It costs," he said. "Whoever uses it. Haverly's edge." He wiped his nose with the back of his hand and looked at the blood. "You knew that."

"Grieve told me. When he gave it to me."

He looked up at the rune.

"It'll fall," he said. "That block. Within the year." His teeth were going too. "Shaped stone's weaker forever after. The toll's split. Some from you and some from the stone." He looked at it. "It'll hold today."

---

The other Orrun went at noon.

She felt him go. At the far end of the wall. Not falling. Unmaking. He sank back into the street as if the street were water, and the cobbles closed over his back, and he was gone, and everything he'd been holding came back into the one who was left.

Orrun went down on his side at the mouth of Dye Street with the wall above him and lay there.

She crawled to him. He was bigger. She could see it now. He'd been as tall at the shoulder as the top of a cart's load in the spring. Now his shoulder was at the height of the first-floor windows. His mane lay across the cobbles like a fall of scree. His side went up and down, slowly, and his eyes were shut.

"Can you stand?"

"No," said Orrun. "Not today."

She lay down against his chest and shook, and he was warm, the only warm thing in Vael, and she shook less.

---

The herd went into the water.

She heard it before she saw it. The old cow. A sound she'd never heard her make: not the rumble through the ground, a trumpet, high and hard and so loud it hurt, going up off the fish-market. Then the splashing.

She got up on her knees and looked over Orrun's flank.

The old cow was in the drowned street behind the fish-market, chest-deep, wading. Grey-faced, with the water up to her shoulder and her one long tusk lifted out of it. Pushing through the brown water and the floating ice and the wreck of the stalls. Toward a house where people were standing in an upper window.

She stopped under the window. She lifted her trunk.

A man climbed out of the window onto her head. Then a woman, handed down to him. Then two children. They sat on her back, on her neck, holding onto her ears and the ridge of her spine, and she turned in the water and waded back toward the steps.

Behind her the herd came. All thirty, except the calves, which the eldest cows had herded up onto the steps of the fish-market and stood guard over. Into the water, into the streets, through the drowned lower city. Under windows. Under roofs where people sat in the frost with their arms round their knees. Wading. Lifting. Carrying.

She watched them all morning. The old cow went back and forth from the steps to the water eleven times. On the eleventh she came back with a very old man sitting on her tusk like a child on a gate, holding on with both arms, his feet trailing in the flood.

---

The Wardens were on the roofs.

She hadn't seen them come. They were up there when she looked, on the ridges of the drowned houses, in grey and blue, with ropes. With their lynx. The big pale cats went along the ridges and the gutters with their noses down, low, testing the air, fast, so sure-footed on the frosted slates that they might have been on grass.

She saw one stop. A big male, at the end of a ridge over a flooded lane. It put its head down over the gutter and stood very still and then turned and called. A short harsh cough.

The Warden with it went down the rope into the water.

He came up with a boy. Limp, blue, his hair full of ice. The Warden held him over his knee on the ridge and hit him on the back, and hit him again, and the boy coughed up water and screamed, and the lynx stood beside them with its ears flat and watched.

All morning. Up and down the drowned streets. The lynx finding them by scent, the ones under the water, the ones under the ice, the ones nobody else could have found. The Wardens going down the ropes.

The big short-haired one was among them. She saw him go down four times.

---

The Coil went at midday.

She was on top of the wall. She'd climbed it, with her one hand that worked, from the cart someone had left against it, because she had to see. She stood on the top of the old stone in the frost and the spray, with the water still leaning on the far side of it, and looked out.

The lake was gone.

Not gone. Lower. Much lower, and still going down. The water was pulling back off the lower city, out of the streets, leaving them brown and wrecked and full of ice, and the lake beyond them was a grey churn of broken ice going south. All of it. Going south down the length of the lake, toward the lake's foot, where the water left Glasswater for the valley.

The Coil was going with it. Ahead of it.

She saw it go. The pale coils going down the lake, one after the other, slow, in the fog that came off them, the long blunt head out in front, low now, close to the water. Going south. To the lake's foot. To the narrow place at the end of Glasswater where the hills came down on either side and the lake spilled over a lip of stone into the valley.

It went over the lip.

She saw it go. The head first, down out of sight. Then the coils, over and over, pouring down into the valley like a river of pale stone, in the fog. And the lip of stone broke under it. She saw the rock go. A whole shoulder of the hill at the lake's foot sliding down after the Coil in a long grey fall, and where it had been, a gap.

The lake went through the gap.

All of it. All of Glasswater, it seemed, pouring through the broken lip into the valley, brown and white, roaring. So loud she could hear it from a league off. Going down the valley after the Coil. Down the long valley south that heads the Wend, toward every town and steading and ferry along the river, toward the Marches, toward home.

She stood on the wall and watched it go and didn't know what she was feeling. Her hand was dead. The water behind her was falling, foot by foot, and leaving frost a finger thick on everything it had touched.

---

Idony left soon after.

He came up onto the wall beside her, slowly, with his white hands in his armpits. He'd been with Nessa. He'd gone up Rope Street to find her as soon as he could stand, and come back down. He stood looking south at the gap.

"She's alive," he said. "All of them. The ferry-folk. Tib. Mother Talley. They were on Candle Row." He was quiet a moment. "She kept them there."

"Because you told her."

"Because I told her five." He looked at the gap. "I said perhaps fewer. It was five." His voice did something. "If I'd said four, she'd have let them go down yesterday with the dyers."

He didn't say anything else about it. She didn't either.

He looked up at the hill. At the hole in the crown of it. At the long cliff-front of the Library under it, the tall narrow windows, the great door.

"I have to go up," he said.

"Why?"

"The hill's open. The Undercroft's open, the lowest halls, everything. Half the Wardens are down here on the roofs. The proctors are counting the dead." He was still looking at the Library. "Anyone who wants anything in Vael can walk into the Library today. Anyone at all."

She thought of the bird on its perch at the hill end. Of its one good eye. Of the roll.

"Go," she said.

He went down the wall and up through the wrecked fish-market and up Ferry Street, walking fast, with his hands in his armpits. She watched him go until she couldn't see him.

She didn't go with him. She looked down at Orrun, lying on his side at the foot of the wall, and sat down on top of it, and stayed.

---

She saw the dragons in the early afternoon.

She was still on the wall. Shaking less. Her hand had begun to hurt, which she thought was better than not feeling it. She was looking south, at the valley, where the lake was still pouring through the gap in a long brown fall, and the valley below it was full of water from side to side, brown and white, with the tops of trees standing out of it.

Two shapes, low over the water. Coming up the valley.

North. Against the flood. Low, so low their shadows ran on the brown water under them, coming up the drowned valley from the south toward the lake's foot. A red one, big, heavy in the wing. A green one, small, quick, a little ahead, turning like a swallow over a pond.

She knew them.

She'd seen the green one at dusk from the Library window, going north along the snow-line. She'd heard of the red one from a salt-merchant at the lake gate. A red and a green, over Gallows Quay in broad day.

They came up over the broken lip of the lake, over the gap, and went up the lake low over the churning ice, and over the drowned lower city, and up. Up over the broken hill. Over the Library.

She watched them go over the hill and out of sight.

The farmer, she thought.

She didn't get up. Orrun lay at the foot of the wall with his eyes shut. The old cow was in the water with a woman on her back. The pages for the farmer were inside her coat, with the black book, eleven of them, folded, where they'd been for a month.

She stayed on the wall.

---

Later there was smoke on the hill.

She saw it go up from behind the Library's cliff-front, black, thick, in the frost-still air. And then she felt something, through Orrun, through the stone. A roar that was no sound but a shaking in the old stone under the city, coming down from the hill. Then another.

Orrun opened his eyes.

"Fire," he said. "On the hill. And kin." He was quiet. "Young kin. Bound."

She looked at the smoke.

"Can you stand?"

"No," said Orrun.

She didn't go. She sat on the wall with her dead hand in her lap and the sword across her knees and watched the smoke go up from the hill, and didn't go, because he couldn't stand and she couldn't leave him at the foot of a wall in a drowned city with the frost on him. She'd told herself a hundred times since the Moot that she wasn't the one who went to everything. She told herself again.

The smoke went up all afternoon.

---

The lamps died at dusk.

She was on the wall when it happened. The sun had gone down behind the High Crown and the sky over the Fells had gone green and then grey, and the lamps of Vael were burning white all over the city as they had for five days, in every window of every tower, on the hill, in the drowned streets of the lower city where the water had left them.

They went out.

One after another this time, where the hill at noon on the binding day had gone all at once. Down the hill from the top. She watched them go. The tall windows of the Library, white, then grey, then dark. The towers. The Hall of Rings. Down the hill toward Candle Row, street by street, like somebody walking down through the city putting them out with their fingers. Down Rope Street. Down Ferry Street. Mother Talley's lamp at the corner of the drowned fish-market, white, and then nothing.

Then the lower city. Then the last one, on the shore, by the steps.

Dark.

She looked down at the sword across her knees.

The runes were going out. From the point toward the hilt. A thin white light along each stroke, fading, like frost when the sun gets onto it. Grey. Then nothing. Steel. The letters dark in the steel, as they'd been on the jetty and at the Ring and every day since the stair but this one.

She put her hand on the wall beside her.

It was stone. Just stone. Cold. She couldn't feel anything in it. Nothing under it. Nothing in the ground under the city, nothing in the old stone running down to the water. She put her hand flat and pressed and felt for it, as she'd felt for it every day since the bond, and there was nothing there.

She felt Orrun feel it too. Below her. He lifted his head off the cobbles and put it down again.

"It has gone," he said. "All of it. With the pale one."

---

The big Warden came along the shore in the dark.

She saw his shape against the frost. He'd come up out of the water at the end of the fish-market, soaked to the chest, with his lynx beside him, its fur flat with wet. He stopped at the foot of the wall and looked up at her. Then at Orrun. Then at the rune in the middle of the wall, the four strokes and the hooks, cut in the stone at the height of her chest.

It was dark too. Just a cut in the stone now.

He looked at his arm.

His sleeve-ward, the band of something round his forearm under the grey cloth that she'd seen burning steadily on every Warden at the lake gate since the day she came. It was dark. He turned his arm over and looked at it, in the frost-light, as if he'd never seen it before.

"Five days I've been pulling people out of that water," he said. Not to her. To the arm. "And the College counting rings up on the hill."

He looked up at the hill. The Library. The Hall of Rings. All of it dark.

"A ring means nothing on dead ground," he said.

He went on along the shore in the dark with his lynx, toward the next drowned street.

---

A Library clerk came for her when it was fully dark.

A young one, in a grey gown torn at the hem, running down Ferry Street through the wreck with no lantern, slipping on the frost. He stopped at the foot of the wall and looked up at her and at the lion, and couldn't speak for a moment, from running.

"Reader Vael," he said. "Sent me. For you. The lion-woman." He swallowed. "He says come up."

"Why?"

The clerk looked at her. In the dark she couldn't see his face. She heard his breath.

"The Old Reader," he said.

She waited.

He didn't say anything else. He didn't have to. She'd heard it in his voice.

She looked down at Orrun.

He opened his eyes. Very slowly, with the frost cracking off his mane, he got his forelegs under him. Then his hind legs. He stood. Swaying. As tall as the first-floor windows of Dye Street, with the wall at his shoulder and the dark rune cut in it.

"I will come," he said.
