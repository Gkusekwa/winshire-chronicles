---

He watched the camp for twenty days, from the snow.

From the ridge on the east side of the bowl, where he'd lain the first night with the stone digging into his hip and Lisska pressed against his neck. He'd dug himself a hole there now. Down into the snow, out of the wind, with a roof of his coat and a wall of Kharrak, who lay along the back of the ridge where he couldn't be seen and said every morning that he was bored, and every evening that he was cold, and every night nothing at all, because he was asleep.

The camp didn't change.

The grey tents. The rope corral. The stone hearth with its iron pots, smouldering, and the honey-smoke running along the cut trench to the stakes. The four young dragons on their chains. The big bronze. The black. The two-year-old red. The yearling, small and red and still, at the end of the line.

Every morning the binders went down the line and touched each head in turn. Every evening they did it again. In between, the four dragons lay on the trodden snow with their eyes open and looked at nothing.

He watched the yearling most. Since the night Lisska had called to her from the ridge, and she'd lifted her head. He watched her every day for that again. She never did it.

*She knows my voice,* said Lisska. *She does. She's just too far in.*

---

The lantern owl came every few days.

A grey bird, big-eyed, out of the south-west, low over the snow at dusk. Into the captain's tent. Out again the next morning, back the way it had come. He knew where it went. He didn't know what it carried.

On the night of the hundred-and-seventy-second day the sky went white.

Not over the camp. Far off. South-west, over the Fells, low on the clouds, so far away that at first he thought it was lightning. But it didn't flicker. It came up white along the bottom of the clouds, as if somebody had opened a door in a dark room a long way off, and stayed, and then went out.

*What was that?* said Lisska.

He didn't know.

The next day a wind came off the south-west so cold that the snow in his hole went hard as stone, and Kharrak stopped saying he was bored.

---

The captain went on the hundred-and-seventy-first day.

Calder saw him go. The man with the grey beard who'd stood at the stakes the first evening with his hands behind his back. With fifteen hunters behind him, in a line, with packs and crossbows and coils of rope, down out of the bowl by the cornice pass. The way Tasso had guided them in. He watched them go down the gully till he couldn't see them.

Four stayed. Four hunters and the four binders and the silent man from the coast, who sat on his rock as he always did, with his hands on his knees, and didn't look up when the captain went.

*They're going to the city,* said Lisska.

"On foot."

*It's six days on foot. Tasso said.*

He thought of what Tasso had said. *Not yet. When the hill burns all through.*

He thought of the white light on the clouds.

---

They went on the night of the hundred-and-seventy-sixth day.

He didn't sleep. He'd known they would, because at dusk the binders brought out the pads. Riding pads, leather, stuffed, with straps, like the pads a carter put on a horse's back under the load. They buckled them on the four dragons behind the shoulders. Then they slung iron baskets under the dragons' chests, on chains, and put a honey-pot in every basket, smouldering, with a lid of iron mesh so that the smoke came out in a thin sweet thread and ran back under the dragons' bellies.

Then a binder climbed up onto each neck and sat there with his hand on the head.

The silent man got up off his rock. He climbed up onto the bronze behind its binder and sat with his hands on his knees.

The four camp hunters got onto the wyverns, two to each. Up on the ledge above the stakes. The black-green wyverns with their barbed tails, crouched, their wings half open.

At midnight they went up.

Six shapes against the snow-light, without a sound. No roar. No fire. Up out of the bowl and over the cornice and west.

He was on Kharrak before they'd cleared the ridge.

---

He followed them by smell.

The honey-smoke. A thread of it on the wind, sweet and thick, coming back to them from six shapes he couldn't see. A league ahead. Kharrak flew low along the ridges behind them with his head stretched out and his nostrils wide, and every time the wind changed and the smell went, he slowed, and turned his head, and found it again.

Lisska went ahead. High, in the dark, where she could see them against the snow.

*West,* she said, small at the back of his head. *Still west. They're going along the end of the mountains.* A long time later: *South now. They're going round.*

"Round what?"

*Round the end of the mountains. Down.*

He didn't know the country. He'd come into it from the south-east, over the Fells, and he'd seen Vael only through Esk's lens. He held onto Kharrak's neck in the dark and felt the cold come up off the snow, and thought: they're not going in by the pass. They're going in by the back.

At the first grey of dawn Lisska said: *They've come down.*

---

A valley. He saw it as the light came.

A long deep valley going south out of the mountains, with steep wooded sides and a river in the bottom of it, white with rapids. And at the head of it, north, where the valley began, a lip of grey rock between two hills, and over the lip, water. A lake. Long and grey and still, going away north between the mountains, with fog lying on it.

And at the far end of the lake, small, a city on a hill.

*That's it,* said Lisska. *That's the city.*

The hunters had come down on a shelf above the river a little below the lake's lip. Six shapes on the grass, folded. Calder saw them from a league down the valley and put Kharrak down on a ledge in the valley's side under an overhang of rock, out of sight, where they could see the shelf.

He understood it then. Why they'd come round. The Wardens watched the pass road into Vael, on the north-east. Starwatch looked north-east. Nobody looked at the lake's foot. Nobody would look for six dragons coming up the lake from the south, low over the water, in the fog.

He lay on the ledge with his chin on his hands and watched the shelf.

*It's cold,* said Lisska.

It was. Colder than the camp. Colder than the snow. A cold that came down the valley off the lake, in the fog, and lay on the ledge, and got into his coat, and his boots, and his teeth.

---

The owl came in the middle of the morning.

Down the lake, out of the fog, low. Grey, big-eyed. It went over the lip and down to the shelf and in among the six shapes.

They went up.

All six. At once. Off the shelf and up the valley over the lip of rock and into the fog over the lake. He saw them go into it. The bronze, the black, the two reds, the wyverns. Into the white. Gone.

He was getting up when the fog moved.

It thickened. All at once. Rolling down off the lake over the lip into the valley, white, boiling, as if the lake had begun to steam. And with it, the cold.

He'd never felt cold like it.

It came down the valley in a wave. He felt it on his face first. Then in his chest, when he breathed. The air went thin and sharp and burned his nose. Frost came on the rock of the ledge in front of him, he watched it come, white, creeping out across the stone like mould across bread. Frost came on his sleeves. He felt it come in his beard.

Kharrak went flat on the ledge.

Calder had never seen him do it. He went down onto his belly with his wings pressed tight against his sides and his head down on the rock, and his eyes shut, and didn't move.

*Don't breathe fire,* said Calder. He didn't know why he said it.

*I'm not breathing at all,* said Kharrak.

Lisska crawled in under Kharrak's wing and pressed herself against his side.

---

It came over the lip in the fog.

He didn't see it clearly. He wasn't meant to. Nobody was. He saw something pale moving in the white, very large. A coil. A curve of something going over the lip of rock, white-blue, with a scaled back that caught what light there was. Then another coil. Then another. Pouring over the lip, down into the valley, like a river of pale stone.

And the lip broke.

He heard that. He'd never heard anything like it. A crack so huge it was felt more than heard, and then a roar, and the whole grey shoulder of rock between the two hills at the lake's foot went down into the valley in a slide. And behind it, the lake.

The lake came through.

Brown and white. A wall of it. Coming down the valley after the pale thing, filling it, from side to side, trees going down in front of it like grass.

The pale thing came down the valley past them.

Below the ledge. In its own fog. So close that he could see the scales: each one as big as a door, white at the edge, crazed across like frost on a window. Coil after coil going past below him, down the valley, so slow and so long that he stopped counting. And the cold coming up off it so hard that he felt his eyelashes freeze together and had to put his hand over his eyes.

He felt Lisska shaking under Kharrak's wing.

He felt Kharrak not breathing.

Then it was past. Going on down the valley in its fog, south, ahead of the water. And the water came after it, roaring, below the ledge, so high that the spray came up over the edge of the rock and froze on Kharrak's back.

---

They waited for the fog to go.

It went slowly. Down the valley, after the thing that made it. The cold went with it. The frost on the ledge stopped growing and lay there, white, a finger thick. Below them the valley was full of water from side to side, brown, moving fast, with whole trees turning over in it.

Kharrak lifted his head.

*It's gone,* he said.

"What was it?"

*I don't know.* He was quiet. *It was very old. It was asleep a long time. It didn't see us. It didn't see anything.*

Calder looked up the valley. At the broken lip, where the lake was still pouring through the gap in a long brown fall. At the lake beyond it, lower than it had been, grey, churning with broken ice. At the city at the far end of it, on its hill.

Six shapes had gone up the lake in the fog an hour ago.

*They'll be there,* said Lisska.

He got up onto Kharrak's neck.

---

They went north up the valley against the flood.

Low. Kharrak's shadow ran on the brown water under them. Lisska went a little ahead, turning, quick, as she always did when she was frightened and didn't want him to know. The flood came down under them. A drowned barn went past. A drowned cow. A roof with a cat on it, looking up at them.

Over the broken lip. Over the lake.

The fog had gone off it. The lake was grey and lower than it should have been, with a wide band of wet mud and frost and wreckage all round its shore where the water had been. Up the lake, low, over the churning ice. Toward the city.

He saw the city come up out of the lake.

The lower city first. Along the shore. Or what had been the lower city. Half of it was under water. Brown water in the streets, up to the doors, up to the windows, with roofs standing out of it and people on the roofs. A dead city. A drowned one. And over it, on the hill, the towers. Grey-blue slate. Covered bridges between them. And every lamp in every window of every tower burning white in broad day, as if the whole city had been set alight from inside.

At the top of the hill a long grey front of stone cut into the hillside, half a league long, with a row of tall narrow windows. The Library. He'd seen it through Esk's lens. It was bigger than the lens had made it.

There was smoke coming up from the hill.

---

He came over the hill low and saw it all at once.

The crown of the hill was broken. A great hole where something had been, with dust still lying grey on everything round it, and frost on the dust. A heap of grey-blue slate and stone beside it that had been a tower. And the Library running along the top of the hill behind the broken place, its long slate roof going away along the ridge for half a league, its tall windows blazing white.

The bronze was on the broken terrace by the hole.

Standing. Its binder on its neck. The iron basket under its chest, smoking. Its head turned toward the great door of the Library, with its eyes on nothing.

The black was on the slates above the great door. Crouched on the roof like a cat on a wall, with its binder.

The two-year-old red was on the dome of a round building across the terrace. A domed hall with a ring of windows.

The yearling was on the ridge of the Library's roof at the far end. The hill end. Over a great window, taller than the rest. Small and red against the grey sky, with a binder on her neck.

The wyverns were in the air. Circling over the front, slow, black-green.

And along the front of the Library, on the steps, by the great door, the iron baskets set down in a row. Smoking. The honey-smoke going up off the steps in a long sweet drift and lying along the front.

A man lay on the steps by the great door in a grey gown. He didn't move.

The smoke he'd seen came from further along the hill. A college roof, burning. Black smoke going up thick from slates that had caught. He saw Wardens there in grey and blue, running, with buckets. Not many. Three. Four.

*Drawing them off,* said Kharrak. *The black did that. To bring them away from here.*

The great door of the Library was open.

---

He didn't see the drawer until he'd drawn.

He was looking at the yearling. At the binder on her neck. At the great door, open, and the dark behind it. Kharrak was coming round over the terrace in a long low turn toward the bronze, and Calder was holding on, and the cold air was in his face.

Then something hit them.

Not a blow. Not like a blow. Like a wall. Like the whole air on one side of them turning to stone and coming at them all at once. It hit Kharrak on the left side, under the wing, and Calder felt it go through Kharrak into him, through the bond, a weight so huge it emptied his chest. And they went sideways. Out of the turn. Out of the air.

Onto the Library's roof.

Kharrak hit the slates and they broke under him. Calder came off his neck. He went over the slates on his back, sliding, fast, down toward the edge and the long fall to the front steps. Slates going with him. His hands found nothing. Then a gutter. Lead. He got his fingers into it and it bent and held, and he hung there, on his front, with his legs over nothing, and the sweet smell of the honey-smoke coming up from the steps below.

He looked up along the roof.

There. On the edge of the broken terrace. A man standing on the old stone at the very edge of the hole, where the paving had been torn off and the grey stone under it showed. Big. Bald. A heavy jaw. Standing with his feet apart and his hands out in front of him, open, palms forward.

His coat was smoking.

As Calder watched, the big man's knees went. He went down on one knee on the stone, with his hands still out, and smoke coming up off his sleeves, and his face grey. In the surge, Calder thought. He didn't know the word yet. He thought it later. In the surge the stone was too full, and the man had drawn on it as hard as he would have drawn on ordinary stone, and it had gone through him like a river through a sluice.

The big man got up again. Slowly. He turned his hands toward the roof.

---

Kharrak got up first.

Off the broken slates, with a roar that shook the roof under Calder's hands. And went. Not for the drawer. For the bronze. Off the roof and across the terrace in two wingbeats and onto it, all of him, his forefeet on its back, his jaws on its neck behind the binder.

The bronze didn't scream. It didn't do anything. It went down under him on the broken paving and didn't fight and didn't stop. It was trying to get up. Under Kharrak, with his teeth in its neck, it was still trying to get up and turn toward the great door, because that was where the hand on its head had told it to look, and it didn't feel anything.

Kharrak let go of its neck and breathed.

Not on the bronze. On its binder. A short hard rush of fire, close, along the bronze's neck where the man sat. The binder went off the bronze's neck burning and rolled on the paving, beating at his coat with his hands, and got up, and ran for the great door with his back on fire.

The bronze stopped trying to get up.

It lay on the terrace under Kharrak's forefeet, slack, its eyes open, looking at nothing, waiting for a hand.

*It doesn't feel anything,* said Kharrak. His voice in Calder's head was strange. *I bit it and it didn't feel anything.*

---

Calder got onto the roof.

Off the gutter, slowly, with his knees on the lead and then on the slates. He lay flat on the slope of the roof and looked for Lisska.

She was over the front. Over the great door and the steps. Low, turning, coming round toward the yearling at the far end. And under her, along the front, lay the honey-smoke.

She went into it.

He felt her go. Not her body. Her. She went into the sweet drift over the steps and he felt her go away from him. A pulling. As if someone had taken hold of the bond at her end and begun to draw it out of him like a thread out of cloth. He felt her slow. Felt her wings slow. Felt her start to come down. Toward the steps. Toward the iron baskets. Gently. As if she were tired, and the steps looked soft.

"Up," he shouted. Aloud. Not through the bond. He didn't trust the bond. "Lisska. Up. Up."

She heard him. He felt her hear him, a long way off, through the sweetness.

She came up.

Out of the smoke, shaking, her wings going too fast, climbing, up over the roof. She came down on the slates beside him and pressed herself against his side and shook.

*It was nice,* she said. Very small. *It was so nice. I didn't want to come back.*

"I know."

*I didn't want to come back to you.*

"I know," said Calder. He put his hand on her neck. "You came back."

---

The yearling was watching her.

He saw it. At the far end of the roof, on the ridge over the tall window. The small red dragon, with the binder on her neck. She'd turned her head. She was looking along the roof, along the ridge, at Lisska. The binder had his hand on her head and she was looking past it.

Lisska saw it too.

She stopped shaking. She lifted her head off his side.

*She heard me,* she said. *Just now. When I was in the smoke. She heard me.*

"Call her."

*He'll stop her. Like at the camp.*

"Call her anyway."

Lisska went up off the roof. Not far. Above the ridge, twenty paces from the yearling, hanging in the cold air with her wings going. Where the yearling could see her. And sang.

He'd heard it once, from the ridge over the camp. He felt it more than heard it. Not a sound a person makes. A hum, low, through his chest, through the roof under him. The sound of the ledges at the Roosts when the eggs were near hatching and every mother on the cliff hummed to them through the stone. A sound that meant: *here. Here. You're here.*

The yearling lifted her head.

---

Everything was too much that afternoon. He understood that afterwards. Everything in Vael was too full. The stone, the lamps, the words. The bound beasts too. The binding was only words and smoke and a man's will, held in an iron collar, and in the surge it held as a wet knot holds when the rope swells.

It slipped.

He saw it slip. The binder's hand was on the yearling's head, and she was looking at Lisska, and he saw the binder press down, hard, as he'd pressed at the camp to empty her. And she didn't empty.

She turned her head on her neck.

All the way round. Quick, like a bird turning to its own back. She took the binder off her neck in her jaws, the whole man, by the shoulder, and lifted him off her, and turned, and opened her jaws over the edge of the roof.

He fell past the tall window to the steps below and lay there.

He didn't move. He didn't move again. Calder knew it from the way he lay.

---

The yearling stood on the ridge of the roof in the frost.

She was shaking. All of her. Her legs, her wings, the end of her tail. She stood on the ridge with her four feet together and her wings half open, and looked round her.

At the roof. At the sky. At the towers, with their white lamps burning in broad day. At the drowned city, and the lake, and the mountains. At Lisska, hanging in the air in front of her. At him.

She didn't know where she was. He could see it. She'd been in a box and a trench and a cart and a cold bowl in the mountains, with a hand on her head, for a year, and she'd never once seen anything with her own eyes, and now she was standing on a roof over a drowned city in the frost with the hand gone, and she didn't know what anything was.

He went along the ridge to her.

On his hands and knees. Along the top of the roof, on the lead of the ridge, with the slates going down on either side of him to the long fall. Slowly. With the cold coming through his breeches.

He stopped an arm's length from her and sat down astride the ridge.

He didn't touch her.

"It's all right," he said. Out loud. "It's a roof. You're on a roof. It's all right."

She looked at him. Her eyes were gold, like Lisska's, but darker. Wide.

"There's a lake down there," said Calder. "It's done something odd today, but it's a lake. And that's a city. People live in it. Too many of them. And over there," he pointed, slowly, west, "there's barley. A long way off. Fields of it. You'll like barley. Everyone does. It's no use to you, you can't eat it, but you'll like it. It goes gold at the end of summer, and when the wind comes over it, it goes all one way, like water."

He didn't know why he said it. He kept saying it. Barley, and the farm, and the river, and the reeds. Lisska came down out of the air and landed on the ridge on the other side of the yearling and lay down on the lead with her chin on her forefeet, small and green and close, and hummed.

The yearling stopped shaking.

Not all at once. Slowly. Her wings came down. Her tail came down. She folded her legs under her on the ridge, as Lisska did, and lay there between them, and looked at the drowned city.

---

A wyvern came for Lisska.

He heard it before he saw it. A hiss, and the snap of leather wings, close, and then it was over the ridge, black-green, its barbed tail swinging, its two riders flat on its back. Coming down at Lisska where she lay on the lead.

It didn't reach her.

Kharrak came off the terrace like a stone off a sling. Up, across the front, over the roof. He took the wyvern out of the air. Calder saw it. Kharrak's jaws closed on its neck behind the head, and its wings went up, and Kharrak went on over the ridge with it, and shook it once, like a dog with a rat, and let it go.

It fell. Riders and all. Down the front of the hill, turning over, and into the smoke from the burning college roof, and was gone.

The other wyvern went away north over the towers, fast, and didn't come back.

Kharrak came down on the ridge behind him with a thump that set every slate on the roof rattling.

*Say it,* said Kharrak.

"You did well."

*Twice.*

"You did well," said Calder. "Twice. Three times. Kharrak."

---

Then the roof jumped under him.

From inside. Under the slates. A blow. Heavy, deep, a thud he felt through the lead of the ridge into the bones of his legs. The slates on both sides of the roof jumped and rattled and slid. Somewhere a window broke, a long way off along the front.

Then a sound. From inside. Through the roof. Far down. Shouting.

The yearling lifted her head.

Kharrak was very still on the ridge behind him.

*The bald one,* he said. *He's not on the stone any more. He's inside.*

Calder looked at the terrace. At the edge of the broken place where the big man had stood with his hands out and his coat smoking. Nobody there.

*He's inside,* said Kharrak. *With the ones who went in. I can smell them through the roof.* He turned his head. Along the ridge. Toward the far end, toward the hill end, toward the tall window. *And there's something else. Something old. Old as the cold thing. A bird.*

Calder thought of Tasso. Sitting in the snow in his fleece with the frost in his hair. *They go to the library. To the old bird. For a paper.*

*Inside,* said Kharrak. *The old bird.*

"Go," said Calder.

---

Kharrak went off the ridge.

Down the slope of the roof, the slates going under his feet, and off the edge, and his wings opened. He went along the front of the Library low, past the great door, past the smoking baskets, past the tall windows one after another, toward the far end, toward the hill end, toward the great window under the place where the yearling lay. Fast. As fast as Calder had ever felt him go.

The yearling watched him go.

Calder sat astride the ridge with his hand an arm's length from her and didn't move.

He looked down.

He didn't mean to. He was looking for Kharrak. But from the ridge at the hill end he could see the whole of the city below him, the drowned lower city along the shore, the brown water in the streets, the roofs with people on them. And down by the shore, at the low end of the lower city, behind what had been a market, a wall.

Grey. Stone. Across the mouth of a street. Fifty paces of it, the height of a house, with the brown water leaning on the lake side of it and the street behind it dry. A street with houses, and people in the windows.

At its foot, lying on its side, a lion. Grey. Bigger than he remembered.

On top of it, a small figure. Sitting.

The water leaned on the wall. The wall didn't move.

It held.
