---

He went in by the readers' bridge, because the great door was half a league away and he had a key.

Up Ferry Street through the wreck. Up Rope Street through the frost, past the crowds on Candle Row, who didn't look at him. He kept his hands in his armpits. They'd gone from white to red and they hurt now, a deep aching hurt in every joint, as if he'd held them in snow-water for an hour. He couldn't have held a pen. He thought about that, climbing. He couldn't have held a pen.

The College of Tides stood on the hill's south shoulder, below the Library, with a covered bridge from its fourth floor into the Library's upper gallery at the middle of the hall's length. The Tides readers used it in winter, so as not to go out into the snow. He'd used it for eleven years.

The College was empty. Every lamp white. The soft blue of the Tides' lamps gone to a white so hard it hurt to look at. He went up the stairs and along the corridor and the floor hummed under his boots again, wild, uneven, not the steady full hum of the stir but a shaking, like a drum being beaten by someone who didn't know how. The surge. He'd felt it every day for five days and he still wasn't used to it.

The bridge's door was locked. He got the key out with his teeth.

---

The Long Library was empty.

He'd never seen it empty. In eleven years, at every hour, there'd been someone at the tables. Now there was no one. Chairs pushed back. Books open on the tables, and books on the floor where they'd fallen when the hill shook. A reader's spectacles. A cup of something, frozen. Every lamp along the half-league of the hall burning white, and the tall narrow windows white with the afternoon, and the frost.

Frost on the shelves. On the spines of the books. On the floor, in a fine white fur along the joints of the old stone. He stood at the gallery rail and looked down the length of the hall toward the hill end and the frost went away from him in two white lines along the bottoms of the shelves, as far as he could see.

He went down the gallery stair and along the hall toward the hill end. Fast. Not running. He didn't run in the Library. Nobody did. His boots left prints in the frost.

He heard them before he saw them.

Voices. Far down the hall. Men's voices, low. And then a voice he knew better than his own mother's, a voice like dry pages turning, answering.

He ran.

---

He stopped at the last of the shelves, where the hall went into the hill and the air was cold and smelled of stone, and looked out between them.

The perch of black oak. The reading-desk at its foot, where he'd sat for eleven years. The great window behind, taller than the rest, white.

Men in front of the perch. A dozen. In grey and brown, hunters' coats, with crossbows. One of them sitting on the floor against a shelf with his coat burned to rags down one side and his face blistered, rocking. Two of them holding a cage between them on poles. An iron cage, bars as thick as his thumb, the kind they kept hawks in at the Blue College's mews, but larger. Big enough for a large dog. Or a large bird.

A man in front of them all, a little apart, with his hands behind his back. A grey beard. A plain coat.

And on the floor at the foot of the perch, the Old Reader.

It was standing on the case.

He saw that first. The dark-wood case, bound in tarnished silver, with the old mark of his college on its lid, the wave over the glass. Lying on the stone at the foot of the perch. And the bird standing on it, with its feet on the silver, its grey-black feathers lifted all over its body so that it looked twice its size, and its one good eye on the man with the grey beard.

Behind it the carved panel at the back of the perch's foot was open, and the niche empty. It had taken the case out itself. When it heard them coming down the hall. It had climbed down off its perch and opened the panel and taken out what it had kept for three hundred and four years and stood on it.

---

"...the College of Tides' contract," the man with the grey beard was saying. Politely. Like a man asking for a book at the door-end tables. "With the lord of the river hill. And its appendix. That's all. Nothing else in the hall. You can keep the rest."

"I know what you want," said the Old Reader.

"Then you'll know I've come a long way for it."

"Everyone has come a long way," said the bird. "Everyone who has ever come to this hall has come a long way. I have kept that case for three hundred and four years. In all that time, nobody has asked for it." Its feathers didn't settle. "You are the first."

"I'm asking."

"And the answer is no."

The man with the grey beard looked at it for a moment.

"It wasn't a question," he said. Almost gently.

He nodded to the two with the net.

---

They threw it well. Idony would remember that. They'd done it before. A weighted net, round, the size of a cart-sheet, with lead balls round its edge. They threw it from either side at once, so that it opened in the air over the bird like a hand closing, and came down.

The bird opened its wings.

He'd never seen it open them. Not fully. Not in eleven years. They went out from its body in a great ragged black-grey spread, wider than the reading-desk, wider than a man's arms, the long flight-feathers stiff and frayed at their ends, and the net came down on them.

And slid off.

Off the feathers. As if the feathers were oiled glass. The net touched them and slid and fell in a heap on the stone round the bird's feet, and the lead balls rang on the floor, and the bird folded its wings again and stood on the case.

One of the men swore.

"Old things," said the man with the grey beard, looking at the net. "In a flood. Everything's full today. Even feathers." He didn't sound angry. He sounded like a man noting the weather. "Bring the cage."

They brought the cage. They put it down in front of the bird with its door open. They waited.

The bird looked at the cage. It looked at it with its good eye. Then it looked up at the man with the grey beard.

"No," it said.

Behind it the great window stood white and tall, with the sky behind it. A bird could have gone through it. A bird could have opened its ragged wings and gone up off the case and through the glass and out over the drowned city and the broken lake and the mountains and away. Idony saw the men see it too. Saw two of them lift their crossbows toward the window, ready.

The bird didn't go.

---

He walked out from between the shelves.

He didn't decide that either. Twice in five days. He felt his feet go, out of the shadow of the last shelf, across the open stone in front of the perch, past the men with the crossbows, who turned, past the man with the grey beard, who turned. To the bird. And turned round, and stood in front of it, with his back to it and his raw red hands at his sides.

He could feel it behind him. The cold of its feathers against the backs of his legs.

The man with the grey beard looked at him.

"Reader Vael," he said.

Idony didn't say anything.

"The tide-reader. The one who left the ring." The man looked at him with something that might have been interest. "There's talk of you on the hill. They've been counting the dead up there since noon on the hundred-and-seventy-second, and they say your name between every one."

"Who are you?"

"A hunter," said the man. "Hired." He looked at the case under the bird's feet, past Idony's legs. "I was hired to fetch a paper. I didn't know till I came here that I'd been hired to fetch a reader too. But I think I have."

---

"He'll need someone who can read it," said the man with the grey beard.

He said it reasonably. Like a man laying out the terms of a sale.

"My master. The man who pays for all this." He moved a hand, a small movement, at the hunters, the cage, the net, the crossbows. "He knows what's in that case. He was told. He's wanted it a long time. But he can't read it. I've been told that too. Nobody can read it but your oldest masters, and they won't. And one Second-ring reader of the College of Tides who was seen at a reading-desk at the foot of this perch for a whole night this summer with his palm on the vellum." He looked at Idony. "Who's read it. Who's got it in his head."

Idony said nothing. He felt the bird behind him. Very still.

"My master pays better than your college," said the man. "He doesn't pay in rings. He pays in coin."

He took one out of his coat and put it on the reading-desk at the foot of the perch. A new silver coin, bright, unworn. Ship on one side, wave on the other. A burr on the edge. And in the rim, struck in the die, small, a ring of six.

Idony had never seen one. He'd heard of them. On the road south from the Ring to the Moot, Maren had told him about a ring of six on a door under Greyhold and on a coin in a stranger's hand, and a name that went with it. She'd said the name once. Flatly, with her eyes on the road ahead. The man who'd paid for the gate.

"Corvin," he said.

The man with the grey beard didn't say yes. He didn't say no. He looked at Idony with that same mild interest.

"Come with us," he said. "Read it for him. You'll never want for anything. You can measure every tide in Winshire."

"No," said Idony.

---

The man with the grey beard nodded.

Not to Idony. Past him. To the side. To the end of the hall where the floor of the lowest halls had broken open into the Undercroft stair, where a man was coming up out of the dark.

Big. Bald. A heavy jaw. His coat burned black from the cuffs to the elbows and his hands below it grey, not dirty, grey, the colour of ash. He came up the last steps out of the Undercroft onto the floor of the hall, slowly, and stood with his feet apart on the old stone.

Idony knew what he was. He'd seen Wardens stand like that on the ring-stones in the grey. He'd seen Pask.

"Step aside, Reader," said the man with the grey beard. "He's only got one more in him."

Idony didn't step aside.

The big man put out his grey hands.

---

He didn't feel it hit.

He felt the floor go. Out from under him. And then he was in the air, and the shelves were going past on either side, the spines of the books, the white lamps, all of it going past fast in a long blur, and the cold air in his face, and no sound at all. He thought: I'm being thrown. He thought: the bay is thirty paces. He thought, absurdly, of a table of readings, and how far a body of his weight would go under a blow of that kind, and whether anyone had ever measured it.

Then the reading-desk.

At the far end of the bay, thirty paces from the perch, where the shelves opened out and an old reader had sat every day for forty years under the third window. He went into it. Back first. The desk went over and he went over with it and the floor came up and hit him and everything went white, and then dark, and then white again.

He heard the slates jump on the roof. Far up. A long rattle, all along the hall.

He lay on the floor among the books and the broken desk and couldn't breathe.

---

When he could see again, the big man was down.

On both knees, on the old stone by the Undercroft stair. With his grey hands in front of him on the floor, palms down, and smoke coming off them. Thin. Grey. Going up from the backs of his hands in the white lamplight like smoke off a snuffed candle. His head hung. He didn't get up. Idony didn't think he was going to get up again.

He'd drawn on the floor of the Long Library in the surge. On the old stone, full, brimming, wild. And it had gone through him as it had gone through Pask on the stone at the Ring. All of it, like a river through a sluice that someone has opened too far.

Idony got onto his elbows.

He looked down the bay. Thirty paces. The perch, the black oak. The bird on the case at its foot. The men round it.

He saw the man with the grey beard lift his hand.

He saw three crossbows come up.

"No," he said. He didn't have the breath for it. It came out as nothing. "No..."

---

They shot it from six paces.

Three bolts, together. He heard them go. A flat hard sound, three at once, like three doors slammed.

The bird didn't move off the case.

He saw them hit. In the breast. Where the grey-black feathers were thickest. It didn't open its wings this time. Perhaps it couldn't. Perhaps an old thing's feathers could turn a net in a flood and not a bolt. Perhaps it didn't try. It rocked back on its feet with the blows. Then forward again.

Then it sat down.

On the case. Slowly, carefully, folding its legs under it, as it settled on its perch at night, with its feathers coming down, all of them, flat, smooth, settling against its body for the first time since he'd come out from between the shelves. It sat on the dark-wood case with the three bolts in its breast and looked down the bay at him with its one good eye.

He crawled.

On his elbows. Over the books. Over the frost on the floor. He couldn't get up. Something was wrong in his back, or his chest, and his legs wouldn't come under him, so he went on his elbows, thirty paces down the bay, between the shelves, past the men, who let him. Nobody stopped him. He heard someone laugh, once, and someone else tell him to shut up.

He got to the foot of the perch.

The bird was still looking at him. Its eye was very bright. Black, with a ring of gold round the black that he'd never noticed in eleven years, because he'd never been this close to it.

"Ferryman," it said.

Its voice was the same. Dry pages. Very quiet.

"Ask," it said.

He didn't understand.

"Ask, ferryman." The good eye didn't leave his face. "Ask everything."

Then it put its head down on its breast, among the bolts, the way it slept, and it didn't lift it again.

---

He knelt there.

He didn't know for how long. He knelt on the stone at the foot of the perch with his raw hands on the floor on either side of the bird, not touching it, and looked at it. Its feathers were very still. The frost was coming on them already, a fine white fur at the ends of the ragged flight-feathers.

The man with the grey beard bent down and lifted it.

Off the case. He lifted it in both hands, carefully, as if it might break, and Idony saw his face change for a moment at how light it was. Nothing. A bundle of feathers and old bones. Three hundred and four years and more, and it weighed less than a cat. The man laid it down on the stone beside the case, on its side, with its head on its breast.

Then he picked up the case.

He opened it. Standing there, at the foot of the perch, with Idony kneeling beside him. To be sure. He lifted the silver-bound lid and looked in. Idony saw the vellum. Brown, soft as cloth. The roll, and under it, folded, the appendix. He saw the man's eyes go over the first lines.

The man with the grey beard looked at the hooked script for a long moment. He turned the case a little in his hands, as if the letters might make more sense from a different side. They didn't. Idony watched him look at it as anyone in Winshire would have looked at it, but two old men in the Sixth-ring house and one Second-ring reader of the College of Tides.

He shut the lid.

---

And Idony understood.

Kneeling there, on the cold stone, with the bird on its side beside his knee and his hands burning, he understood it all at once, as he understood a chart when the last reading went in and the curve came clear.

The man who'd paid for the gate had the chain-craft now.

The contract. The forms for six chains, every link bearing on its inner curve the rune of holding. The charm for many voices. And the appendix: the rune drawn stroke by stroke, and the working-words that went with it. Everything Hearthmere's first knights had been sold three hundred years ago to hold the thing under the river. Everything that still lived in the broken chain-ends on the Titan's legs, saying *hold*, feeding on what they held, so that whoever could speak to them had a hand on it.

He had it all.

And he couldn't read a word.

He'd need a reader. Somebody who could read the Sixth-ring script. Somebody who'd read this roll. Somebody who had the rune and the working-words in his head, unwritten, because a bird had told him to keep them there.

There was one.

He was kneeling on the floor of the Long Library with no ring of any use to anyone and his hands too burned to hold a pen.

---

The great window came in.

All at once. A sound like the whole sky breaking. The tall window behind the perch, the tallest in the hall, its glass and its lead and its stone mullions, came in over them in a great glittering sheet, white with frost and light, and something came through it.

Red. Huge. Wings filling the end of the hall.

The red dragon came down on the floor of the Long Library among the shelves with the broken glass still falling round it and the shelves going over on either side of it like dominoes, books going everywhere in a storm of pages, and roared. The sound filled the hall from end to end. Half a league of it. Every lamp shook.

He'd read it once before, on a green glass at this end of the hall: two coming over the Fells, close together.

It was too late.

He knew it before the dragon did. He saw it turn its great head along the bay, looking, its nostrils wide. Looking at the perch. At the bird on its side at its foot. At him.

The hunters were gone.

They'd gone while the window was still coming in. Out by the readers' door at the hill end, the small low door under the great window that let onto the outside steps. The man with the grey beard first, with the case under his arm. All of them. Running. He'd heard their boots.

The dragon turned in the hall.

It couldn't turn fast. The hall was wide for a hall and narrow for a dragon. Its tail took down another shelf. Its wing hit a lamp and the lamp burst white. It turned, and went back the way it had come, up over the broken desk and the fallen shelves and out through the shattered window in a crash of lead and stone.

Idony got up onto his knees and looked out through the broken window.

On the steps below, three dragons. The black. A red, young. And on the terrace beyond, a bronze, lying slack with its head down, until a hunter ran to it and climbed onto its neck and laid a hand on it, and it lifted its head and its eyes stayed empty.

They went up. All three. Off the steps and the terrace, with the hunters on their backs, and the man with the grey beard on the black with the case held against his chest. Up over the towers. South-east. Fast. Toward the Fells.

The red dragon went after them, out of the window, over the steps.

He watched it go up after them over the towers. He watched it chase them till they were small. He watched it turn, a long way off, over the mountains, and come back.

---

He sat on the floor by the bird.

He didn't do anything. He couldn't think of anything to do. He sat with his back against the black oak of the perch and his burned hands in his lap and the bird beside his knee, and the frost coming down out of the broken window, and the white lamps burning the whole length of the hall.

The red dragon came back to the window and put its forefeet on the broken sill and stood there, filling it.

A man climbed down its leg.

Down onto the sill, and over it, and down onto the floor of the hall among the broken glass. A big man. Not tall. Broad. Fair hair, uncut, matted with frost. A farmer's hands, and a farmer's shoulders, and a coat full of things, heavy in the pockets. His shirt was torn open at the neck, and Idony saw, in the white light, on the left side of his chest over the heart, three marks. One under another. Bronze. Rust. Green. Like bruises that would never fade.

He came across the floor to the perch and stopped and looked at the bird.

Then he knelt down beside it. On the other side from Idony. He didn't look at Idony. He looked at the bird, on its side, with its head on its breast and the three bolts in it and the frost on its feathers.

He didn't touch it either.

"I know," he said. Very low. Not to Idony. Not to the bird. To something nobody else could hear. His head was a little on one side, as if he were listening. "I know. I know."

Behind him, in the broken window, the red dragon put its great head down on the sill and shut its eyes.

---

A clerk came down the hall from the far end, running, in a torn grey gown. A young one. He stopped at the last shelves and saw the dragon in the window and the farmer and the bird, and couldn't speak.

"Go down to the shore," said Idony.

His own voice surprised him. It was steady.

"Behind the fish-market. Dye Street. There's a wall across the end of it. A woman with a lion. Tell her..." He stopped. "Tell her I said come up."

The clerk looked at him.

"Tell her it's the Old Reader," said Idony. "She'll understand."

The clerk went.

The farmer was still kneeling by the bird. He'd stopped saying *I know*. He was just kneeling there, with his big hands on his knees.

Idony looked at him across the bird.

"It said please," Idony said. "Once. To me. It said please." He didn't know why he was telling a stranger. "Three hundred years in this hall and it said please once."

The farmer looked at him for the first time.

"What did it say just now?" he said. "At the end. The dragon says it said something."

"Ask," said Idony. "It said ask."
