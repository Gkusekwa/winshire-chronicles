They went down to the shore because they needed a floor.

It was Idony who said it, on the bottom step by the coals, with the farmer's question still in the air between them. *An old one. With stones.* He'd looked at Calder for a long time, bent over to one side with his burned hands in his armpits and a little grey bag in one of them, and then he'd said:

"How big is it?"

"The road?"

"What you've got in your head."

Calder had thought about it.

"Big," he said. "Bigger than a table."

"Then we need a floor," said Idony.

---

The lake had gone back.

From the hill in the dark Calder couldn't see it. When they came down through the drowned lower city with a rushlight, past the wrecked fish-market and out onto the shingle by the old quay, he saw what the water had left.

A plain. Flat and white, going away into the dark. Where the lake had been that morning, mud and shingle and weed and wreck, a league of it at least, lying under a finger of frost that the cold had laid on everything it passed. In the starlight it was white as a sheet. Smooth. You could have written on it.

He walked out onto it a little way. The frost crunched. Under it the mud was frozen hard.

"That'll do," said Idony, behind him.

Kharrak came down onto the shore with the yearling at his heels and lay down at the edge of the white, and Calder dragged wreck-wood together, planks and a broken oar and the ribs of somebody's boat, and Kharrak breathed on it. It caught. The only fire on the whole shore of Glasswater. It threw their shadows out long across the frost.

The lion lay down on the far side of it, slowly, and put his chin on the stones.

---

Nessa came down with the chart.

Idony had sent the boy up for it. Up to the attic over the ferry-folk's boat-shed, where he'd lived, for a roll of oiled silk as long as his arm in a leather case. Nessa came down the steps from Ferry Street with it under her arm and the boy behind her with a lantern, and gave it to him, and stood at the edge of the firelight with her arms folded.

"Eleven years," she said. "Mind he doesn't drop it in the mud."

Idony unrolled a little of it on his knee by the fire. Calder saw lines. Hundreds of them. Fine black lines in a small hand, curving across a pale ground, crossing, bending, with numbers beside them so small he couldn't have read them if he'd been able to read.

"The College's chart," said Idony. "The great chart of the tides. Every reading the College of Tides has taken for three hundred years, on one map." He let it roll up again. "This is my copy. I've been correcting it since I was nineteen."

"What's it a map of?"

"The Deep," said Idony. "Where it runs."

---

Calder took Rook's knife out of his coat and tied it to the end of a stick with a bit of twine from his pocket.

He didn't know how else to do it. He'd never drawn anything bigger than a field on a slate for his father. He stood at the edge of the frost with the stick in his hands and shut his eyes, and found it.

It wasn't words. It never was with her. She'd given it to him on the setters' ledge at Varrhold in the summer, on the long evenings when she couldn't fly and lay with her wing in its splints and her chin on the warm stone, and he sat beside her, and she remembered. Not on purpose. It came up out of her like things out of a pond when you stir the bottom. A piece at a time. And when it came, she opened it, and he saw.

The white bridge. He'd had that since the Wendwood. The road going over it in the sun, and the hand on every milestone. *Nineteen to the bridge. From both ends.*

But in the summer she'd remembered the far end of it.

---

"There was a round place," he said.

He didn't open his eyes. He could feel them listening. Idony by the fire. Maren on the far side of it, by the lion. Nessa with her arms folded.

"At the end of the bridge. On the island. Before the tower. A round paved place, wide, like a market. With the tower on one side of it." He saw it. He saw it from above, as she'd seen it, coming in to land, low, with the river shining on both sides. "And the roads came into it."

"The road," said Idony. "North and south."

"No," said Calder. "Six."

He opened his eyes.

"Six roads," he said. "Into the round. Not two. She remembered it in the summer. She walked the one road with him, north and south, so she'd only remembered that one. But when they came into the round there were six. Going out. Like a wheel." He held up his hand with the fingers spread. Then he put it down, because it was the wrong number. "And over the mouth of every one there was a milestone. The first. The same mark on all six, on the back where you'd see it as you came in. And he put his hand on all six of them. Going round. Every time they crossed."

He could feel it. The hand on the stone. Warm. *As if he were greeting it.*

"She knows which way two of them went," he said. "One north. To the cold, she said. One south. To the sun. She doesn't know the others. She never walked them." He looked at the frost. "But she knows the shape. From the air. She knows the angles. Like you'd know the shape of your own field."

---

He drew it.

He walked out onto the frost to a place twenty paces from the fire and drew a circle. The round place. He scored it into the frost with the point of the knife, round, the width of a cart. He drew the river through it. A long line from top to bottom, wavy, splitting round the circle and joining again below it. The island, where the Wend split.

Then the roads.

He didn't think. He let her picture sit in his head and walked out of the circle along the first of them, north, with the knife scraping a line behind him in the frost, white on white, forty paces, and stopped, and made a mark. Then back to the circle. Then the second. South. Forty paces. A mark.

Then the others. He didn't know where they went. He only knew the angles. He walked them out from the circle one at a time, the knife hissing in the frost, forty paces each, and put a mark at the end of each one where he guessed, and came back.

The fifth one went a little way south-east, and then turned east and ran along. He didn't know why. That was the shape.

The sixth was short.

He stopped after a few paces. He stood there with the stick in his hands, looking at it. It went out of the circle down the river, along the line he'd drawn for the Wend, south, a little way. Then it stopped.

"That's all of it," he said. "That one. It's short. She says it's short."

He came back to the fire. His hands were cold. The frost-map lay out on the shore in the firelight, a circle with a river through it and six lines going out from it like the spokes of a broken wheel, white scratches on white, and the fire threw their shadows across it.

---

Idony had got up.

He came out onto the frost with the chart under his arm and a stick to lean on, walking bent, slowly, and stood at the edge of the circle and looked at the six lines going out into the dark. He didn't say anything for a long time.

Then he knelt down.

It hurt him. Calder saw it. He went down on his knees in the frost by the circle with a sound in his throat and stayed there a moment with his eyes shut. Then he unrolled the chart. On the frost. Carefully, with his burned hands, a little at a time, weighting the edges with stones from the shingle as he went, until it lay flat beside the circle, as long as a man.

He looked at it. At the frost-map. At the chart.

"Where's the island?" he said. "On this. Exactly."

Calder pointed at the circle.

"No. On the chart." Idony put his finger on the silk. A place where the Wend split. "Here. Is that it?"

Calder looked. He couldn't read the words. He could read a river.

"That's it," he said.

---

Idony took the chart up again and turned it.

Round. Slowly. On the frost. Like a man turning a map to match the ground, which Calder supposed he was. He laid the island on the chart over the circle in the frost and turned the whole silk round it until the river on the chart lay along the river Calder had drawn.

Then he sat back on his heels and looked.

Calder looked too. He didn't know what he was looking at. The lines on the chart. Hundreds of them. Fine black curves, bending, crossing, with little numbers. And under them, through the silk, faint in the firelight, his own six white scratches in the frost.

"The College has marked six places," said Idony. His voice was very quiet. "For three hundred years. Six places where the glass stands higher than the country round it, and nobody knows why. We call them anomalies. Every first-year draws them. Every master has a theory." He put his finger on the chart. Out along the first frost-line, north-east. A small mark in red ink. "The Hollow Ring."

His finger went to the next. North. Up toward the top of the chart.

"Vael," he said. "Under the Library hill."

West.

"Orrin. In Kessmoor. The Scald."

South. A long way.

"The Sun Stair. In Sarovel."

South-east, and then along the coast, east.

"A reef. Off the Saltreach coast. The fishermen won't go near it."

His finger came back. Down the river from the island. A little way. To a red mark so close to the circle that Calder hadn't seen it.

"The river hill," said Idony. "Under Greyhold."

He took his finger off the chart.

---

"Look at the lines," he said.

Calder looked.

"Not mine. The College's. The tide-lines. Where the Deep runs." Idony put his hand flat on the silk and moved it, slowly, along one of the great black curves that went out from the island toward the Ring. "Every reading for three hundred years, on one map. And the lines go between the six. Like rivers between lakes." His hand moved to the next. Toward Vael. "And they all go in. To one place. A place the College never marked, because there's nothing to mark. The glass there doesn't go up and it doesn't go down. It just stands. Dead still. The only place on the whole chart where the tide doesn't move at all."

His hand was on the circle. On the island.

"Under the Old Ruin," said Idony. "Under the bridge."

He took his hand away.

Under the silk, through it, Calder could see his own white scratches. The six frost-roads. Going out from the circle at the angles he had from her.

They lay along the lines.

Each of the six white scratches in the frost lay under one of the great black curves on the chart, from the circle out, as if one had been drawn from the other. The north road under the line to Vael. The south road under the line to the Sun Stair. The one that went south-east and turned and ran east along, the one he hadn't understood, under the line that went down to the coast and along it to the reef. All six.

The short one, down the river, under the short line to the river hill.

---

"They built where it runs," said Idony.

He said it to the chart. Not to anyone.

"The old realm. The road. They didn't build it where it was easy. They didn't build it from city to city. They built it where the Deep runs. On the lines." He touched the silk. "And the bridge in the middle. Where it all goes in. And at every end, a temple. Over a sleeper." He was quiet. "And every milestone counting in. Toward the middle."

"Nineteen to the bridge," said Calder.

"From every end."

Nobody said anything. The fire cracked behind them. Out on the frost the lion had lifted his head.

"I've looked at this chart every day for eleven years," said Idony. "I've drawn those six marks a thousand times. I never once saw a road."

"I've never seen your chart," said Calder. "I can't read it."

Idony looked up at him.

"No," he said. "And I've never seen your road. And neither of us would have seen it without the other one." He looked down at the silk and the frost. "Neither of us had it alone."

---

"The short one," said Calder. "Down the river."

"Hearthmere's." Idony touched the red mark below the island. "The river hill. Where Greyhold is. That's the first sleeper. The one with wings. On the wall at the Ring." He didn't look at the lion. "It's been empty three hundred years. A lord broke the stone with a hammer and it woke slowly, over days, with the chains already on." He was quiet. "It's the one that's going round the others."

Calder thought of the Ruin. The lock. The roof coming down on Aurveth, and a lion holding it.

"Going round them," he said.

"In order." Idony put his finger on the frost-map. Not the chart. The scratches. "It came out from under the river and went north-east. To the Ring. That's the first end." His finger went along. "Then here. Vael. The second." Along. "And five days ago, west. Along the snow-line. Toward the third." He stopped on the west road. On the mark at the end of it. "The Scald."

His finger went on, round the wheel, without touching it.

"Then south. Then the coast. Then..."

He stopped at the circle.

---

"Then the middle," said Calder.

"*Here the breath is kept,*" said Idony.

He said it in a voice Calder hadn't heard him use. As if he were reading it.

"It's carved round the dome under the Ring. In the old script. I copied it. I didn't know what it meant." He looked at the circle in the frost. At the dead-still place on the chart, where the tide didn't move. "It's here. Whatever it is. Under the bridge. Where all the lines go in and nothing moves." He looked up. "A held breath."

"What is it?"

"I don't know," said Idony. "Nobody knows."

Calder looked across the fire.

Maren was sitting by the lion with her dead arm in her lap. She was looking at the circle in the frost. Not at Idony. Not at him. At the circle. And her face had gone still, the way it went still when she knew a thing and wasn't going to say it.

He'd seen that face before. On the jetty at Greyhold. In the yard, the first night.

He didn't ask. She didn't say.

---

"There's a milestone on the High Crown," she said instead.

They both looked at her.

"On the herd's road. West of the pass. In a meadow that used to be water." She didn't get up. She pointed with her good hand, across the fire, at the frost-map. At the north road. "There. Somewhere about there. The old cow put her trunk on it. Waist-high. Leaning. With the marks on it worn off." She looked at the line. "We came along under the mountains. It's on that one."

Idony looked at the chart. At the line to Vael. At the place where it went under the High Crown.

"Yes," he said.

---

They sat by the fire after that and didn't say much.

It was the middle of the night. Calder fed the fire. The yearling had gone to sleep against Kharrak's side with her nose under his wing. Lisska was lying on Calder's feet, which she hadn't done since she was small enough to do it without breaking them.

"How fast is the water?" said Calder.

Idony looked up.

"The flood. That went down the valley this morning. With the pale thing." Calder looked south, into the dark, where the valley was. "How fast."

Idony thought about it. Calder watched him think. He did it like a man doing a sum on a slate, with his lips moving a little.

"By water," Idony said, "it's a long way down. The valley, and then the Wend. All the way down to the Marches." He thought. "Five days. About. To the border. A wall of it most of the way, and then less."

"And by dragon?"

"I don't know how fast dragons go."

*Under two,* said Kharrak, from the dark. *Less if he stops talking.*

"Under two days," said Calder.

He thought about the Marches. About Fisk on his roof. About Liss at the ferry house, and the jetty, and the Eelweir, and every steading along the river with its barley in. About the water coming down the Wend in five days with nobody knowing.

He thought about three young dragons going south-east over the towers with a man with a grey beard and a box.

"I can't catch three with two," he said. Out loud. To nobody. "Not today. They've got a day's start and I'd have to fight them all at once." He looked at the fire. "The water's going to Greyhold."

---

"We'll go west," said Maren.

She said it to the fire.

"To the Scald. On foot. He'll follow it." She looked at the lion. "He's been wanting to since this morning. He wouldn't go while the pale one was here."

"Kessmoor," said Idony. He said it as if it tasted of something. "They hang people for drawing. They jail them for saying a charm." He looked at his hands. "I've got no ring. I've got no college. I'll be a man with some glass in his pocket."

"Your glasses'll work," said Maren.

"Wherever it lies down. Yes."

Calder looked at the frost-map. At the west road, going out of the circle into the dark. At the mark he'd made at the end of it, guessing.

A race, he thought. Not a hunt. You couldn't hunt it. It went where it went, round the wheel, end to end, toward the middle. All you could do was try to get to the next end first. And then the one after that. And the one after that.

And then the middle.

---

He opened the pages before dawn.

He hadn't meant to. He'd meant to wait. For Brask, or Mira, or anyone who'd read them to him slowly in a room with the door shut. But the fire was there, and nobody was looking, and his hand went into his coat and found them behind the mint-book.

Eleven sheets. Folded. FOR THE FARMER, on the outside, in letters as big as his thumb.

He unfolded the first one.

She'd written big. She'd said she had. Big plain letters, black, pressed hard into the cream paper, so that he could feel them with his finger on the back of the sheet. Lines of them. Too many for him. He couldn't do lines. He could do words, one at a time, slowly, a letter at a time, as Mira had made him do at Starwatch.

He found one at the top. On its own. Bigger than the rest.

He put his finger under it.

E. N. N. I. S.

He said it. Under his breath. The way you'd say a word in a language you didn't know, to see what it felt like in your mouth.

"Ennis."

He knew the name. Aurveth had given it to him under the Ruin, the night the verse came back to her, with a face to go with it at last. But he'd only ever heard it. In her voice, at the back of his head. He'd never seen it. Somebody else had written it down. Somebody who'd never met her, in a city she'd never been to, had put it on paper in letters as big as his thumb, so a farmer could read it.

He thought of a hand. Flat on a white stone in the sun. Warm. *Nineteen.* Low and easy and pleased.

He folded the sheet again and put it back with the others, inside his coat, and put his hand on it.

She was too far to hear him. Aurveth. On the ledges at Varrhold, seventy leagues off, with her crooked wing. He couldn't have told her if he'd tried. He'd tell her when he got there. He'd sit beside her on the setters' ledge and take the pages out and find somebody to read them, slowly, and he'd show her the name. Written. Where anyone could see it.

He sat with his hand on his coat and didn't move till the sky went grey.

---

Maren was out on the frost when it did.

He saw her. Alone, at the edge of the map, in the grey light. Walking. Slowly, along the west road, with her eyes down on the white scratch in the frost. Her dead arm hanging. She went along it one step at a time, carefully, as if every step mattered, as if she were counting.

He watched her walk it to the end. To the mark he'd guessed. She stood there.

Then she turned and came back along it. Counting again.

---

They went at dawn.

Nobody said much. There wasn't much to say. Nessa had gone back up the steps in the night with the boy. The fire was ash. The frost-map lay out on the shore of Glasswater in the first light, a circle and a river and six lines, and the sun was coming up over the Fells and would have it gone by noon.

Idony rolled up his chart. He did it on his knees, slowly, with his burned hands, and Maren knelt and helped him with her one good one, and they didn't speak. He put it in its case. He got up with his stick.

The lion got up.

Taller than the sheds on the shore. He shook the frost off his mane in a white spray and stood looking west along the shore, toward the end of the lake and the road that went round it under the mountains. Toward the snow-line, where the Titan had gone.

Maren came over to Calder. Not close. A few paces off.

"The water," she said. "Greyhold."

"I know."

"Liss is there."

"I know."

She looked at him for a moment. Then she nodded, and turned, and went back to the lion, and put her good hand on his neck. And they went. West along the frozen shore, the lion and the woman and the bent man with the stick, getting smaller, toward the end of the lake.

Calder watched them go.

He didn't call after them. They didn't look back. He'd known they wouldn't.

---

He got up onto Kharrak's neck.

Lisska went up first. Quick, green, into the grey light, turning. The yearling stood up off the stones and looked at Kharrak, and at Lisska in the air, and at him, and spread her small red wings.

*She'll follow,* said Kharrak. *She doesn't know where anything is. But she'll follow.*

"That's enough to start with."

They went up off the shore of Glasswater. Over the frost-map, so that he saw it once from above as Aurveth would have seen it. A circle. A river. Six roads going out like spokes.

Then south. Over the broken lip of the lake. Down the drowned valley. Low over the brown water, going the same way it went, fast, to get ahead of it.
