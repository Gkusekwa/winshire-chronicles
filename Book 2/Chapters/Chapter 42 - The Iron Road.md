---

The country was dead all the way to the mountains.

She knew it before Idony told her. She felt it through Orrun, every step, like a floor going from boards to stone under bare feet. West round the end of the lake, west under the High Crown, along the foot of the snow on the old road where the herd had brought them in summer. Every step the same. Nothing in it. Ground. Stone that was only stone.

Idony took his glass out every evening and set it on a rock and looked at it, and it lay there with its quicksilver at the bottom of the bulb like a bead of lead.

"Dead," he said. Every evening. As if he had to say it to believe it.

Her arm came back on the first evening. She was sitting by the fire with it in her lap and it began to hurt, all at once, from the fingers up, as if somebody had poured hot water down the inside of it. She sat there with her teeth together and let it hurt. By the third day she could close her hand. By the fifth she could hold the cup.

Idony walked with a stick. He'd got it from Nessa, a broken boathook with the hook sawn off. He walked bent over to one side, slowly, and stopped every hour and stood with both hands on the stick and his eyes shut, and then went on. He didn't complain. She'd never heard him complain about anything but colleges.

Orrun went ahead and broke the way.

---

The snow came on the sixth day.

They'd turned south-west by then, off the High Crown road, up toward the saddle at the western end of the Fells where the road went over into Kessmoor. The pass. Idony knew its name in three languages and didn't like any of them. The snow came down on it in the afternoon, wet and heavy, out of a sky the colour of slate, and by evening it was over Orrun's knees.

He went through it like a ship. She walked in the channel behind him. Idony walked behind her, in her footprints, with his stick.

They went over the saddle on the ninth day in a wind that took the breath out of her mouth.

On the far side, halfway down, Idony stopped and took out his glass.

He set it on a flat stone in the snow. The quicksilver lay at the bottom of the bulb.

Then it moved.

A hair. Not more. It lifted off the bottom of the bulb a hair's breadth and stood there, trembling.

Idony looked at it for a long time.

"Kessmoor," he said. "It's live." He picked the glass up and held it in his burned hand. "Thin. Very thin. Like a hill farm. But live."

She felt it too, through Orrun's feet. Faint. A little give in the stone. Like a floor with something under it.

Orrun lifted his head and looked west.

"It is that way," he said. "Far. It is not lying down yet."

---

Idony marked it every night after that.

On the silk, in his small hand, with the pencil of Kessmoor graphite that he said was the one good thing Kessmoor had ever made. The draw. He set his glasses out on a stone in a ring, four of them, and watched which way the quicksilver leaned, and drew a line.

It wandered.

West, the first night. North-west, the second. Then back. Then west again. Over the moors at the head of the Coldrun, he said. Circling. Like a dog turning round and round before it lies down.

"It doesn't know yet," said Idony. "Or it knows and it's waiting."

"For what?"

"I don't know." He looked at the line. "At the Ring it came straight. At Vael it wandered first. Two weeks." He drew the line on. "It wanders longer each time."

---

The first canal was at the bottom of the valley.

She saw it from above, coming down out of the snow on the eleventh day. A town at the head of the river where the Coldrun came down out of the moors in a white rush and ran into a lock. A long straight cut of grey water going away west between brick banks, as straight as a rule, with towpaths on either side, and lock-gates every half-mile stepping it down the valley like stairs. Brick lock-houses. Iron bridges over the cut that lifted, Idony said, so the barges could pass. Chimneys. Smoke.

"Coldrun Locks," said Idony. "The top of the canals."

She'd never seen a canal. She'd seen the Wend, and the Mere, and the ditches in the Marches that drained the water-meadows. Not a river that somebody had straightened and put in a box.

At dusk the lamps came on in the town below. Green. A soft, sick green, in glass globes on iron posts along the towpaths and the lock-sides.

"Is that a working?" she said.

"No." Idony looked at them. "That's the one thing they're allowed. It's not the Deep. It's a salt in water and a metal in the salt, and they light it with a spark. It burns green. It's very clever. Isvaren laughs at it."

---

The lock-keeper's house had a sign on the door.

Painted. Black letters on white, in the common tongue, big, and under them the same in letters she didn't know.

SPOKEN WORKINGS FORBIDDEN BY ORDER OF THE ASSEMBLY.
BEASTS OF POWER TO BE PAPERED AT THE GUILD-HOUSE.

She read it twice.

The guild-house was on the lock-side. A brick room with a counter and a clerk behind it, a young man with ink on his cuffs and a brass gauge on a chain round his neck, a little round case like a watch with a needle in it. He looked at Idony. Then out of the window at Orrun, lying on the towpath with his chin on the cobbles and the barge-folk keeping well away from him.

"Name," said the clerk.

"Idony Vael."

"Ring?"

Idony held up his hand. There was nothing on it. A pale band on the finger where something had been.

The clerk looked at the hand. He wrote something. Maren leaned over and read it upside down, because she could, a little.

*Measurer. Unringed. Isvaren.*

"If you've a gauge for the Bleed," said the clerk, "it's to be declared."

"The what?"

"The Bleed." The clerk said it as if he were saying *the damp*. "What comes up out of the ground. What they work with, up in Vael. Your gauges. Your glasses."

Idony looked at him for a moment. Then he took out the case with the four glasses in it and opened it on the counter.

"Isvaren instruments," he said. "Glass. For weather."

The clerk looked at them. He wrote it down. *For weather.*

---

He wouldn't paper the lion.

"No guild'll stand for him," he said. He said it reasonably. "A beast of power wants a guild to stand for it. Carters stand for horses. The Drovers' Guild for oxen. The Mines' for the pit-ponies and the ironbacks." He looked out of the window. "Nobody's going to stand for that."

"He's not mine to stand for," said Maren.

"Then he's nobody's," said the clerk. "And nobody's beasts don't go down the canals." He shut the book. "You'll wait. I'll send to Ironhythe. They'll know what to do with you."

They waited.

---

The Assembly's man came the next afternoon.

By water. Down the canal from the west, against the current, on a long low iron boat with no sail and no oars and a chimney in the middle of it putting out black smoke. It came up the cut faster than a man could run, with a sound like a kettle, and slowed at the lock, and stopped.

A man got off.

Neat. In a grey coat and a waistcoat, with a brass gauge on a chain at his waistcoat pocket, like the clerk's but bigger. Forty, perhaps. Clean-shaven. A dry, careful face, as if he'd been told a great many things in his life and believed about half. Behind him six men in oilcloth coats with crossbows. The crossbows had marks on them. Little cut marks, along the stocks.

He walked along the towpath to where Orrun lay and stopped at ten paces and looked at him.

Then he looked at her.

"Inspector Harl Moss," he said. "The Assembly's Office of the Cordon." He didn't hold out his hand. "You're the woman from the Ring. With the lion. And the tide-reader from Vael."

She didn't say anything.

"It came down the canals with the salt-barges," said Moss. "A lion at the Ring, holding stakes in two places. A lion at Vael, raising a wall. A woman with it, and a man with glass." He looked at Idony. "And everyone in Kessmoor who's ever watched a gauge has been waiting to see which way you'd come."

---

"Why?" said Maren.

Moss took the brass gauge out of his waistcoat pocket and opened it and held it out to her.

A needle. On a dial. Numbers round the edge. The needle stood a little way up.

"I've carried one of these since I was nineteen," said Moss. "My father carried one. There are three hundred of them on the cordon round the Scald. We read them every hour. Every hour for forty years." He shut the gauge. "On the hundred-and-eighty-fourth day by your count, they began to climb. All three hundred. Slowly. They've been climbing since." He put it away. "Something's coming to the Scald. Or something under the Scald is waking up. Or both."

"Both," said Idony.

Moss looked at him.

"We've been waiting forty years for it to wake," he said. "We'd as soon not wait alone."

---

He papered Orrun himself.

On the lock-keeper's counter, in his own neat hand, on the Office's paper, with the Office's seal pressed into red wax at the bottom. Maren watched him write it. *A beast of the cordon. Kept on the Office's account. Not to be hindered.*

"There," said Moss. He blotted it. "The Office stands for him."

She looked at the paper. She thought of the lake gate at Vael, and the clerk with his columns. *Name. Beast. Gift. Reach. Mark.* She'd refused that. She'd said he wasn't a gift. She'd said he wasn't hers.

Now here he was. In a register. Kessmoor's.

"Write what you like," she said. "He won't read it."

Moss looked at her. Something moved in the dry face that might have been a smile.

"No," he said. "I didn't think he would."

---

They went down the canals in the sleet.

Nine days. On a long horse-barge sheathed in iron along the waterline, with a cabin at the back and a cargo of pig-iron under tarpaulins in the middle, and a big grey horse on the towpath pulling it, and a boy walking beside the horse. Moss's iron launch went ahead, smoking.

No barge would take the lion. He walked the towpath. On the far side from the horse, because the horse wouldn't go near him. Behind the barge, at his own pace, which was faster than the horse's, so that every hour or so he had to lie down on the towpath and wait for it to catch him up.

The sleet came down on the canal. Grey, and then white, and then grey again. It lay on the tarpaulins and on the towpath and on Orrun's mane and his back and didn't melt. It hissed on the water.

The canal went down the valley in steps. Lock after lock. The barge going into a brick box, and the gates closing behind it, and the water going out with a roar, and the barge going down with it, slowly, until the walls were high on either side and dripping, and the gates in front opened, and they went out onto the next pound a man's height lower than before.

Idony sat in the cabin with his glasses on the table and his back against the wall and didn't move for hours.

---

The country got blacker as they went down.

Not dead. She could feel it was live, a little, thin, through Orrun's feet on the towpath. But black. Chimneys. Spoil-heaps. Brick houses in rows, all alike, with black on their walls from the smoke. Engine-houses by the canal with tall chimneys and great iron wheels turning in their roofs, and a thudding from inside them that she felt through the water.

At one of them, on the fourth day, Idony came out of the cabin.

He stood at the rail in the sleet with his stick and one glass in his hand and looked at the engine-house going past. A big one. Brick, with four chimneys. Its wheel turning. The thud coming out of it.

"It's pulling," he said.

"What?"

"Something in there." He looked at the glass. The quicksilver in it was leaning. Toward the engine-house. Leaning, as it leaned toward the Titan when the Titan was near. "It's pulling like a draw. Like a drinking." He watched it. "But there's no Deep in it. None. The ground round it's not going dead. The glass is leaning toward it and there's nothing there."

The engine-house went past. The glass came upright again.

He went back into the cabin and wrote something down.

---

Ironhythe came up out of the smoke on the eighth day.

Brick. Iron. Smoke. A whole city of it, along the canal, on both banks, going up a hill. Locks and lift-bridges. Chimneys like a forest. Green lamps on every corner, though it was noon. And at the top of the hill a great square building of grey stone with a tower, and in the tower a clock, white-faced, with black hands.

"The Hall of Measures," said Idony. "Where the Assembly sits." He looked at the clock. "They say it's never been wrong."

"Has it?"

"Nobody in Kessmoor would tell you if it had."

They didn't stop at Ironhythe. Moss's launch took them through the city and out the other side, to a new cut going south-east, straighter than the rest, with a road beside it.

"The Scald Cut," said Moss, from the launch. "And the Iron Road. They go together to the border." He looked south-east, toward the sky over the moor, which was a strange colour. Yellow-grey, low down. "And past the Scald, if you want to go to Hearthmere. Nobody does."

---

The envoy got on at the last lock out of Ironhythe.

A man in a cloak of crown-blue, with the Hallowmere seal on a chain at his breast and two guards behind him in Hearthmere grey and a clerk with a ledger. He came aboard the barge as if it were his and went into the cabin and sat down at Idony's table and moved Idony's glasses to one side to put his ledger down.

Maren was on the towpath beside Orrun. She heard him through the cabin window. She didn't turn round.

"Chain," he was saying, to the barge-master. "For the crown's work. Twelve hundredweight from the Gannet forge, and the same from Holloway's, and they'll have to make the links bigger than they've ever made them, and they're telling me it's a year's work." He laughed, without pleasure. "A year. As if we've got a year."

"What's it for?" said the barge-master.

"The crown's work," said the envoy.

He didn't say anything for a while. Then:

"And I'll tell you what else. While I'm up here buying chain, there's a farmer in the Marches running about with dragons. Three of them, they say. An outlaw. Burned a Saltreach mint to the waterline. Flies up and down the river in broad day where anybody can see him." He thumped something. "And the Marches folk feed him. Feed him! The crown's put a bounty on his head and they feed him and hide him and tell the crown's men they've never heard of him."

"Wendmouth," said the barge-master. "I heard about Wendmouth."

"Everyone's heard about Wendmouth." The envoy's voice went sour. "Everyone's heard about the farmer. Nobody's heard of the crown."

Maren walked on along the towpath beside the lion, in the sleet, with her hand in his mane, and didn't turn round.

---

The country went dead again on the Iron Road.

It went over three days. She felt it going, through Orrun's feet, a little thinner every league. The give going out of the stone. Like walking out of a house into the cold.

Idony's glass began to lean on the first day out of Ironhythe. South-east. Along the cut. Toward the strange yellow-grey sky.

"It's lying down," he said. On the fifth evening. He had the glasses out on the table in the cabin, all four, and they were all leaning. "It's turned. It came south out of the moors four nights ago, and now it's lying down." He drew it on the silk. A line going south-east, and at the end of it, a small circle. "On the Scald."

The steam wagons came past on the road beside the canal. Iron, on iron rails, with fire in their bellies and black smoke coming out of their chimneys, pulling strings of carts loaded with coal and iron and things under tarpaulins. They went past faster than a horse could gallop and the ground shook.

Orrun watched them go with his ears back.

"They are not alive," he said. "They move and they are not alive."

"No."

"I do not like them."

---

They came to the cordon on the two-hundred-and-fourth day. She'd lost count on the canal. Idony hadn't.

A bank first. Earth, grassed over, the height of a house, going away on either side as far as she could see, curving. A ditch outside it. Huts on top of it, every few hundred paces, with smoke coming out of their chimneys and men in oilcloth on the bank between them, with crossbows. Forty years of it. The grass on the bank was old.

Moss took them through a gate in the bank.

On the far side, the cage.

---

She didn't understand it at first.

Water. That was the first thing. A canal. Running round the inside of the bank, wide, grey, smoking with cold. A grey haze lying on it, and frost on its brick edges, and the water in it moving, fast, running along between its banks in the same direction all the way round.

Beyond it, iron.

Pylons. Iron towers, each as high as three men standing on each other's shoulders, in a ring, a hundred paces apart, going round. With cables strung between them. Thick iron cables, sagging, from tower to tower, all the way round, out of sight.

Beyond the pylons, another canal. Grey. Smoking with cold.

Beyond that, another ring of pylons.

Beyond that, a third canal.

And beyond the third canal, low, under the yellow-grey sky, glass.

---

"Three canals," said a voice. "Two rings of pylons. Four engine-houses, one at each quarter. Forty-one leagues of cable." A pause. "And eleven thousand marks."

A woman had come out of the nearest hut.

Tall. Heavy in the shoulders. Sixty, or more. Grey hair cut short, like a man's. A leather coat, old, black at the cuffs. A pair of spectacles with smoked lenses pushed up on her forehead, and soot in the creases of her knuckles, so deep it would never wash out.

She looked at Orrun as a smith looks at a horse that's to be shod. Measuring.

"Agathe Pike," she said. "Master Engineer. Of the Cordon." She looked at the lion a moment longer. "He's bigger than they said on the barges."

"He grew," said Maren.

"Did he." Pike looked at him. "How much?"

"Two feet. At Vael."

"In how long?"

"A morning."

Pike didn't say anything. Maren saw her store it away somewhere behind the smoked spectacles. Two feet. A morning.

---

She showed them the cage.

All afternoon. Along the inner bank of the first canal, in the cold haze off the water, with Moss behind them and Idony limping on his stick. Pike walked fast and talked in numbers.

"The water's chilled," she said. "In the engine-houses. Run through coils and cooled till it's near ice, and pumped round. Never stops. Never has, not since the first cut was flooded." She looked at the water. "Running water breaks a draw. Your Wardens know that. Your colleges know it." She didn't look at Idony. "Cold running water breaks it harder."

The first ring of pylons. She put her hand on one. Iron, black, cold, rough. Up the face of it, from the ground to the height of Maren's head, marks. Struck into the iron. Square and spare, with little hooks at the ends of the strokes.

Maren knew them.

She looked at Idony. He was looking at the marks with his face very still.

"Marks," said Pike. "Engine-wright's work. Every pylon." She took her hand off the iron. "They hold the cables. The cables hold the field. The field holds whatever's in it."

"Runes," said Idony.

Pike looked at him.

"Marks," she said. "In Kessmoor we call them marks. Runes are spoken of. We don't speak of things."

---

"Since Orrin," she said.

They'd come to the top of a watch-tower at the north quarter, by the first engine-house. Iron stairs, inside an iron frame, up to a platform with a rail. The wind came across it from the south-east and smelled of nothing at all.

"Forty years," said Pike. "My father began it. He was a junior wright at Orrin. He was in the shaft-house the night it came up. He came out." She looked across the canals and the pylons. "Most didn't. Those who did went mad within the week. He didn't. He said it was because he'd been holding a gauge and looking at the needle and not at anything else." She was quiet. "He spent the rest of his life building this. I've spent mine finishing it. It was done four years ago."

"What's it for?" said Maren.

Pike looked at her. As if it were a strange question.

"For whatever comes up," she said.

---

She gave them the glass to look through. A long brass one, like a little of Esk's lens. Maren put it to her eye.

The Scald.

A basin. Wide. A league across, near enough. Low in the middle, curving up at the edges to the third canal, under the yellow-grey sky. And all of it glass. Fused. Black and green and brown, glinting, smooth in places and in places heaped and rippled like a pond frozen in a wind. Shapes in it. Lumps, and lines, and squares. She looked at the squares a long time before she understood them.

Streets. Walls. The footings of houses. A town, melted into the glass. Orrin.

Small things moving over the glass in the low light. White. Fluttering.

"Moths," said Pike. "Glass moths. Nothing else lives on it. Nobody knows what they eat. They ring, when they hit the glass. Like little bells. You'll hear them at night."

Maren moved the brass glass.

In the middle of the basin, lying down, the Titan.

Grey on the black glass. Head on its paws. Wings folded along its back like a roof. The broken chain-ends on its forelegs. Lying in the middle of dead Orrin as it had lain on the island at Glasswater, as it had lain on the stones of the Ring.

Breathing. Slowly. She could see it from here.

"Three days," said Pike. "It came down out of the north three days ago and lay down. The gauges have climbed a mark an hour since." She took the brass glass back. "The glass in the basin's warm. You can feel it from the third canal. It's never been warm."

---

Orrun had lain down on the outer bank.

Maren looked down at him from the tower. He'd gone along the top of the earth bank to the north quarter, past the cordon men, who'd got out of his way, and lain down on the old grass facing the basin, with his chin on his paws, as he'd lain on the shingle at Glasswater.

As close as three canals and two rings of iron would let him.

---

Pike turned to Idony.

"They tell me you can read when it'll go down," she said.

Idony looked at her. He'd taken out a glass. He was holding it in his burned hand, on the rail of the tower, and the quicksilver in it had climbed a little way up the throat and stood there.

"Yes," he said.

"How long?"

"I don't know yet. Days. Give me days of it." He looked at the glass. "A month. Perhaps less. It's quicker every time."

"Good," said Agathe Pike. "I'd like to know when to close it."

Maren looked at her.

"Close what?"

Pike turned and looked out over the three smoking canals and the two rings of iron towers and the forty-one leagues of cable, to the glass basin, where the Titan lay breathing in the middle of a dead town.

"The cage," she said. "On whatever comes up."
