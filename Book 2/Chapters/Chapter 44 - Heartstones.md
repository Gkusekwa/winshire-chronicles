---

The engine-house at the north quarter was as big as the Hall of Rings.

He'd seen it every day from the tower. A long brick building on the inner bank of the first canal, with four chimneys and iron doors and no windows, and smoke going up from two of the chimneys day and night. He'd felt it every day, too. The pull. The thing his glass leaned toward, as it had leaned toward the engine-house on the canal, as if something in there were drinking and there was nothing to drink.

On the two-hundred-and-sixth day Pike took him in.

"You want to read it," she said. At the tower's foot, in the morning, with her smoked spectacles down over her eyes against the glare off the basin. "I've seen you looking at the pylons. You read them like a page."

"I read them."

"Then you'd better read the core," said Agathe Pike. "Nobody else alive can."

---

The first hall was ordinary.

He'd seen engines before. In Vael, at the Blue College's mews, a little steam engine that pumped water up to the cisterns, which the Blue College was very proud of. These were bigger. Four of them, in a row, iron, black, with fire in their bellies and great arms going up and down and wheels turning, and a noise so loud that he couldn't hear Pike speak beside him. Men in leather aprons feeding them coal. Pipes going out of the hall through the wall.

"Pumps," shouted Pike, in his ear. "For the water. They run it through the coils and chill it. Coal. Nothing clever."

She took him through an iron door at the far end and shut it behind them, and the noise stopped.

---

The second hall was quiet.

Long, and high, and dim. Lit by the green alchemical lamps along the walls, their sick soft light. Cold. And in the middle of it, lying on its side on a bed of iron trestles, a drum.

Iron. Three men high, lying down. As long as a ferry. Black, and smooth, and ringed all round, from end to end, with bands of iron a hand's breadth wide. And on every band, marks.

Out of its far end, iron rods as thick as his arm went to the wall and through it. Toward the canal. Toward the pylons beyond, and the cables.

"The core," said Pike. "One at each quarter. This is the north."

He went up to it.

He didn't need to be told not to touch it. He stood a pace off with his stick and his hands at his sides and looked at the marks.

---

He'd thought they'd be like the pylons.

They were and they weren't. The same script. The hooked letters, square and spare. But closer. Cramped. Cut by men who'd learned it from a book, he thought, and then cut it ten thousand times, and got quick at it, and made it their own. Kessmoor's hand. A dialect. He'd never read it. He'd never known anybody wrote it.

He read it anyway.

Slowly. Band by band. Along the iron, from the near end toward the far. Pike stood behind him and didn't speak. He heard her not speaking. She'd have been a good reader in the Long Library, he thought. She knew how to wait.

The first band said *turn back*. Over and over. All the way round.

The second said *within*.

The third said *turn back* again, and the fourth *within*. And so on. Down the drum. Hundreds of bands. Thousands of marks. Two words. Over and over, all the way down.

---

"It doesn't hold it," he said.

Pike said nothing.

"The thing in the basin. Whatever comes up. This doesn't hold it. It's not a chain." He was looking at the bands. "It holds the Deep. In it. Whatever's inside the field, when it draws, the draw comes back on itself." He moved his hand, not touching. "Turn back. Within. It can't drink from anything outside the field. Only from itself."

"Only from itself," said Pike. "Yes."

"It'll starve."

"It'll stay where it is," said Pike. "Which is what a cage is for."

He looked at the drum for a long time.

It was very good. That was the thing he couldn't get past. It was very, very good. It was the best working he'd ever seen that wasn't old magic. Nobody in Vael could have made it. Nobody in Vael would have thought of it.

"What feeds it?" he said.

---

She took him round the end of the drum.

At its top, at the near end, a hopper. An iron funnel, like a mill's, as big as a cart. And from the hopper a chute went back, to a door in the wall behind. And the door was open.

He'd felt it from the first hall. Through the wall. His glass leaning in his pocket, toward this. The pull. The drinking with nothing to drink.

Through the door, a store-room. Long. Racks.

Grey plates on the near racks. Stacked like roof-slates, edge to edge. Each one as big as a door, thick as his hand, ridged on one face. Hundreds. He knew what they were. He'd seen them in the Bestiary at the College, drawn.

"Ironback," he said.

"Plate," said Pike. "The Mines' Guild breeds them. In the Fells. Herds of them. For this." She put her hand on one, as she'd put her hand on the pylon. "They shed it, a little. Not enough. Mostly it's cut."

From the dead ones. She didn't say it. He heard her not say it.

He went past the plates. To the back.

---

The shelves at the back were full of crates.

Wooden crates, with straw in them. Stencilled on the sides with the marks of Saltreach houses he knew from the College's lamp-crystal ledgers, and Kessmoor guilds he didn't. He looked in the nearest one.

In the straw, a stone.

Dark red. Almost brown. Glassy. As big as his fist. He bent and looked at it closer, and it was the shape of something. Lobed. With a stump at the top, cut off clean. The shape of a heart, gone to glass.

The next crate. Bigger ones. Two. As big as his head.

The next. Small. Dozens of them. Each no bigger than an egg.

"Hearts," said Pike behind him. "The Bestiary calls them heartstones. Certain beasts, when they die, their hearts set. Like this. If they're taken quick." She came and stood beside him and looked into the crate as he did. "It holds a store. Of what the beast was. You can burn it, like coal. The marks burn it. It goes grey. Then it crumbles. Then you put in another."

He couldn't stop looking at it.

"What beasts?"

"Ironback, mostly. Sabrecat. Stonehorn. Wyvern, when we can get them, the Saltreach houses sell those. Lynx." She looked along the shelves. "A few from over the river, from the Wyldmarch. The clans won't sell. Somebody does."

Lynx.

He thought of Dane's lynx, standing on the path in the sweet smoke at the Ring with nobody behind its eyes. Of the snow lynx on the roofs at Vael, going along the frosted slates with their noses down, finding the drowned.

---

"It doesn't touch the Deep," said Pike.

She said it with something in her voice that he'd heard in Quell's, once, when Quell talked about the roll. Pride.

"That's the point. That's the whole of it. It never touches the Deep at all." She looked at the drum through the door. "Your colleges draw on the ground. Your Wardens. Your chains under Hearthmere, whatever they were. They all drink from the same well as the thing they're trying to hold. And it drinks from it too. Harder. That's what it does. It drinks the ground dead and then there's nothing for your workings to drink." She put her hand on the crate. "This doesn't drink. This burns. It brings its own. The Titan can't take it. Dead ground can't starve it." She looked at him. "It'll hold on the deadest ground in Winshire. It'll hold on that." She nodded toward the basin, through the walls. "That's why my father built it here."

---

"How many?" said Idony.

"How many what?"

"Hearts. When you close it."

Pike thought about it, briefly. She knew. She just had to find the number.

"Four cores," she said. "When the field's closed, each core burns about a hundred and fifty a day. Big ones, ironback, sabrecat. More if they're small." She looked along the shelves. "Six hundred a day, near enough. We've got stores for forty days. The cranes are bringing more. There's a cart in every day from Ironhythe."

Six hundred.

He did the sum without meaning to. He always did the sum. Six hundred a day. Forty days. Twenty-four thousand. And more coming.

Twenty-four thousand beasts.

---

He asked.

He hadn't known he was going to. He'd stood in the Long Library for eleven years and not asked. He'd sat at the foot of the perch with his charts and his questions, every question but the one about the panel at the back of the perch's foot, and never once looked behind it. He'd read the noon oath in a contract and not asked who'd chosen the words. He'd stood at the centre of a hundred magisters while they said *and do not ask*, and not asked till the end.

*Ask, ferryman. Ask everything.*

"What does it cost?" said Idony.

Pike looked at him.

"Cost?" she said. As if he'd asked her the colour of the number nine. Then her face cleared. "Ironback plate's eleven silver the hundredweight, at the Guild's yard. Hearts are dearer. The Saltreach houses charge by weight. Forty to sixty the hundredweight, depending." She frowned a little. "It's on the Office's account. I don't see the bills. Moss does."

He looked at her.

"I didn't mean silver," he said.

She looked back at him. Behind the smoked spectacles her eyes were steady and clear and she didn't understand. He saw her not understand. He watched her look for what he meant, honestly, the way she'd look for a fault in a cable, and not find it.

"What else would it cost?" said Agathe Pike.

---

He went out and sat on the canal bank.

In the cold haze off the chilled water, with his stick across his knees. The pylons going away on either side of him in their long curve. The cables sagging between them. The basin beyond the third canal, glinting, black and green, and the grey shape lying on it in the middle.

He thought about Pike.

He thought: that's me. That's what I'd have been. If the bird hadn't said please. If it had died on its perch of old age with the case behind the panel and nobody asking. I'd have gone on reading. I'd have read everything. Every glass, every chart, every line in every book in the Long Library, and done every sum, and never once asked what any of it cost, because I'd never have thought it was the kind of question you asked.

He thought: she's very good. She's better than I am. She made a thing nobody in Vael could make.

He thought: she's never asked. Not once. Forty years.

He sat on the bank until the green lamps came on along the canal.

---

The stir had begun.

He'd known it would. He'd known it the day they came, with the glass leaning on the tower rail. But he watched it come, day by day, as he'd watched it at Vael, and wrote it down.

The basin's glass was warm.

He went to the inner edge of the third canal on the two-hundred-and-eighth day, where the brick ended and the fused glass of the basin began, and knelt, slowly, with his stick, and put his palm flat on it. Black glass. Smooth. Warm. Like a stone in the sun, except that the sky over the Scald was always the same low yellow-grey and the sun never came through it.

By the two-hundred-and-fifteenth day it was hot to his hand. He couldn't keep his palm on it longer than a count of ten.

The glass moths came thicker at dusk. White, in clouds, over the basin, so many that at the last of the light the whole basin seemed to be moving. They hit the glass and rang. Little high notes, like a tray of glasses being carried down a stair. All night. He heard them from the hut Moss had given him, lying on his cot with his back aching.

---

The Bleed came up in the ditches.

Not inside. Outside. In the ditch that ran round the foot of the earth bank on the outside of the cordon, in the ordinary ground, where the ordinary rain collected.

He walked round to it on the two-hundred-and-twelfth day because a cordon man told him to. The water in the ditch was warm. He put his hand in. Warm as bathwater. There was a shimmer on it, all along, like oil on a puddle, colours sliding over the surface. It smelled of hot metal. Of a forge, when the iron's in the fire.

Along both sides of the ditch the grass was dying. In a band a pace wide. Brown. Then grey.

"Forty years we've kept that ditch," said the cordon man. A grey old man with a gauge on a string. "Never been warm."

Idony set a glass down on the ditch's edge. The quicksilver went up and stood high and trembled.

The Bleed. Kessmoor's word for it. He'd thought it was a stupid word, when the clerk said it at the locks. *What comes up out of the ground.* He looked at the warm shimmering water and the dying grass and thought it wasn't such a stupid word.

---

Outside the cordon, the country was going dead.

Deader. It had been dead when they came. Thin, grey, the moor round the Scald where nothing much grew and nobody lived. Now it was deader. The cordon men's gauges showed it: high at the basin, high in the ditch, and then nothing, a league out, the needles down on the pins. He walked out a league on the Iron Road on the two-hundred-and-eighteenth day, slowly, with his stick, and set his glass on a milestone, a Kessmoor one, iron, with a number cast in it. The quicksilver lay at the bottom of the bulb like a bead of lead.

All of it going in. Toward the middle. As the lower city at Vael had gone dark while the hill blazed.

He went back and set out his four glasses on the tower rail in a ring, as he did every evening, and watched them all lean toward the middle of the basin. All four. Harder every day.

He drew it on the silk. The slope. He laid it beside Vael's.

Steeper.

---

He gave it a month.

He wrote it in the silk on the two-hundred-and-nineteenth day, in the smallest hand he had, first, before the numbers.

*A month. Perhaps less. Every time, less.*

At the Ring, three months. At Vael, seven weeks. Here, from the day it lay down, a month. Perhaps a little more than a month. The cup filling faster each time. The beast drinking faster. As if every relic it took made it thirstier for the next.

He took it to Pike on the tower.

She read the silk. She couldn't read his notation, but she could read a slope. She looked at it for a long time.

"When?" she said.

"Near the two-hundred-and-thirty-third day. That's when it'll go down for what's under the basin."

"And after?"

"Then the surge. Then the waking." He looked at the basin. "At Vael it was five days between. Here it may be fewer."

"Which day? For the waking."

"I can't tell you the day yet. I can tell you the week."

"The week," said Pike. She took her spectacles off and rubbed her eyes with the backs of her sooty knuckles. "That's more than my father ever had." She put them back on. "I'll want the day."

"When I have it."

"When you have it," said Agathe Pike, and went down the tower stairs to see about the carts.

---

He went down to the main gate on the two-hundred-and-fourteenth day because he heard Maren's voice.

The main gate was on the west of the cordon, where the Iron Road came through the earth bank. A gatehouse of brick, with a cordon man on it, and a desk under an awning, and a clerk at the desk with a book.

Maren was standing in front of the desk. Orrun was behind her, outside the gate, lying in the road with his chin on his paws and his eyes on the basin. A long way off over the bank and the canals.

The clerk was one of Moss's. Young, neat, with the Office's seal on his cap.

"It's for the cage's account," he was saying. Reasonably. "Every beast inside the bank goes in the book. By kind, and weight, and where it's kept. It's the Assembly's order. In case."

"In case of what?"

"In case," said the clerk.

"He's on your paper," said Maren. "Your Inspector wrote him on it. At the locks. With a seal." She didn't raise her voice. "That's enough."

"That's a travel paper, miss. This is a different book."

Idony looked at the book.

It was open on the desk. Ruled in columns. He could read it upside down, as Maren could. He read the headings across the top of the page. *Kind. Weight. Kept at. For.*

And under them, down the page, the entries. In the clerk's neat hand.

*Ironback. Forty hundredweight. North-quarter pens. Plate.*

*Ironback. Thirty-eight hundredweight. North-quarter pens. Plate and heart.*

*Sabrecat, wild. Six hundredweight. South-quarter store. Heart.*

He read three more. Then he stopped reading.

---

"No," said Maren.

The clerk looked at her.

"Miss, it's only..."

"No," said Maren. "He's not going in your book."

She didn't know what was in the other columns. Idony could see that. She hadn't read past the headings. She was refusing it as she'd refused the register at the lake gate at Vael, because it was a book with Orrun's name to go in it, and she didn't put him in books. *He's not a gift. He's not mine.*

She didn't know what *For* meant, at the top of the last column.

Idony did.

He didn't tell her. He stood behind her at the gate with his stick and looked at the neat young clerk, who had no idea either, he thought; who wrote it down because it was the Assembly's order, in case; and at the open book; and at the word at the top of the last column, in a clerk's tidy hand.

"She said no," said Idony.

The clerk looked at him. At the bare hand on the stick, with the pale band on the finger.

He shut the book.
