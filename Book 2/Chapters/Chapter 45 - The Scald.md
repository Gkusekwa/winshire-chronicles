---

The glass rang under their feet.

She'd thought Pike was speaking in pictures. *They ring, when they hit the glass. Like little bells.* She hadn't thought it was true. On the first night in the cordon she'd lain in the hut Moss had given them and heard it, a thin high ringing coming over the bank from the basin, on and on, like a hundred glasses touched with a knife, and thought she was dreaming.

Now she was on it.

On the glass. Out past the third canal, on the fused black floor of the basin, in boots wrapped in sacking and tied with string because Pike said the glass would scorch leather in a few hundred paces. And the moths came up round her feet as she walked. White. In little clouds. Startled up off the glass, fluttering, and coming down again, and every time one touched the glass, a note.

She walked in a ringing.

---

Pike had let them over the canals on the two-hundred-and-thirtieth day.

"While it's open," she said. At the north quarter, at the edge of the first canal, where a narrow iron bridge went over the smoking water on a pivot. "When the field's closed these swing aside. Nobody crosses. Nothing crosses." She put her sooty hand on the rail. "It's open now. Go and look at it, if you have to. Moss will write it down."

Moss wrote it down.

They crossed all three. The iron bridges, narrow, with the cold coming up off the water underneath so that her breath went white. Between the canals, the rings of pylons, humming now. She hadn't heard them hum before. A low steady note from the cables, like the wind in a ship's rigging. The marks on the pylons were dark. Pike said they'd stay dark until the field was closed.

On the far side of the third canal, the basin.

---

Idony rode.

She'd made him. He couldn't walk it. Half a league out across the glass to the middle, and half a league back, with his back the way it was and his stick sliding on the smooth places. She'd said she wasn't leaving him at the canal. He'd said he could watch from the canal with the brass glass. She'd said no.

So he rode. On Orrun's back, behind the shoulders, where the mane began, with both hands wound in the broken-rock mane and his stick across his knees and the brass glass on a cord round his neck. He sat very straight, or as straight as he could, and held on, and his face was grey.

"I've never," he said.

"I know."

"Nobody's ever..."

"I know," said Maren. "Hold on."

Orrun walked carefully. As carefully as she'd ever felt him walk. As if he were carrying something that would break. She supposed he was.

---

Orrin was in the glass.

She'd seen it from the tower through the brass glass. Squares and lines. She hadn't understood it until she walked on it.

The footing of a house. Under her feet. A square of stones, a hand's breadth high, melted into the glass round them so that they stood up out of it like the bones of a hand under skin. A doorway: two stones standing, and a third across them, fused. A well-head, round, its rope gone, its windlass gone, the stone ring of it sunk into the glass to the lip. A street. She walked along it. A street of doorways, on either side, in a row, with the glass between them smooth as ice. And in the middle of the street, flat, the iron tyres of a cart's two wheels, melted down into two black rings on the glass, a pace apart, where the cart had been standing when it happened.

Forty years.

She didn't look for anything else. She didn't want to see what else was in it.

---

The Titan lay in the middle of the basin with its head on its paws.

They came up to it at noon on the two-hundred-and-thirty-first day. The moths were thicker here. The glass was hotter. She felt it through the sacking and through her boots, a steady heat, like standing on a hearthstone. The air shimmered over it.

Orrun stopped at ten paces.

He lay down. As he'd lain on the grey grass at the Ring, in the summer, with the stones round them. With his chin on the glass and his eyes on it. Idony stayed on his back, holding the mane, and didn't speak.

She stood beside him.

She looked at it. So close she could see the stone of its skin, the old cracks in the plates, the moss in the cracks, dead now, brown. The broken chain-ends on its forelegs. Each link as thick as her body, iron, rusted black and red, hanging from the great bands round its legs, and the broken end of the last link lying on the glass beside its paw.

It didn't turn its head. It never had.

---

Orrun got up again.

She didn't expect it. She felt him decide, through the bond. A slow decision, like a stone tipping. He got his legs under him, with Idony clutching his mane, and stood.

And went the last ten paces.

She went with him. She didn't decide to. Her legs went. Across the hot glass, through the ringing, five paces, three, one. Until she was standing beside his shoulder at the Titan's foreleg, and the foreleg went up over her like a wall, grey, plated, warm, smelling of stone and something older than stone. And the chain-end hung beside her, rusted, the size of a cart.

Orrun put his head down.

And put his forehead against it.

Against the Titan's foreleg. Above the band, above the chain-end. Flat. The broken-rock mane against the grey stone of the great leg. And held it there.

---

She felt it come back through him.

Not words. It never was words, with the Titan. She'd felt it once before, at the lock, through Orrun, the thing below breathing out. This was the same, and not. This was the thing awake. And it was so big that it didn't come into her like a voice, or a feeling, or anything she had a name for. It came into her like weather. Like the whole sky changing.

A want.

That was all. A want. Huge, and slow, and old, and so sure of itself that there was no room in it for anything else.

She felt it go through Orrun and through her and out again. She'd never forget the shape of it. Even afterwards, when she tried to say it, she couldn't say it right. The nearest she got was this.

*All of them. Awake.*

And under it, at the bottom of it, where the want went down so deep she couldn't feel the end of it:

*And one.*

---

The chain-end lit.

She saw it. Beside her. The great rusted links hanging from the band round the Titan's leg. Something in them. Coming up. From inside the curve of each link, from the inner side where the iron bent round, a light. Thin. White. Coming up through the rust like light through a crack under a door.

Every link. All the way down the broken end, to the last one on the glass.

"Hold," said Idony.

His voice above her, on Orrun's back. Very quiet. She looked up. He had his face close to the links, leaning down over Orrun's shoulder, with his hands wound in the mane and his eyes on the light.

"It's the rune," he said. "Inside the curve. Where the College cut it. Struck into every link." He was reading. She could see his lips move. "*Hold. Hold. Hold.*" He stopped. "It's still saying it. After three hundred years. After it broke. It's still feeding."

"On what?"

"On it," said Idony. "On what it holds."

---

Three breaths.

Then Orrun took his head away. Slowly. He stepped back, one pace, two, with Idony swaying on his back, and stood.

The light in the chain-end went out. The links hung dark and rusted and still.

The Titan hadn't moved. It hadn't lifted its head off its paws. It hadn't turned an eye. It lay in the middle of dead Orrin on the hot glass with its wings folded along its back and breathed, slow and deep, and the moths rang round it.

Orrun turned and walked away from it. Back across the glass toward the canals. He didn't look back.

She walked beside him.

"What did it mean?" she said. After a long time. When the Titan was small behind them. "*And one.*"

"I do not know," said Orrun.

He walked on.

"It wants them up," he said. "All of them. My kin. The sleepers. It wants them all awake." He was quiet. "And it wants... one. I do not know what one is. It knows. It is very sure."

On his back, Idony said nothing. She looked up at him. He was looking straight ahead, at the canals and the iron towers, with his face grey and his lips moving. Saying it over. *Hold. Hold. Hold.* As if he were learning it by heart.

---

Lisska came in over the glass on the next day at dusk.

From the east. Maren saw her from the tower. A small green shape, low over the moor beyond the cordon, coming in fast and then not fast, labouring, as if she'd come a long way. Over the earth bank. Over the canals. She came down on the rail of the north-quarter tower beside Maren with a thump and folded her wings and sat there with her sides going in and out.

She was thinner. Maren could see her ribs under the green.

She looked at Maren with her gold eyes. Then she turned her head and looked down at the outer bank, where Orrun lay. And Maren felt it.

Not Lisska. Lisska couldn't speak to her. Never had. But Orrun could hear her, kin to kin, when she was close enough to see. And Maren heard Orrun.

*She says,* said Orrun, from the bank below, *the farmer stood in a square.*

---

She got it a piece at a time. Lisska to Orrun, Orrun to her, in the dusk on the tower with the moths beginning to ring out on the basin.

The farmer had gone to Hallowmere. The capital. The dead one, Lisska said, where all the lamps are glass. He'd come down in the big square in front of the council's hall with the red one and with her, in daylight, where everyone could see.

He'd had papers. The account, Lisska said. Ada's writing, and Calder's own, and the books from the mint. He'd stood in the square and the council had come out on the steps, and a crowd had come, a big one, and he'd told them. Where the money came from. Who'd paid for the war. Who was paying now.

*A knight spoke for him,* said Orrun. *A woman knight. With grey hair. Lisska says the one from the Ring.*

Rowan. Maren thought: Rowan.

*She said he was telling the truth. She said she had seen what chains do now. And a man read his paper aloud to all the people, because the farmer reads slowly. And the people listened.*

"And?"

*The river people are not to be driven out any more. Over the river. The ones from the farmer's side.* Orrun was quiet. *They still want his head. The crown. Lisska says they still want his head. But the river people can stay.*

Maren stood on the tower in the dusk.

She thought of a hundred people on the grass below Greyhold's river wall, waiting since the spring with their carts and their blankets to be ferried back to a stripped bank. Of her mother on the jetty with a coil of rope.

"Tell her," she said. "Tell her..."

She didn't know what to tell her.

*She knows,* said Orrun. *She says she is staying. Till after. He told her to bring your answer back.*

"Answer to what?"

*To whatever happens here,* said Orrun.

---

Idony named the day on the morning after the glass. He'd sat up all night with the four glasses on the tower rail and the silk on his knee, and at dawn he'd gone down to Pike's hut and told her.

"The thirty-third," he said. "Near noon."

He was right.

---

It stood up at noon.

She was on the outer bank with Orrun. Idony was on the tower with Pike and Moss. Lisska was on the rail beside them. The whole cordon was out on the bank, it seemed, every cordon man, every labourer from the crews, standing on the top of the earth bank in a long line round the basin with their gauges in their hands, looking in.

The Titan stood up.

Slowly. On the glass, in the middle of the basin, half a league off. Its forelegs first, the chain-ends swinging. Then its hind legs. It stood on the glass with its wings half open and its head up, and turned it once, slowly, round the whole ring of the cordon, as if it were looking at every one of them.

Then it put its head down.

Between its forepaws. To the glass. As it had at the Ring, on midsummer night. As it had on the Library hill at Vael. Three months it had lain on the Ring before it put its head down. Here it had needed a month.

The glass broke.

---

She heard it from the bank. A crack, and then a long grinding roar, and then a sound like a whole house full of windows breaking one after another. The fused black floor of the basin cracked out from round the Titan's head in rings, wide, wider, cracks running out across the glass toward the canals like the cracks in the shingle round Orrun at Vael. Plates of glass lifting. Turning. Sliding. The moths going up off the whole basin in one white cloud, so many that for a moment she couldn't see the Titan at all through them, only the white and the ringing, louder than she'd ever heard it.

When the cloud thinned, its head was down in the glass to the shoulders.

Down in a hole it had made. Broken black plates heaped round it. Its forelegs braced on either side. Its wings half open, for balance. And its head gone, down into whatever was under the glass, under Orrin, under the basin.

She felt it through Orrun. Through the bank. A long way down, something giving.

---

It came up with the egg.

She saw it come. The head lifting out of the hole, slowly, with broken glass sliding off it in sheets. And in its jaws, small against it, grey, round, a stone.

She'd heard about it. From the bird, at the Library. *A warm stone egg, the size of a cart-wheel's hub.* She'd heard about the one at the Ring, empty, a hollow in a plinth. She'd seen the Titan lift one at Vael, from the shore, too far to see.

This one she saw.

Grey. Old-realm stone. Round, a little longer than round. Carved. She couldn't see with what. Something long and low, going round it in the stone. It held it up in its jaws to the yellow-grey sky.

Then it turned its head.

Back. Over its own shoulder. The long neck bending round, slowly, until its head was over its own back, between the folded wings, where the great plates lay overlapping like the slates of a roof. And it opened its jaws and laid the egg down there. Among the plates.

The plate opened.

She saw it. The grey stone of the Titan's back, a plate as big as a cart, opening under the egg. Like water. Like the surface of a pond when you drop a stone into it, going down and round and closing over. The egg went into it, and the plate closed over it, and was plate again.

Grey. Smooth. As if nothing had been there.

---

"There's two more under there," said a voice beside her.

She hadn't heard anyone come. A cordon man. Old, grey, with a gauge on a string. The one Idony had told her about, from the ditch. He was looking at the Titan's back through a brass glass of his own.

"Lumps," he said. "Under the plates. Either side of the place it just put that one. Two of them. Like there's something under the skin." He lowered the glass. "Didn't see them before. Didn't know to look."

Two.

The Ring. Vael.

She looked at the Titan's back. At the grey plates. She couldn't see anything from here. She didn't need to.

---

It went west.

It stood a moment longer in the middle of the broken basin with the glass sliding off it. Then it opened its wings. Wide, wider than the canals, and the wind of them came across the basin and over the bank and threw her hair back. And it went up.

Low. Over the cordon. Over the west quarter, over the pylons and the cables and the earth bank, so low that the cordon men on the bank threw themselves flat. She stayed standing. Orrun stood beside her. Its shadow went over them, cold, and away.

West. Over the moor. Toward the yellow-grey edge of the sky where Pike said the land ran out and the Western Deep began.

Orrun watched it go until it was a speck.

He didn't follow it. He lay down on the bank again with his chin on his paws, facing the broken basin, and the hole in the middle of it.

"Not yet," he said. "Here first."

---

The Bleed came up at dusk.

Out of the hole. Out of the cracks. Out of the ditches.

She saw it first in the basin. A shimmer over the broken glass, like heat over a road in summer, but brighter, and moving. Then it was over the whole basin. Then the canals. The chilled canals smoked so hard she couldn't see across them, white, thick, boiling up off the water as the heat came up into the cold.

The green lamps along the canals went white.

All of them. At once. The sick soft green of the alchemical lamps flaring up into a hard white light, and some of them cracking in their globes. *It isn't the lamps,* Idony told her later. *There's no Deep in them. It's the glass round them. Isvaren glass and Kessmoor glass is the same glass. It's the glass taking it.*

The cordon men's gauges went mad. She heard them shouting along the bank. The needles bending on their pins.

And it came up in the ditches.

---

The labourers were in the ditches.

Pike's crews. Fifty men. At the east quarter, where the Iron Road went out toward the border and the spoil carts were tipped, clearing the outer ditch and the inner one, as they'd been doing every day for a week. Digging out the silt. They'd been told to come up when the Titan stood. Some had. Most were still down there, in the ditch-bottoms, with their shovels, when the Bleed came up round them.

She heard the first one laugh.

From the east quarter. Along the bank. A man's laugh, high, going on too long. Then another. Then somebody singing.

She ran.

---

The ditch was full of it.

The water in the bottom of it, ankle-deep, shimmering, steaming, hot. She could feel it from the lip. The smell of a forge. And in the water, the men.

One was digging. With his hands. Down on his knees in the hot water, clawing at the silt with his bare hands, his shovel lying beside him, his fingers bleeding, and laughing. Laughing and digging.

Two were lying down. On their backs, in the water, with their eyes open, looking at the sky. Smiling.

One was walking. Along the bottom of the inner ditch, toward the place where it met the first canal, where the chilled water ran fast and grey and smoking. Walking steadily, with his arms out, singing something she didn't know. A child's song.

Others. Standing still. Sitting. One trying to climb out and falling back and trying again, crying.

Orrin, she thought. *Those who lived went mad within the week.* This was what it looked like. In small.

---

"Rope," said somebody behind her. "You want rope."

She turned round.

A man had come along the bank from the east gate. Out of the dark. Thin. So thin she didn't know him at first. All bones and eyes, in a grey coat that had gone to rags at the hem, and boots tied on with string. His hair grown out. A beard, thin, young.

He had a coil of rope over his shoulder. He'd taken it off the gatehouse rail on the way past.

"Tobin," she said.

"Maren." He looked at her. Then down into the ditch. "You'll want to get the one that's walking first. He's heading for the water."

---

She didn't ask. There wasn't time.

She went down into the ditch with the rope.

The water was hot through the sacking on her boots. She felt the Bleed come up into her legs, through her feet, a buzzing, a lightness, like the first cup of strong wine. She felt it go to her head. She shut her teeth on it.

Orrun lay down along the lip of the ditch. Above her. His whole length, along the bank, with his head down over the edge.

The walking man was twenty paces off. Going away from her toward the canal, singing. She ran. Through the hot water, slipping. She got to him. She got the rope round him, under the arms, and pulled it tight, and he didn't stop walking. He was stronger than he looked. He pulled her along the ditch-bottom toward the canal, still singing, with her heels sliding in the silt.

Then the ditch-floor came up under him.

She felt it. Orrun. Through the bond, through the ground. The earth of the ditch-bottom under the man's feet rising. A slow heave, like a wave lifting a boat. It lifted him. Up. Toward the side of the ditch. And the side of the ditch leaned out to meet him, and he was pushed up it, onto the bank, still singing, with the rope round him and Maren hauling on the other end.

He lay on the bank and sang at the sky.

---

Tobin talked.

She'd forgotten that about him. He talked to everyone. He'd talked to everyone at Greyhold, the whole two years, loud, about being unchosen, about the beasts, about everything. It had driven her mad.

He went down into the ditch after her and he talked to them.

"All right," he said. To the one digging with his hands. "All right. That's good digging. That's the best digging I've seen. Let's dig over here now. Come on. Over here. There's better silt over here." And the man looked up at him with his bleeding hands and his wide eyes, and laughed, and came. And Maren got the rope round him. And the ground came up under him, and he went up the side.

"You're all right," he said, to the ones lying in the water. "You're lying down. Good. Lie down up there, though. It's drier. It's much nicer up there. Come on."

He kept talking. All of it. Nonsense. She didn't think he knew what he was saying. It didn't matter. They listened to him. They listened to his voice as a frightened horse listens to a voice, not the words, and while they were listening they didn't run, and she got the rope round them, and Orrun lifted them out.

---

Eleven out of the inner ditch.

Then the outer ditch, outside the bank, in the dark, by the light of the white lamps on the Iron Road. Twenty-six. It took half the night. The Bleed came up into her legs every time she went down, and every time she came up she was lighter in the head, and she had to sit on the bank with her head on her knees and wait for it to go.

Three they found in the water. Face down. Too late.

Three could walk, and sat on the bank with their heads in their hands. The cordon men tied the rest to the iron rail of the gatehouse. With their own rope. Thirty-four men tied to a rail in the white lamplight, laughing and crying and singing and talking to people who weren't there. Pike came and stood and looked at them for a long time.

"Forty years," she said. "My father told me what it looked like."

By morning some of them had gone quiet. By the third day most of them had. Two never did.

---

She sat on the bank with Tobin when it was over.

At the east quarter, at the top of the outer bank, with the white lamps on the road below them and the chilled canals smoking behind them and Orrun lying along the lip of the ditch with his eyes shut. She was shaking. The Bleed, still. Coming and going in her legs.

Tobin sat beside her with his arms round his knees.

"How did you get here?" she said.

"Walked." He looked at the lamps. "From Millbrook. All summer and after. Up the Downs, back, wherever the stories were, over the dead country, here. You're easy to follow, you know that? Stories about you everywhere. Knight with a lion. The lion holds the stakes at the Ring. The lion's in two places at the Moot. The lion raises a wall at Vael." He almost laughed. "Every inn. Every barge. I just went where the stories went."

She looked at his hand.

There was nothing on it. A pale band round the finger, where something had been. Like Idony's.

He saw her look.

"In the river," he said. "At the Ashford ford. In the summer, when the companies came over." He turned his hand over and looked at the band. "I saw what they did at a farm on the Downs. The Restoration. Our side." His voice didn't change. "I took it off and threw it in the river and walked."

"Tobin, I..."

"At the hedge," he said, over her. "In the spring, before the gate. I said to you, *when they throw you out, come and find us.* Meaning us. The ring. As if they'd ever throw you out and keep me." He looked at her. "Well. I threw myself out. So I came and found you."

"I'm glad," she said, after a while.

It was true. She was surprised how true it was.

---

Idony came along the bank before dawn.

Slowly. With his stick. He'd been on the tower all night with the glasses. He stopped in front of them and looked at Tobin. At the rags, and the string on the boots, and the hand with no ring.

"Who's this?"

"Tobin," said Maren. "From Greyhold."

"Unchosen," said Tobin. "Loud about it. Unringed now. Don't know what that makes me."

Idony looked at his own hand on the stick. At the pale band.

"It makes two of us," he said.

He turned and looked out over the smoking canals at the broken basin. At the hole in the middle of it, where the Bleed was coming up white in the dark.

"Five days," he said. "No. Fewer. Four, I think." He looked at the glasses in his hand. "And then whatever's under there comes up."

"And the cage closes," said Maren.

"And the cage closes," said Idony. "On whatever comes up."
