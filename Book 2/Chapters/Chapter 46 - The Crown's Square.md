---

Ada wrote it at Bel's table.

In the hold kitchen at Varrhold, with the fire going and the door shut and the Clan's dogs asleep under the bench. On paper Arn got from the tally-house, thick and grey and rough, the kind the Clan kept its sheep-counts on. With a pen she'd cut herself from a goose feather, because she said a bought pen never wrote what she meant.

He told it. She wrote.

Eleven days. A page a morning, sometimes two. He'd say a thing and she'd stop him and make him say it again, slower, or plainer, or in the right order. "You can't start with the dragons," she said. "Nobody'll listen past the dragons. Start with the money."

So he started with the money.

---

The mint-book first.

He'd brought it out of the cellar on Mint Lane under his arm, through the smoke, with the whole of Gallows Quay looking at him. Red leather. Gold on the spine. Rusk's under-clerk's hand, neat and small, page after page of it. Brask had read him the important lines in a room by the fish-docks, and he'd had them by heart since.

*The Varr boy at the ford: three hundred, for the account of the House, at the instruction of the Council's member.*

*The companies at Ashford: a chest, up the Wend, at every new moon.*

*The four, bound, north, for a library: the hunters' wages, a year's.*

Ada wrote them down in her account in her own hand, word for word, with the page each one was on.

Then Rook's book. Black, swollen with damp, with its brass strap. Delling's paymaster's ledger, that he'd bought at Cotter's Cross with a road over the Scarfell in the Clan's name. Every company. Every man. What he was paid, and when, and in what. *Saltreach silver, new.*

Then what he'd seen. The ford. The Fleece. The coin man's coins with the ring of six in the rim. The mint. The camp in the Fells. Vael.

And the river folk. The ones on the grass below Greyhold's wall.

---

"Who burned them out?" said Ada.

"The companies."

"Which companies?"

"The ones in the book." He put his hand on Rook's ledger. "The red hand, and the green boar, and the black ship. I saw their badges at the Fleece. Liss says it was the red hand at Upper Rush."

"Paid with what?"

"That." He put his hand on the mint-book.

Ada wrote it down.

"So the crown," she said, without looking up, "is driving the people the companies burned out back over the river, to the bank the companies stripped, so as to punish them for the war that the companies fought with Saltreach money." She dipped her pen. "Say it plainer than that."

He tried. It took him an hour.

---

Arn and Ulric made the offer together.

At the two hearths in the hall, on the sixth night, with the chief's chair still empty between them under its grey cloth. Calder told them what he was going to do. Where he was going to take the books. Arn listened with his chin on his fist. Ulric listened with his arms folded and the scar white through his eyebrow.

"They'll not listen," said Ulric. "Not to a farmer. Not to an outlaw."

"They might listen to grain," said Arn.

Ulric looked at him.

"The Marches are going to starve this winter," said Arn. "Both banks. The flood took the late barley. The companies took the rest." He looked at the empty chair. "The Clan's got mutton. The Clan's got the high fields that the water never reached. If the crown stops driving the river folk back over the Wend, the Clan will send grain down the Way to the Marches all winter. To both banks. To whoever's there."

Ulric didn't say anything for a long time.

"Red and grey," he said at last. "Both."

"Both," said Arn.

Ada wrote that down too.

---

At the end of it there were twenty-two pages.

In Ada's hand. Small, and upright, and very black, with the lines straight across the rough grey paper as if she'd ruled them, which she hadn't. She tied them together with a bootlace through a hole she made with a hot skewer in the corner.

"It wants a name," she said. "At the end. Who's saying it."

She held the pen out to him.

He took it. He'd practised on the slate. He'd practised on the frost at Glasswater, without meaning to, and on every door down the Wend. He bent over the last page at Bel's table with his tongue between his teeth, as Mira did at Starwatch over her tables, and wrote.

CALDER FENN.

He looked at it.

"The D's the right way round," said Ada.

"I know."

"First time." She took the pen back. "Well. It's yours now. You'd better go and say it."

---

He went on the two-hundred-and-fifth day.

Off the setters' ledge at dawn. Kharrak under him and Lisska ahead. Aurveth lifted her head from the warm stone as they went and watched them go and didn't say anything. He felt her watching till they were over the Scarfell's edge.

South and east. Over the Marches, brown still where the flood had been, the water-meadows gone to mud and wrack. Over the Wend, grey, back inside its banks. Over the Brackenwood, the royal forest by Greyhold, black and bare. Then the dead country.

He felt it under them all morning. Hearthmere. Grey. Fields that had been sown and hadn't come up. Villages with no smoke. A road with nobody on it.

Six hours.

At noon Hallowmere came up out of the grey.

---

He'd never seen it.

He'd heard of it all his life. The capital. The royal city. On the north shore of the Mere, built up the slope in terraces of grey stone and slate, so that every street looked down on the water. He saw it as Kharrak came round over the Mere, low, from the south. Terrace above terrace. Roofs. Spires. The Mere below it, grey, and low in its reed-beds, and still.

The lamps were glass.

He could see them from the air. In the better streets, along the terraces, on iron posts, in rows. Hundreds of them. Black. Every one. The famous lamps of Hallowmere that burned without oil, that nobody had ever asked about. Glass now. Black glass on iron posts, in rows, all the way up the hill.

Smoke from cookfires. Tallow. A city that had lit itself for three hundred years with something nobody understood, burning candles.

At the top of the hill, along the last terrace, a long hall. Not a castle. A row of grey stone halls joined end to end, with steep slate roofs and carved doors. The chapter-houses, where the knightly orders sat with the crown. And in front of them, a square.

Paved. Wide. With steps up to the biggest of the halls at its head, and a dead lamp on a post at each corner.

He heard the noon bell ring as they came down.

---

It rang from a tower over the chapter-houses. Twelve strokes, slow, over the city. He'd heard it rung at Greyhold, and not answered. Here nobody turned to face anything. Nobody stopped. People in the square looked up at the sound and then went on with what they were doing, which was mostly standing, and waiting, and looking for bread.

Then they looked up at the dragon.

Kharrak came down in the middle of the Crown's Square at the last stroke of the noon bell, with his wings wide, and stood on the paving, and folded them.

The guards ran.

There were four of them at the foot of the steps, in crown blue, with halberds. They ran. Up the steps and in through the carved doors, shouting.

The people in the square didn't.

He looked at them from Kharrak's back. Hundreds. Thin. In coats that had been good once. Women with baskets with nothing in them. Children. An old man with a licence pinned to his coat, a square of parchment with a seal, begging, with his hand out. They stood and looked at the dragon and didn't run, because, he thought, they'd nothing left to run from. Or nowhere to run to. The worst thing had already happened to them. It had happened in the spring, when the lamps went out.

Lisska went up onto the dead lamp at the west corner of the square and sat on top of it with her tail round the post.

---

The council came out at a quarter past.

Through the carved doors, onto the top of the steps. Slowly. Old men, mostly, and some old women, in the colours of the orders, with chains and badges, and clerks behind them with papers, and guards behind the clerks with crossbows that Calder didn't think would shoot a dragon, and didn't think they thought so either.

In front of them all, a thin old man with a chain of office round his neck. Gold links, heavy, on a dark coat. A careful face. He stopped at the top of the steps and looked down at the red dragon in the middle of his square.

"I am Ambrose Thale," he said. "President of the Council." His voice was thin too. It carried. "To whom do I have the honour of speaking?"

"Calder Fenn," said Calder. From Kharrak's back. "Of Harrow's End. On the other side of the river."

There was a sound from the steps. Not quite a word. A councillor in green leaned to another and said something.

"The outlaw," said Thale. "With the dragons."

"Yes."

Thale looked at him for a long moment.

"Will you get down," he said, "so that we can speak to you as a man and not as a weather-vane?"

Calder got down.

---

He stood at the foot of the steps with the three books in his arms.

He didn't know what to do with his hands. He'd thought about it all the way from Varrhold. What he'd say. How he'd stand. Ada had told him: *Say it short. Say it plain. Then give them the paper and stop talking.* He'd said it over in his head a hundred times over the dead country.

He held up the first book.

"This is the mint-book," he said. "From the Halloran house in Wendmouth. From the cellar under their counting-house, where they strike the Tide Council's silver. Every coin they struck, it says who it was for." He held it up higher. "They struck the Restoration's silver. All of it. New. With a ring of six cut in the rim, in the die. They paid for the war."

Nobody on the steps said anything.

He held up the second.

"This is the paymaster's book of Delling's company. At Cotter's Cross. Every company in the war, and every man in every company, and what they were paid. Saltreach silver. New." He looked at the steps. "The same."

He held up the third. The grey paper with the bootlace through it.

"This is the account. Of all of it. Written down." He held it out. "It says the Halloran house sits on the Tide Council. It says a chest goes up the Wend every new moon to the companies at Ashford. It says the companies at Ashford are the ones on your Downs." He took a breath. "And it says the people you're driving back over the river are the ones they burned out first."

---

"The river folk," said Thale.

"The Saltreach folk. From the other bank. From Harrow's End and Upper Rush and the Staithes." Calder looked up at him. "The herald came to Greyhold in the spring and said they're to go back over the river. Because of the war. As if they were the war." He shook his head. "They're not the war. They're what the war did. The companies burned their farms. With that silver." He nodded at the red book. "And you're sending them back to the bank the companies stripped. To starve."

He stopped. He'd said it plainer than he'd thought he could.

"And the Clan," he said. "The Dragon Clan. Both halves. Red and grey. They say: if the crown stops driving the river folk back over the Wend, they'll send grain down the Way to the Marches all winter. To both banks. To whoever's there."

He held out the account.

Nobody came down the steps to take it.

---

They talked.

On the steps, among themselves, in low voices. He couldn't hear most of it. He stood at the foot of the steps with the books and waited. Kharrak lay behind him in the square with his chin on the paving and his eyes half shut and every guard on the steps watching him. The crowd in the square had grown. He could feel it at his back.

A councillor in the blue and silver of some order came down two steps.

"The Clan's grain," he said. "That's a generous offer. The council is grateful." He looked at Kharrak. "And the matter of the Halloran house is a matter for the Tide Council, no doubt. Not for us." He smiled. "But there's another matter. While we have you."

Calder waited.

"The crown is preparing a hunt," said the councillor. "For the beast. The great beast that came out from under Greyhold. When it comes back to Hearthmere, as the colleges say it will, the crown means to be ready for it. We've sent to Kessmoor for chain." He spread his hands. "We'll need dragons. Three dragons, bonded, under a knight's command, would be worth a regiment."

"No," said Calder.

"The council would offer a pardon. A lance. A place in the hunt itself..."

"No," said Calder. "I've seen the hunt."

The councillor stopped.

"I've seen what's left after," said Calder. "At Vael. They had a hundred of their best on a hill saying words at it, and it put its foot on a tower and went where it was going." He looked at the councillor. "Half the city's drowned. I stood on the Library steps and watched them burn the bird." He shook his head. "No."

---

Somebody came down the steps behind the councillor.

Not a councillor. From behind them, from among the guards at the back. A woman. In a knight's surcoat, mended at the shoulder, grey that had been white. Thin. Small and neat. Iron-grey hair cut short. A sword at her hip in an old scabbard. She came down past the councillor in blue and silver without looking at him and stood at the foot of the steps.

Beside the dragon.

Calder knew her. He'd last seen her in the yard at Greyhold on the day of the gate. She'd turned her back.

"Sir Rowan," said Thale, from the top of the steps. Something in his voice had changed. "You have business with the council?"

"I came for grain," said Rowan. "For my knights on the Downs. They haven't eaten properly since the spring." She didn't raise her voice. "I've been waiting three days for the council to see me. I'll wait three more."

She turned and faced the steps.

---

"I was at the Ring," she said.

The square had gone quiet. Calder didn't know when.

"At midsummer. I put a chain on it. The great chain. The best that Greyhold's smiths could make, on stakes driven into the stones, with every knight I had holding the lines." She looked up at the council. "It took the chain. It took the stakes. It took the stones they were driven in. And it went where it was going, as if we weren't there." She was quiet a moment. "I came back from the Ring without my lances, because of that. I've been holding the Downs since with what's left."

The councillor in blue and silver opened his mouth. Rowan didn't let him.

"I've seen what chains do now," she said. "Nothing. That's what they do."

She turned her head and looked at the red book in Calder's arms. Only at the book. Not at him.

"And the companies I'm fighting on the Downs," she said. "Village by village. With steel, because nothing else works on that ground. The red hand, and the green boar, and the black ship." She looked back at the council. "They're paid in that. In new silver with a ring in the rim. I've taken it off their dead." She put her hand in her surcoat and took out a coin and held it up in the grey light. Ship and wave. "The man who pays my enemy's wages," said Rowan, "is the man he's naming. Read the book."

She put the coin away.

She still hadn't looked at him.

---

"I can read it," said somebody in the crowd.

A boy. Not a boy. Seventeen, perhaps. Thin, like all of them, with a clerk's ink on his cuffs and a cap too big for him. He'd pushed through to the front of the crowd behind Calder and was standing there, looking at the grey paper in Calder's hands.

"I can read it," he said again. Louder. To the square. "If nobody up there will. I can read it out." He looked at Calder. "I used to read the papers. On the jetty at Millbrook. For the ones that couldn't."

Calder looked at him.

He didn't know him. He'd never been to Millbrook. But he knew the voice. A voice that had read things out on a jetty, for the ones that couldn't.

He held out the account.

The boy took it. He untied the bootlace. He climbed three steps, so the square could see him, and turned round to face them, past Rowan, past the dragon, to the hundreds of thin people standing in the grey with their empty baskets.

"Davin," he said. "That's my name. Davin, from the Kettle, at Millbrook." He looked down at the first page. "This is Calder Fenn's account. Written down by Ada of Harrow's End."

And he read it.

---

All of it. All twenty-two pages.

It took more than an hour. He read slowly, and loud, and clear, the way a man reads something he wants every word of heard. He read the mint-book's lines, word for word, with the page they were on. He read Rook's ledger. *Saltreach silver, new.* He read the ford, and the Fleece, and the coins with the ring of six, and the cellar on Mint Lane. He read the river folk. *Who burned them out? The companies. Paid with what?* He read Ada's plain sentences in his loud clear voice, and the crowd listened.

It grew. Calder felt it. People coming into the square from the streets below. Up the terraces. More, and more. Standing on the edges of the dead lamps' plinths to see. Standing on each other's shoulders. Children on their fathers' backs. The old man with the licence pinned to his coat, standing at the front with his mouth open.

On the steps the council stood and listened too. They couldn't go in. Not with the square full. Not with the boy reading on their steps.

Rowan stood beside the dragon with her hand on her sword-hilt and didn't move.

At the end, Davin turned the last page.

"It's signed," he said. He held it up. So they could see it. A name, at the bottom, in big black letters, uneven, pressed so hard into the grey paper that the pen had nearly gone through. "In his own hand."

He read it.

"Calder Fenn."

---

They gave the answer at dusk.

The council went in, at last, when the crowd let them. The crowd didn't go home. It stood in the square in the dusk, and waited, and somebody lit a fire in a brazier by the west corner under the lamp where Lisska sat, and people came and stood round it.

Calder sat on the bottom step with his back against Kharrak's foreleg.

Rowan stood a little way off. Talking to two of her knights, who'd come up from somewhere in mended surcoats with their helmets under their arms. She didn't come over.

At dusk the carved doors opened and Thale came out alone onto the top step with a paper in his hand. Thin, old, with the gold chain on his chest catching the brazier's light.

"The council has considered the account of Calder Fenn," he said. His thin voice carried over the square. "It has found the matter of the river folk to be as he says." He looked at the paper. "The order of the spring for the return of Saltreach folk over the Wend is lifted. Pending." A pause. "The council accepts the Dragon Clan's offer of grain for the Marches, with thanks. The matter of the Halloran house is referred to the Tide Council at Corsa, whose member it is."

A sound went through the crowd. Not a cheer. Something lower. Like a held breath going out.

"They'll burn the paper," said Calder. From the bottom step. Not loudly. "At Corsa. They'll burn it."

Thale looked down at him.

"Then take it to them yourself, Master Fenn," he said.

He looked at the paper again.

"The council's order of the spring concerning the farmer Calder Fenn stands. The crown's price is a hundred crowns. Alive."

He folded the paper and went back in.

---

Calder sat on the step.

He'd known. Ada had told him. *They'll give you the river folk and keep your head. If they give you both they'll look like fools.* He'd known all the way from Varrhold.

He sat on the step and found he didn't mind as much as he'd thought.

The crowd was going now. Slowly. In twos and threes. Down the terraces in the dusk, into the dark streets with their black glass lamps. He heard them talking as they went. *The river folk. The Clan's grain. Saltreach silver. The Halloran house.* He heard his own name, twice.

Davin had gone. He'd given the account back to Calder with the bootlace tied again, and said, "You should get someone to write you a better hand," and gone off down the steps with his cap pushed back, grinning.

---

He heard it as he got up onto Kharrak's back.

Two of Rowan's knights, at the edge of the square, by the brazier. Talking low. Young, both. Thin. One with his arm in a sling.

"...a ring of iron round it," one was saying. "A league across. Canals. Round the Scald. And the beast's coming to it, he says. Their needles were all climbing when he went past."

"Who says?"

"The envoy. The crown's man that went to Ironhythe for the chain. He came back through the Downs last week and stopped at our fire." The knight looked at the brazier. "He says Kessmoor's going to cage it. When the thing under the ground comes up. They've been building a cage forty years. Iron and cold water, and some engine nobody'll talk about." He shook his head. "He says it'll hold. He says they're sure."

"They were sure at the Ring," said the one with the sling.

The other didn't say anything.

Calder sat on Kharrak's back in the dusk and thought of a lion, and a woman, and a reader with a stick, going west along the frozen shore of Glasswater toward the snow-line.

Toward the Scald. Toward a ring of iron.

---

He went up off the Crown's Square before the guards found their nerve.

Kharrak spread his wings and the brazier's fire went flat and the last of the crowd ducked. Up. Over the chapter-houses' slate roofs. Over the black lamps on their terraces. Over the Mere, grey in the last light.

He looked down once.

At the foot of the steps, by the brazier, a small neat figure in a mended surcoat stood with her hand on her sword-hilt, looking up.

She didn't lift her hand. He didn't either.

Lisska came off the dead lamp at the west corner last, and caught them up over the water.

*Did it work?* she said.

"Half."

*Which half?*

"The half that matters," said Calder.
