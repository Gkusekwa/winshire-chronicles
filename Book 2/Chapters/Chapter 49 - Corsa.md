---

Aurveth said she'd come.

He hadn't asked her. He'd come back up to Varrhold from Hallowmere and told her on the setters' ledge what the council had said, and what Thale had said at the end, the old man in the gold chain. *Then take it to them yourself.* And she'd lifted her head off the warm stone and looked at him for a long time with her great gold eye.

*Corsa,* she said.

"The Tide Council. Yes."

*I'll come.*

"You can't fly that far."

*I flew a quarter of a league round the glen in the summer and didn't fall. I've flown every day since.* She looked out over the ledges, at the moor, at the grey sky over the Scarfell. *Sigrun took me to Corsa once. Forty years ago. The Council wanted to see a dragon. I'd like to see them again.* She put her head down. *I'll come slowly.*

---

She came slowly.

Off the setters' ledge on the two-hundred-and-thirty-second day, with the whole of the Roosts on the cliffs to watch. She went off the edge and dropped, and he stopped breathing, and she caught. Lopsided. The crooked wing beating a little harder than the other, a little shorter. She went out over the glen low, too low, and then rose, slowly, and turned south.

He went after her on Kharrak.

A league at a time. She'd fly a league and come down on a hill, or a cape, or a beach, and lie with her wings spread out on the ground and her sides going in and out. Then up again. A league. Down. Seven days down the coast. They rested at Skarrow, at the Brack steading, where old Orm Garrick came out and stood looking at her with his hat in his hands. They rested on a cape above Gullhaven where the wind came straight off the sea. They rested on the Saltings, on a hummock of salt grass between the channels, with the wading birds all round them.

Kharrak flew beside her the whole way. Close. Off her crooked side. He didn't say anything about it. He didn't complain once. Calder had never known him go seven days without complaining.

*If she falls,* Kharrak said, on the fourth day, *I'm under her.*

"I know."

*I'm saying it so you know.*

"I know," said Calder.

She didn't fall.

---

Corsa was a harbour with a city round it.

He came over the last headland on the two-hundred-and-thirty-ninth day in the evening and saw it. A deep harbour, round as a bowl, with hills going up steep on three sides. And on the hills, houses. Tall. Narrow. Pressed together. Painted shutters on every one, blue and red and yellow and green, so that the whole hillside looked like a quilt hung out to dry. Ships in the harbour. Hundreds. Masts like a reed-bed.

At the harbour's mouth two round towers of grey stone, one on each side, with a great chain hanging between them, low in the water, black.

Every second building a counting-house or a tavern, Brask had said. Calder thought he'd been generous.

On the harbour front, at the head of the biggest quay, a house bigger than all the others. Grey stone. A copper roof gone green. A balcony on the front of it over the water, with flagpoles. Flags on them. He knew the colours. He'd seen them from the air over Wendmouth. Marrow blue. Quill white. Strand green. Halloran red and gold. And a dozen others, rags and pennants, that he didn't know.

The Tidehall.

---

They came down on its sea-steps at dawn.

All three. Kharrak first, on the bottom step, by the water, red, enormous. Then Aurveth, slowly, on the broad step above, folding the crooked wing with care. Then, because she wasn't there yet, nobody.

The town came out.

He heard it before he saw it. Doors. Shutters. Voices. The whole hillside, it seemed, coming down to the harbour front in the grey light to look. They didn't come close. They stood along the quays and at the windows and on the decks of the ships in the harbour, hundreds of them, and looked at the two dragons on the Tidehall's steps, and at the man between them.

An old woman on the nearest quay threw a handful of something over the edge into the water.

Salt. He saw it go. Grey, coarse, scattered on the harbour.

---

Lisska came in the next morning.

He felt her before he saw her. A long way off, small, at the back of his head. Tired. So tired. And then nearer. He went up onto the Tidehall's roof, onto the green copper, to watch for her, and she came in over the western headland low over the sea, labouring, and came down on the roof beside him with a thump and lay there.

Thin. Thinner than when she'd left. He could see her ribs.

*I'm back,* she said.

"I know."

*I've got a letter.*

He saw it. On her foreleg. A twist of oilcloth, tied on with string. He untied it with his fingers, carefully, and she held still for him with her eyes shut.

---

He read it himself.

Sitting on the green copper roof of the Tidehall with Lisska asleep against his side and the town waking up below. He unfolded it. A sheet of cream paper, soft, the same paper as the eleven pages in his coat. Grey letters on it. Pencil. Plain and square and big.

Five lines.

He did it a word at a time. With his finger under each one. Slowly. As Mira had made him do at Starwatch. As Ada had at the slate. It took him a long time. He didn't mind. Nobody was watching.

*The cage holds. It eats beasts.*

He read that line three times.

*Corvin has the bird's roll and wants Idony to read it.*

*The three young ones you lost went south to Sarovel.*

*Orrun is well.*

The last line had a line through it. And then the same words again underneath, not crossed out.

*I am well.*

He sat on the roof with the letter on his knee and looked at the crossed-out line for a long time.

---

Lisska told him the rest when she woke.

The cage. The ring of iron and cold water round the glass. The thing coming up out of the glass, plated and burning, and the field going up, and the steam. The Titan putting a stone egg into its own back, and the back closing over it like water. Three lions on three banks.

*Three,* said Lisska. *I saw it from the tower. Three of him. And she was on the bank counting. Out loud. To nineteen and back.* She was quiet. *She didn't know she was doing it.*

He thought of a milestone. *Nineteen to the bridge.*

"Is she all right?"

*She says so. On the paper.* Lisska put her chin on his knee. *She crossed it out first.*

"I saw."

*Then she wrote it again,* said Lisska. *So I think she meant it. Mostly.*

---

The Council sat on the two-hundred-and-forty-second day.

In the great room of the Tidehall, on the first floor, behind the balcony. A long room with tall windows on the harbour, and a painted ceiling, ships and waves and fish, gone dark with smoke. A long table of black wood down the middle of it. Forty chairs. The houses at the top end, nearest the windows. The captains at the bottom, nearest the door.

They let him in because of the dragons.

He knew that. He stood at the bottom of the long table with the red book under his arm and the account in his coat, and through the tall windows behind the Council's chairs he could see Kharrak on the sea-steps with his chin on the top step, and Aurveth on the broad step above him with her head up, looking in.

They knew it too. Every one of them kept glancing at the windows.

---

The Speaker sat at the head of the table.

A broad woman, in her sixties, in blue silk. Salt-grey hair in a net. A face like a ship's figurehead, square and weathered and calm. *Mistress Orla Marrow,* the clerk at the door had said, *of the Marrow house, Speaker of the Council,* and looked at Calder as if he ought to bow.

He didn't know how.

On her left, three chairs down, an old man in red and gold. Pale. Very still. Thin white hands folded on the table in front of him. He didn't look at the windows. He looked at Calder.

Behind his chair a plump man was standing with his hands folded on his belly. Soft-handed. Polite. Calder had seen him once before, from across Gallows Quay, through a crowd, with a purse of new silver in his hand.

Rusk.

Rusk looked at the red book under Calder's arm, and his face didn't change, and his hands on his belly went very white.

---

"You've brought something for the Council," said Mistress Marrow.

"Yes."

"Show it."

He walked up the long table. Past the captains. Past the houses. They watched him come. He stopped by the Speaker's chair, three chairs from the old man in red and gold, and put the mint-book down on the black wood.

He opened it.

At the page. He knew it without reading. He'd turned to it so many times the book fell open there.

"The Halloran house's mint-book," he said. "From the cellar on Mint Lane. Under their counting-house. Where they strike your silver." He put his finger on the page. Not on the words. He couldn't read them fast enough, here, with forty people watching. He put his finger on the margin beside them. "This line. And this. And this one."

Mistress Marrow looked at him. Then she nodded to the clerk.

The clerk came and stood at Calder's shoulder and read them aloud.

---

One line at a time.

*The Varr boy at the ford: three hundred, for the account of the House, at the instruction of the Council's member.*

Nobody said anything.

*Wages for the drover at Varrhold.*

*The Halloran woman, lent, for the gate.*

The old man in red and gold didn't move.

*The four, bound, north, for a library: the hunters' wages, a year's.*

*The companies at Ashford: a chest, up the Wend, at every new moon.*

The clerk stopped.

Calder turned the pages. The clerk read on. Every new moon. Every chest. The weight of silver in each one. Struck in the cellar on Mint Lane, under the Council's licence, with the ship and the wave on it, and in the rim, small, a ring of six.

Somewhere at the bottom of the table a captain laughed. Short. Not amused.

---

He watched them understand.

Not the war. He saw that. They didn't care about the war. It was in another kingdom, over the river. A war for a ring of six and a crown that wasn't theirs. He saw them hear *the companies at Ashford* and not care.

He saw them hear *under the Council's licence*.

He saw the woman in white three chairs down from the Speaker look at the old man in red and gold. He saw the man in green beside her look at him too. He saw them look at each other. He saw something come into their faces that he'd seen in the faces of buyers at the Ashford fair, when a good farm came up because the farmer had died.

A rival's throat, he thought. Bare.

---

"A house of this Council," said Mistress Marrow.

She didn't raise her voice. She didn't need to.

"A house of this Council has paid for a war. In a neighbour's kingdom. Out of the Council's own mint. Under the Council's own licence. Without the Council's leave." She turned her square calm face to the old man in red and gold. "Brand."

"Orla," said the old man.

"Is there anything you'd like to say?"

The old man looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked down at the red book open on the black table.

"No," said Brand Halloran.

---

They voted before noon.

He didn't understand all of it. A clerk read things out, and hands went up round the table, and the clerk wrote things down. But he understood the end of it. The clerk read it out in a loud voice, and the Speaker said it again after him, plainer.

The Halloran house's licence to strike the ship-and-wave, revoked.

The Halloran seat on the Council, struck.

The Halloran warehouses and ships at Corsa and Wendmouth, and everything in them, seized for the Council.

"And no more silver," said Mistress Marrow, "goes up the Wend to anybody. Not from any house. Not from any mint. Not one coin." She looked down the table. "The Council doesn't pay for other kingdoms' wars. Not without being asked."

Calder stood by the red book and thought: the chests. Every new moon. Up the Wend to Ashford.

No more.

The companies on the Downs, paid in new silver with a ring in the rim. Their pay stopped. Today.

---

Rusk was gone.

He saw it when he looked up. The space behind the old man's chair was empty. He hadn't heard him go. Nobody had. He'd gone out of the back of the great room while the clerk was reading, quietly, like a plump man leaving a dinner early.

Lisska saw him from the roof.

*There's a man running,* she said. *Down the back stairs. Out of the back door. Down to the harbour. Fat. Fast for fat.* A pause. *There's a ship. Black. Two masts. The sails are already going up.*

Calder went to the window.

He saw it. A black two-master at the end of the Halloran quay, with its sails going up, and men casting off. And the plump figure going up the plank at a run, and the plank coming in behind him.

The chain across the harbour mouth was down. For the morning tide. Lying low in the water between the two towers.

The black ship went out past the towers on the tide before anybody in the great room thought to say a word about it.

*Shall I?* said Kharrak, from the steps.

"No," said Calder.

He watched it go. Out past the towers. Out onto the grey sea. Smaller.

He didn't know why he let it go. He'd think about it later, on a lot of nights. He thought it was because he'd come to Corsa to say a true thing to the Council's face and make them hear it, and they'd heard it, and that was what he'd come for. Not a fat man on a ship.

He was wrong about that, he'd think later. But that was later.

---

They asked him onto the balcony the next day.

Mistress Marrow. The woman in white, who was the Quill house's member. The man in green, the Strand house's. They stood over the sea-steps with the three dragons below them and the whole harbour watching.

"The Council's price on your head is lifted," said Mistress Marrow. "In Saltreach. From today. Hearthmere's is Hearthmere's affair."

"And there's a contract," said the man in green. He had a soft voice, like a man selling a horse. "Your three dragons, on the Council's account. For whatever the next beast is. A hundred silver a month. Each. And a house on the hill."

"There's always a next beast, Master Fenn," said Mistress Marrow.

He looked down at Kharrak, with his chin on the step. At Aurveth, with her head up, looking at the balcony. At Lisska on the copper roof above.

"I'm not for hire," said Calder. "They're not mine to hire out."

"Everyone in Corsa is for hire, Master Fenn," said Mistress Marrow. "Only the price differs."

"Then I've not got one," said Calder.

He went down off the balcony and out onto the sea-steps, and sat down on the bottom step between Kharrak's forefeet, and nobody followed him.

---

Idris Vey bought him a drink on the two-hundred-and-forty-fifth day.

He hadn't seen him come into the tavern. A harbour tavern, low and dark, with a ship's bell over the door and nets on the ceiling. The Bell and Anchor. He'd gone in because it was raining and the dragons were asleep on the steps and Lisska said she was tired of watching him sit in the wet.

He was at a table by the wall with a cup of something hot when a small man sat down across from him.

Neat. About fifty. Grey gloves. Spectacles. A soft, pleasant, dry voice, like a voice reading aloud in a quiet room.

"Master Fenn," said Idris Vey. "Good afternoon."

Calder looked at him.

The last time he'd heard that voice it had been calling up the side of a crag in the Fellwood, under a white cloth on a pole, through the smoke of green-wood fires, pleasant as a man reading aloud, asking him to come down.

---

"You'll forgive me," said Vey. "I won't stay long." He put a cup down on the table in front of Calder. "That's for you. It's better than what you're drinking."

"Why?"

"Because I was paid to take you, at Crow Scar. And I didn't. And it cost me the contract." Vey took off his gloves, finger by finger, and laid them on the table. "And because I wasn't paid to like it. And I didn't." He picked up his own cup. "No hard feelings, Master Fenn. Business."

Calder didn't drink.

"Are you being paid now?"

"I'm always being paid," said Vey. "By somebody. For something. My company's on the Council's books for anything they want. Sable and Hask are in the harbour pens eating the Council's mutton." He smiled. "But nobody's paying me to sit here. This is my own time."

---

He did drink, after a while. It was better.

Vey talked. Pleasantly, about nothing. The rain. The harbour. The Halloran ships, which the Council had seized and was already arguing over. The price of rope.

Then he said:

"There's a reef. East of Gullhaven. Off the coast. You'd know it from the air."

Calder waited.

"The fishermen won't go near it. They never have. My grandfather wouldn't." Vey turned his cup. "And all this autumn the tide's been doing odd things there. Coming in when it should go out. Standing still. The Gullhaven men say the reef hums at night." He looked at Calder over his spectacles. "You were at Vael, they say. And they say something came up out of a lake there and took the lake with it."

"Yes."

Vey nodded slowly.

"If anything comes up out of the sea at Gullhaven," he said, "the Council will hire me to empty the harbour. The town. Get the people off the spit, get the ships out." He drank. "They'll want it done for nothing. For the good of the League." He put the cup down. "And by the Drowned Lady, Master Fenn, I'll never empty a harbour for free."

---

He said it the way Calder had heard men in the Marches say *by the river*. Or *by the Clan*. As if it were a thing everybody said. As if everybody knew.

"The Drowned Lady," said Calder.

"You'll have heard her sworn by. Every captain in Corsa swears by her. Every ship's boy." Vey looked faintly surprised. "You'll have seen the salt."

He had. On the quay, the first morning. The old woman, throwing a handful of grey salt into the harbour. And at Vael, on the Library steps by the bird's fire, a square woman in a scarf pushing a little grey bag into the reader's burned hands. *You throw it. Every crossing.* And the boy beside her: *For the Lady.*

"Who is she?"

Vey looked at him over his spectacles for a long moment, as if he'd asked who the sun was.

"The Lady?" he said. "She's under the water, Master Fenn." He picked up his gloves. "Everyone knows that."

He got up, and put his gloves on, finger by finger, and nodded, pleasantly, and went out into the rain.

---

He heard the news on the quay two days later.

A Gearmouth ship had come in. Iron-sheathed, from round the western cape, with a hold full of something. He was sitting on the sea-steps with Aurveth when the crew came ashore and went into the counting-house on the corner, and the traders came out of it after them talking loudly, and he heard them over the water.

"...a Titan," one of them was saying. "In a cage. In Kessmoor. They've caught one."

"Never."

"They have. Iron and cold water round it. A league across. The thing came up out of the ground and they shut it in. It's sitting there now."

"What's it eat?"

"That's the thing," said the trader. "That's the thing. They're buying every heartstone in the west. Every heart, every plate. The Assembly's buyers are in Gearmouth with open purses." He lowered his voice, but not much. "It's gone up threefold since the new moon. Heartstone. On the beast-market here. Threefold. They're burning hearts like coal up there."

Calder sat on the step with his hand on Aurveth's neck.

He thought of five lines on cream paper in his coat.

*The cage holds. It eats beasts.*

---

He didn't send Lisska anywhere.

She was too tired. She'd come back from the Scald with nothing left and she was still sleeping half of every day on the Tidehall's copper roof. And he didn't know what to write. He'd tried, on the steps, with a stub of charcoal on the back of a Council notice he'd taken off a post. He'd got as far as her name, and stopped.

He'd learn. Before winter. He'd go back up to Varrhold with Aurveth, slowly, the way they'd come, and sit at Bel's table with Ada and a slate, and learn to write a letter that was worth sending.

He folded the Council notice and put it in his coat, with her five lines, and the eleven pages, and Rook's book, and the place where the red book had been. The Council had kept it. It was the Council's now, Mistress Marrow said. Evidence.

On the steps beside him, Aurveth lifted her head and looked out past the two towers at the grey sea, where a black two-master had gone a week before.

*He'll come back,* she said. *That kind always does.*

"I know," said Calder.
