---

They came to look at it.

All of them. That was the thing he hadn't expected. He'd thought Kessmoor would keep it quiet, a caged Titan's kin in the middle of a dead moor, behind three canals and forty-one leagues of cable and Inspector Moss. He'd thought it would be a Kessmoor secret, like the Bleed, like the engine-houses, like the crates in the straw.

It wasn't. Kessmoor wanted it seen.

By the two-hundred-and-fiftieth day there was a camp outside the west gate. On the moor, beyond the outer ditch, along the Iron Road. Tents. Wagons. Horses. Flags. A field of them, growing every day, with cookfires and latrines and guards and servants and a smell of horse-dung and woodsmoke and the hot-metal stink that still came up out of the ditch when the wind was wrong.

Moss kept it.

"Nobody inside the bank without the Office's paper," he said. To every one of them, at the gate, pleasantly. "Nobody on the inner banks at all. You can look from the tower. Two at a time. One hour."

They looked from the tower. Two at a time. One hour.

---

Hearthmere came first.

A man in a cloak of crown-blue with the Hallowmere seal on a chain on his chest, and two knights in grey, and a clerk with a ledger. Idony knew him before Maren did. He'd heard his voice through the cabin window of a barge on the Scald Cut, complaining about chain.

"Master Gervase Lorne," said Moss, at the gate. Writing it down. "For the crown of Hearthmere."

"I've been here before," said Lorne. "On my way home. In the autumn. Before it..." He looked over the bank at the shimmer of the field going up into the grey sky. "Before."

"You were buying chain," said Idony.

Lorne looked at him. At the bare hand on the stick.

"I'm still buying chain," he said.

---

Isvaren came on the two-hundred-and-forty-ninth day.

Two Wardens in grey and blue, with a snow lynx. Two Scribes in black with writing-cases. Mules. And a tall man, very straight, on a grey horse, in the grey gown and blue collar of the Blue College, with gloves on his hands.

He got down at the gate and stood there.

Idony saw his hands when he took the gloves off to sign Moss's book. Scarred. White and pink and shiny from the fingertips to the wrists, the skin drawn tight and puckered, the fingers stiff. The binding-lamps had done that, shattering in his hands at the Thornwood's edge when the Antlered stood up. He'd been bandaged to the wrists at Vael.

"Magister Dane," said Moss. "For the Council of Colleges."

Dane looked up from the book.

He saw Idony standing behind Moss with his stick. He looked at him for a long moment.

"Vael," he said.

"Magister."

Dane put his gloves back on.

---

Sarovel came with more horses than the rest together.

A court mage of the Bond-King, the Lady Ismay Aurel, in white and gold, on a white horse with a gold bridle. Young, for a court mage. Thirty, perhaps. Very beautiful, and very polite, and she looked at everything with the same bright interested eyes: the bank, the canals, the pylons, the cordon men, Moss's book, Idony's stick. Twenty riders behind her. Servants. A carriage.

"No unicorn?" said Tobin. He'd come to the gate to look.

"The Bond-King's unicorns don't leave the Sun Stair," said Lady Aurel, pleasantly, as if she'd been asked it a hundred times. "They're needed there."

Behind her, a secretary. A quiet young man in plain dark clothes with ink on his fingers and gloves on his hands. He looked at everything too. But not as she did. He looked at the engine-houses. At the canals. At the basin.

Idony noticed him because he noticed everything that looked the way he looked.

---

Saltreach sent the Quill house's factor. A thin woman in white, with a clerk.

Kessmoor's own came in steam-launches up the Scald Cut. Guildmasters from Ironhythe, in heavy coats with their guild-badges. Engine-wrights from Gearmouth with soot in their knuckles like Pike's.

The Wyldmarch sent nobody.

A horse-clan rider came on the two-hundred-and-fifty-fifth day, on a shaggy brown horse, with red bars painted on his cheeks, and asked at the gate for the lion-woman. He gave Maren a message. He didn't have the common tongue well and he said it carefully, as if it had been given to him to carry like a full cup.

"From Aun," he said. "From the shingle. *You don't cage kin. Tell the iron people.*"

Maren thanked him. He rode away again east without going into the camp.

Maren didn't tell the iron people. Idony thought Aun had probably known she wouldn't.

---

The Sarovel secretary tried to buy his way down on the two-hundred-and-fifty-sixth day.

Idony didn't see the first time. A cordon engine-wright told Moss afterwards. A quiet young man with ink on his fingers had asked him, at the camp's fire, very politely, how deep the shaft was. The one the Furnace had come up. Under the basin. Into whatever was under the basin. And whether, when it cooled, a man might go down it. And what a man who knew the way might be paid to show him.

The engine-wright had laughed and said nobody was going down that shaft for a year.

The second time Moss was standing behind him.

---

Idony saw that.

The secretary at the fire, at dusk, with a different engine-wright, an old one, Pike's foreman. His purse in his hand. Not open. Just in his hand. And Moss stepping out of the dark behind him with his gauge on its chain and his dry face.

"The Office thanks you for your interest," said Moss.

The secretary turned round.

He was very quick. Idony would give him that. His face didn't change at all. He put the purse away and smiled and said something about a misunderstanding, and held out his hand to Moss. And took off his glove to do it, because in Sarovel, Lady Aurel had said, you didn't shake with a glove on.

On the third finger of his bare hand, a ring.

Plain iron. No notches. Not a College ring. Idony saw it in the firelight as the hand went out. He saw it again as it came back. Turned a little. And on the inside of the ring, against the skin, where you'd never see it unless the light caught it just so, something scratched.

Six small marks. In a circle.

---

The secretary was sent out of the camp that night.

Lady Aurel apologised to Moss very prettily. A misunderstanding. An over-eager young man. He'd be sent home to the Sun Stair at once. She was mortified. She looked at Moss with her bright interested eyes while she said it, and Moss looked back at her with his dry ones, and wrote it down.

Idony told Maren.

"Six," she said.

"Inside the ring. Scratched. Where you'd only see it if you were looking."

She was quiet.

"He's in Sarovel," said Idony. "The three young dragons went south to the coast. Toward Solenne. With the case." He looked at the dark where the secretary had gone. "And his men are here. Wanting to go down the shaft before anybody else."

"Why?"

"I don't know," said Idony. "Yet."

---

The second cage was decided in Pike's hut on the two-hundred-and-fifty-second day.

Idony wasn't asked. He was there because Pike wanted him there. She'd sent for him. "You read things," she said at the door. "Read this." And put him on a stool in the corner by the stove, with his stick across his knees, and went and sat at her table with Lorne and Dane and three guildmasters from Ironhythe and a Gearmouth wright with drawings.

He listened.

"The beast will come home," said Dane. He said it as if it were a reading. "The colleges are agreed. It came out from under Greyhold. It's going round the old realm's ends. When it's been round them it will come back to where it started." He laid his scarred gloved hands flat on the table. "Hearthmere would like to be ready."

"Hearthmere would like a cage," said Lorne. "Like this one."

---

They talked it through.

A cage for Greyhold. Set in the cavern under the river hill, where the chains had been, where the beast had lain for three hundred years. The anchor-stones were cracked; they'd be replaced with iron. Kessmoor heartstone cores, four of them, to feed it. Kessmoor iron. The chain the crown had been buying in Ironhythe since the summer, twelve hundredweight from the Gannet forge and as much from Holloway's, with links bigger than any forge had ever made.

"And on the iron," said Dane, "the College's runes."

Pike looked up.

"Not your marks," said Dane. "Not *turn back* and *within*. Those hold the Deep in a thing. They don't hold the thing." He looked at the Gearmouth wright's drawings. "The College's rune of holding. The one the first chains were cut with. Struck into every link. Fed by your hearts instead of the Deep." He spread his hands. "A cage and a chain together. Your field to stop it drinking. Our rune to stop it moving. It's never been done."

"It'll need twice the hearts," said Pike.

"Hearthmere will pay for twice the hearts," said Lorne.

"A year's work," said the Gearmouth wright.

"Then start," said Lorne.

---

Idony sat by the stove with his stick across his knees and didn't say anything.

He watched them shake hands on it. Lorne and Dane and the guildmasters. Pike didn't shake hands. She was looking at the drawings. Already working out how many crates a day, he thought. Already working out the chute.

The Council of Colleges would sell the runes. Kessmoor would sell the engines and the iron and the hearts. Hearthmere would pay.

The College had sold the rune of holding to a lord on a river hill three hundred years ago, for silver and a tenth of the light. It was selling it again. To the same kingdom. For the same hole in the ground.

He thought of the case. Dark wood, bound in tarnished silver. *Three hundred and four years. Nobody has asked.*

He got up and went out before they'd finished.

---

Dane came to find him the next evening.

Outside Pike's hut, at dusk, on the bank, where Idony was sitting with his glasses. Dane came along the top of the bank alone, without his Wardens, very straight, with his gloved hands at his sides, and stood a little way off and looked at the basin. At the Furnace, half-sunk in its own glass in the middle of it, glowing dull red at the joints. At the shimmer of the field.

"It's very good," said Dane.

"Yes."

"Nobody in Vael could have made it."

"No," said Idony. "Nobody in Vael would have thought of it."

Dane was quiet for a while.

"I need someone to draw the runes," he said. "For the second cage. For Greyhold. The College's rune, struck into Kessmoor iron, fed from Kessmoor hearts. It has to be drawn to fit their cores. Their marks and ours, on the same iron, not fighting." He looked at Idony. "Nobody living reads Kessmoor's marks and the College's script both as well as you do. Pike says so. I've seen your silk."

Idony said nothing.

"The Council would restore your ring," said Dane. "For it. I've asked. Quell has agreed." A pause. "And the half-notch. For the Third."

---

"No," said Idony.

Dane didn't seem surprised.

"You've sold it twice," said Idony. He didn't look up from his glasses. "The College has. Three hundred years ago to a lord on a hill, who wanted a thing under the river held so he could sell its light. And this summer to a hundred magisters on a terrace, who thought they could hold it for one breath." He turned a glass in his hands. "Both times it ended in the same place. A hole in the ground. And people under it."

"And this time Hearthmere's buying."

"Hearthmere bought it the first time."

Dane looked at the basin.

"Knowledge belongs to whoever can pay for it, Vael," he said. "That's always been true. Everywhere. In every kingdom." He said it without heat, as he'd said *register it at the gate*. "The College only ever said it out loud."

"The bird said ask," said Idony.

Dane looked at him.

"The bird's dead," he said.

He stood a moment longer. Then he turned and went back along the bank toward the gate, very straight, with his gloved hands at his sides, and didn't look back.

---

Idony sat on the bank in the dark until the moths began to ring.

He didn't feel brave. He didn't feel right. He felt as he'd felt on the floor of the lowest hall at Vael, with the cracked glass in his hand: that he'd seen a thing he couldn't unsee, and that nobody else wanted to see it, and that it would be there whether they looked at it or not.

He thought: they'll find someone else. A Fifth ring who reads Kessmoor's marks half as well. It'll take longer. It'll be worse.

He thought: worse might be better.

He didn't know if that was true. He wrote it down anyway, in the margin of the silk, very small.

---

The Gearmouth engine-wright told him about the ship on the two-hundred-and-fifty-eighth day.

At the camp's fire. An old man. Deaf in one ear from forty years of engine-houses, so that he turned his head to listen like a bird. Abner Quayle. He built boilers for the iron-sheathed ships that went out of Gearmouth into the Western Deep, he said, and he'd built the boiler of every ship that ever came back.

"And some that didn't," he said.

He'd been talking about ships to anyone who'd listen. Idony listened.

"The *Margery*," said Quayle. "Out of Gearmouth, in the spring. Going west. Past the edge of the charts, the way they do, to see if there's anything out there." He spat in the fire. "There never is. Sea and more sea. They come back in a year with nothing and half their teeth."

"And the *Margery*?"

"Came back in the summer." Quayle turned his good ear to the fire. "Half her crew. The rest dead. Fever, and the sea. And the ones who came back said they'd seen a far shore. Land. Out there. West of everything. And on it, a mountain."

He looked at Idony.

"Walking," he said.

---

"Walking," said Idony.

"That's what they said. Every one of them. A mountain, on the far shore, walking along it. They watched it a whole morning from the masthead." Quayle shrugged. "Sailors' talk. They'd been at sea too long. Half of them had fever. You see things." He poked the fire. "The *Margery*'s master drinks in the Anchor at Gearmouth now and won't go out again. Says the sea's not empty after all."

He took out the silk, there, at the fire, and his graphite, and wrote it down. In the margin. Small.

*The* Margery *(Gearmouth), summer. A far shore west of the charts. A mountain walking. Sailors' talk.*

He looked at it. He didn't cross out *sailors' talk*. He didn't cross out anything else either.

---

He marked the Titan every night.

He'd lost it, nearly, after the two-hundred-and-thirty-third day. It had gone up off the broken basin with the egg in its back and gone west, low, over the cordon, over the moor, toward Gearmouth and the coast. His glasses had followed it. Fainter every night. Out to the very edge of his readings, where the quicksilver barely leaned at all, where a tide-reader learns to look for the faintest things.

Then past Gearmouth. Past the coast.

Out over the Western Deep.

It hadn't settled. That was the thing. Every other time, at Vael, at the Scald, it had wandered and then turned and then lain down. At the Ring it had come straight. Here it didn't lie down. There was nothing to lie down on. It was over the sea.

It went round.

He marked it every night on the silk, at the chart's edge, past the last line of the coast, where the College's great chart of the tides went blank. A dot. The next night, a dot a little further round. The next, further. A ring of dots, growing, night after night, round the same empty place on the edge of the chart.

Circling. Out there over the Western Deep. Slowly. Patiently.

As if it were waiting for something to grow in it.

He looked at the ring of dots for a long time on the two-hundred-and-sixtieth night. Then he looked at what he'd written in the margin beside it two nights before, very small. *A far shore. A mountain walking.*

He didn't draw a line between them. He wanted to. He didn't.

---

Orrun lay in the outer ditch.

He'd gone down into it on the two-hundred-and-forty-sixth day, when the Bleed had gone out of it and the water had gone cold again. Down off the outer bank into the bottom of the ditch, and lain down there, along it, in the cold water and the dead grass, against the foot of the earth bank. With his head toward the basin.

He didn't come out.

Idony went and looked at him every day. From the top of the bank. The great grey lion lying in the bottom of the ditch, bigger than the ditch now, so that his back stood up out of it like a wall, with his chin on the earth of the bank and his eyes shut. Lying very still and not asleep. Idony could tell.

He didn't eat. Tobin brought him a sheep from the camp every second day and it lay on the ditch's lip till the crows had it.

"Why there?" Idony asked Maren, on the two-hundred-and-sixty-second day. She'd come and stood beside him at the top of the bank.

"It's as close as the iron lets him get," she said.

She looked down at the lion.

"He says it's in a box," she said. "He says it can't drink. He says it's so hot, and it can't drink, and it doesn't know why."

Down in the ditch the lion opened his eyes. He didn't lift his head. He looked along the foot of the bank toward the gate, and the field going up into the grey sky beyond it, and the dull red glow in the middle of the basin.

Then he shut them again.

Idony stood at the top of the bank with Maren. Neither of them said anything.

Then he went back to the tower, and set out his four glasses on the cold rail in a ring, and marked the Titan, a dot at the chart's edge, a little further round, over the empty sea.
