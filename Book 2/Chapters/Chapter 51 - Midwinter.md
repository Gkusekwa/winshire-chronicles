---

It snowed on the moor and not on the glass.

She watched it all through the dark of the year from the top of the bank. The snow came in off the moor on the north wind and lay on the heather and the Iron Road and the roofs of the cordon men's huts, white, and on the earth bank, and on the outer ditch where Orrun had lain. It lay on the canals' brick edges and on the pylons' iron, a white line along the top of every cable.

It didn't lie on the basin.

Inside the field it fell, and melted as it fell, and came up again as steam. All winter the middle of the Scald smoked. A white haze over the black glass, going up into the grey sky, and in the middle of it, half-sunk, glowing at the joints, the thing in the cage.

---

The envoys went.

Not all at once. By the two-hundred-and-seventieth day the camp outside the west gate was half the size it had been. Lorne went first, in his crown-blue cloak, to Ironhythe, to sign for the second cage at the Hall of Measures. Dane went with him, very straight, with his gloved hands, without looking back at the bank. The Lady Aurel went south with her twenty riders and her carriage and her bright interested eyes, back to the Sun Stair, apologising prettily to Moss at the gate for nothing in particular.

The Quill house's thin woman in white went back to the coast.

The Kessmoor guildmasters stayed. They came out every few days in their steam-launches to look at the cage and went back again.

"They'll come back," said Tobin. "When it does something."

"It won't do anything," said Pike. "That's the point."

---

Orrun came out of the ditch on the two-hundred-and-sixty-sixth day.

She didn't see him decide. She came along the bank in the morning and he was standing in the bottom of it, with the snow on his back, looking at her.

"I am hungry," he said.

She nearly sat down in the snow.

He ate a sheep that day, from the cordon's stores, that Moss had sent out. He ate another the next day. On the two-hundred-and-sixty-eighth day he went to the gatehouse and looked at the stone by the door, the small one, the one that wouldn't crack for him after the Furnace rose, and it split in two from top to bottom with a sound like a log going on a fire.

He looked at it for a while.

"It came back," he said.

"You said it would."

"I was not sure," said Orrun.

---

He grew.

All winter. She didn't notice it day to day. She noticed it at the gate. In the autumn, when they came, his back had been at the height of the gatehouse's lintel. By midwinter he had to put his head down to go under it, and then he stopped going through the gate at all and went over the bank instead, in one long step, the way she'd step over a stile.

She stood next to him by the north tower one morning and looked up, and thought of the red dragon on the Library steps at Vael, lying beyond the bird's fire with his head on his forefeet.

The same. His shoulder where the red one's had been.

"You're as big as Kharrak," she said.

"Who is Kharrak?"

"The farmer's red one."

Orrun thought about it.

"Lisska says he is very loud," he said.

---

They stayed.

Moss asked her why, once, at the gate. Pike never asked. Pike didn't think about why people did things, only how.

"He won't leave," said Maren. "Till he's seen it."

"Seen what?"

"What's under there."

Moss looked at the basin, smoking.

"Something's still under there," said Idony. He'd come up behind her with his stick. He could walk without it now, mostly. He carried it anyway. "The glasses lean to it. Even with the field closed. A point under the hole, a long way down. Something it didn't take." He looked at the steam. "Or something that isn't the kind of thing it takes."

"You can't get down there," said Moss. "Nobody can. Not with that sitting on top of it."

"No," said Idony. "Not yet."

---

Orrin's ruins were at the north end, outside the bank.

Where the glass stopped. The cordon's earth bank went round the basin, and the basin was the glass, and the glass was the town that had melted. But not all of it. At the north end, beyond the outer ditch, where the ground rose a little toward the moor, the glass thinned and stopped, and there were houses.

Stone. Small. Roofless forty years. In a street, with their doors standing open and their window-holes black, and snow lying in the rooms. A well at the top of the street with its stone curb cracked. At the end, a chapel. Square, with a stubby bell-cote and no bell. Its roof gone, its door gone. Inside, a hearthstone, as every chapel had.

The cordon men didn't go there. They said it was unlucky. They said you could hear people talking in the houses at night, if the wind was right.

"Good," said Tobin, when she told him. "Then nobody'll come."

---

It was his idea.

Midwinter night. The longest. The two-hundred-and-eighty-first day by Idony's count, which was the only count any of them trusted. They went out to the ruins at dusk, the three of them, over the outer ditch, across the snow, with a bundle of wood from the cordon's stores and some bread and a skin of something Tobin had got off a Kessmoor wright.

Orrun went ahead of them. He lay down across the bottom of the street, in the snow, with his head toward the chapel. Like a gate.

They made the fire on the chapel's hearthstone.

With flint. Tobin struck it. He tried the hearth-word first, the one every child knew, two fingers and three turns, and laughed at himself when nothing happened.

"Dead," said Idony. "The surge went out with the Furnace. It's all dead again outside the bank."

"I know," said Tobin. "I just like saying it."

The fire caught on the flint's third spark. They sat round it on the cold floor of the chapel with their backs to the walls and the snow coming down out of the dark through the roof, and the light going up.

---

"At home they'd be saying it," said Tobin.

He was looking at the fire.

"At noon. Today. Facing the wall." He picked up a stick and put the end of it in the flames. "The ones that are left. Grieve and the rest. In the yard. Facing the inner wall. The way they did every day." He turned the stick. "I used to stand at the back and listen. I wasn't let say it. Unchosen. You only say it once you're sworn."

Maren didn't say anything.

"Do they still?" said Idony. "After the gate?"

"Grieve does," said Tobin. "I'd bet you. Grieve'd say it if the wall fell down on him."

---

She said it.

She didn't decide to. She was looking at the fire, and the words were there, as they'd always been, since she was smaller than Wren. Her father's oath. She'd said it at noon every day for two years in the yard at Greyhold, facing the wall, and before that she'd heard him say it, in the kitchen, under his breath, while he tied his boots.

She said it to the fire.

*I will hold the river.*

*I will keep the road.*

*I will stand between the weak and what would harm them.*

She stopped.

The next line was there. She could feel it. *And I will keep the watch below.* And then the last one. The one the College had chosen. The one the bird had laughed at, and Idony had read in a contract in a dark-wood case, and a hundred magisters had said on a terrace with their bare feet on the stone, at the noon bell, the moment before it all went out.

*And I will not ask what I keep.*

She didn't say it. Either of them.

*I will keep the watch,* she said.

And stopped there.

---

Nobody said anything for a while.

The fire cracked. Snow came down through the roof and hissed on the edge of the hearthstone. At the bottom of the street Orrun lifted his head and put it down again.

"That's not how it goes," said Tobin. Quietly.

"I know how it goes."

"You left out..."

"I know what I left out," said Maren.

Tobin looked at her across the fire. For a long time. Then he looked at the flames.

*I will hold the river,* he said.

His voice was rough. He'd never said it. She'd never once heard him say it, in two years. He'd stood at the back.

*I will keep the road.*

*I will stand between the weak and what would harm them.*

He stopped where she'd stopped. He looked at her.

*I will keep the watch,* said Tobin.

---

Idony didn't.

He sat against the chapel wall with his knees up and his hands round them and looked at the fire, and didn't say anything.

"I read who wrote it," he said at last. Not to them. To the fire. "In the case. Under the perch. My college wrote it, for a lord, to keep a thing under the river still. The last line's a muzzle. It's there so the voices won't doubt." He was quiet. "*Keep the watch below* fed the chains. Three hundred years of people saying it at noon, not knowing, and it fed the chains."

"I didn't say that bit," said Maren.

"No."

"Or the muzzle."

"No," said Idony. "You didn't."

He looked at the fire for a long time.

She thought he wouldn't. She thought that was all right. He'd never been sworn. It had never been his. He'd read the bill of sale.

---

*I will hold the river,* said Idony.

Very quietly. The way he said things he'd had to work for.

*I will keep the road.*

He stopped. He looked at her.

"What's the third?"

"*I will stand between the weak and what would harm them.*"

*I will stand between the weak,* said Idony, *and what would harm them.*

He was quiet.

*I will keep the watch.*

He let his breath out.

"That's not their oath," he said. "Theirs has a floor under it, and a gag in it. That's yours." He looked at her. "That's a different working."

"It's not a working. It's just words."

"Everything's just words," said Idony. "Till enough people say them."

---

Three voices. In a dead town. Out on a moor with no Deep in it, outside a cage with a burning thing in it, on the longest night of the year, by a fire made with flint.

Nothing happened. She hadn't thought anything would.

She sat in the chapel of Orrin with her back to the cold wall, and Tobin on one side of the fire with his stick in the flames, and Idony on the other with his arms round his knees, and Orrun lying across the bottom of the street in the snow like a gate. And she felt, for the first time since the river, since the gate, since her name in the black book under Hale's, that she knew what she was for.

Not what she was for. What she was keeping.

She didn't say so. She didn't need to.

---

Lisska came in at dawn.

Maren saw her from the chapel door. She'd gone out to look at the morning, grey and white and very still, the snow stopped, the smoke from the basin going straight up into a sky like slate. And out of the east, low over the moor, small, green, fast, a dragon.

She came in over the cordon. Not through the field. Over it. High, higher than Maren had ever seen her go, up over the shimmer and the steam where the snow turned to vapour, and down again on the near side, and along the street of Orrin's ruins to the chapel.

She landed on the broken wall of the house beside the door.

Thin. Frost on her wings. Her breath going up white. She looked at Maren with her gold eyes and held out her foreleg.

A twist of oilcloth. Tied on with string.

---

*She says,* said Orrun, from the bottom of the street, *she would not go through the shimmer. It felt wrong. Like a mouth.*

"Tell her she was right."

*She says it is from him.* A pause. *She says he wrote it himself. All of it. She says he was two weeks at it and threw away eleven.*

Maren untied the string.

---

Clan tally-paper. Grey. Rough. Folded small. She unfolded it standing in the chapel door in the grey light.

Charcoal. Big letters. Pressed so hard the paper had torn in two places. The lines not straight. Going uphill across the page and then down again, as if the writer had got tired halfway along each one and let his hand drop. Some of the letters backwards. An S like a Z. Two Ds facing the wrong way, crossed out, and written again the right way above.

Nine lines.

---

*Maren. This is me riting. Ada is not helping, she is siting there.*

She stopped.

She read it again. She pressed her lips together.

*My father payd the dues every year and kept his hed down and dyed in his bed. I thout that was the best a man cud do.*

*Yours dyed when his hound dyed. Ada told me. One life between two.*

*Senna dyed of the fever and I lived. There was no bond. It felt like there was.*

*I have one life between four now. It is not like a quarter each. It is like being a house with the doors open.*

*I think you know what that is like. I think you are the only one who does.*

*We never had a nite to say any of this. So I am saying it on paper.*

*Lisska says Orrun is too big. Kharrak says Lisska is a lier.*

*Ada says send you this: till the six are one agen?*

And under it, on its own, big, the letters very careful.

*CALDER.*

The D the right way round.

---

She read it twice so as not to laugh.

At *riting*. At *siting*. At *hed* and *thout* and *cud* and *nite*. At *lier*, which made her shoulders shake in the chapel door so that Tobin looked up from the fire. At Ada not helping. She could see Ada not helping. Sitting at a kitchen table with her arms folded and her mouth shut tight and her eyes on every letter.

She read it twice.

Then she read it once more for the rest of it.

*Yours dyed when his hound dyed.*

*It is like being a house with the doors open.*

*I think you are the only one who does.*

She stood in the chapel door with the paper in her hand and didn't laugh any more.

---

She sat down on the step.

With the letter on her knee. In the cold, in the first light, with the snow on the ground and Lisska on the broken wall beside her and Orrun at the bottom of the street with his head up, watching her.

Her father had died when she was fourteen. Thirteen years ago. Poachers had killed his hound-beast, and he'd come home, and sat down in the kitchen, and he'd been dead within the week. One life between two. She'd watched it. She'd never talked about it. Not to anyone. Not to Hale. Not to her mother, who'd been there.

She'd never told the farmer. Ada must have had it from Liss. Or from her, in the Fellwood, on one of those nights by the fire, when Ada had told her about Senna, and she hadn't said anything back.

*We never had a nite to say any of this.*

No. They'd had a night on the Library steps with a bird burning, and they'd stood on either side of the fire and said what they were each doing. And not asked. Either of them.

---

"What is it?" said Tobin, behind her.

"A letter."

"From who?"

"A farmer," said Maren.

She folded it. She didn't put it away at once. She held it.

Then she unfolded it again and looked at the last line.

*Ada says send you this: till the six are one agen?*

She knew it. Every child in Hearthmere knew some of it. *Before the six there was one.* She'd heard Ada sing the whole of it, low, in the Fellwood, the second night, by the fire, to Wren, who'd said she was too old for it. *Hush now, the last one's sleeping.*

*Till the six are one again.*

With a mark after it. A question.

---

She took it to Idony.

He was still by the fire. He read it slowly, all nine lines, with his face very still, and when he got to the end she saw him stop.

He read the last line again.

"The song," she said. "The old one. The one every grandmother knows. *Before the six there was one.*" She looked at the paper. "That's the last line."

"I know the song." He didn't look up. "Everybody knows the song. The Restoration took the end of it for their oath. *One again.*" He was quiet. "Why's she sent it with a question?"

"I don't know."

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he took out the silk and his graphite and copied the line into the margin. Small. Exactly. With the question mark.

*till the six are one again?*

"Why?" said Maren.

"I don't know," said Idony. He put the silk away. "It nags."

---

She sat in the chapel door till the sun was up.

Lisska had gone to sleep on the broken wall with her chin on her forefeet and the frost melting off her wings. Tobin was making something out of the bread over the fire. Idony was sitting with the silk on his knee, not writing, looking at the margin.

At the bottom of the street, Orrun had put his head down again on the snow.

She took the letter out once more and read it. Not the spelling. Not the song. The sixth line.

*I think you are the only one who does.*

She folded it small, and put it inside her coat, in the pocket, behind the black book, where the eleven pages had been.
