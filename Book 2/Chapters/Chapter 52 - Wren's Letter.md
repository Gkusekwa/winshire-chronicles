---

They came back up the coast slowly, the way they'd gone down.

Eight days. Aurveth rested more on the way home. She'd lie on a cape with her wings spread out on the cold grass and her eyes shut, and Calder would sit beside her with his back against her side and watch the sea, and Kharrak would go off and come back with a sheep that somebody would be angry about, and they'd go on.

At Skarrow old Orm Garrick came out again with his hat in his hands and didn't say anything.

At the Saltings the wading birds had gone south.

She came down onto the setters' ledge at Varrhold on the two-hundred-and-sixty-third day, heavily, and lay there, and the whole Roosts came out on the cliffs and hummed.

*Corsa,* she said, with her eyes shut, *was smaller than I remembered.*

---

He sat at Bel's table for two weeks.

With Ada, and a slate, and a stack of the Clan's grey tally-paper that Arn had given him without asking what for, and a stub of charcoal. In the hold kitchen, by the fire, with the door shut and the dogs under the bench. Every evening, after the grain wagons had gone down the Way and come back, and the dragons had been fed, and there was nothing else to do.

He wrote to her.

He'd thought it would be like the account. Him saying it and Ada writing it. It wasn't. Ada wouldn't.

"It's your letter," she said. She sat across the table from him with her arms folded and her mouth shut tight. "You write it."

"I can't spell."

"Then spell badly." She looked at the slate. "She won't care. Or she will, and she'll laugh, and that'll do her good."

"You could help."

"I am helping," said Ada. "I'm sitting here."

---

He threw eleven of them in the fire.

Some because the spelling was so bad even he couldn't read them the next morning. Some because he'd said the wrong thing. Some because he'd said the right thing and then looked at it in charcoal on grey paper and couldn't believe he'd said it, and put it in the fire before Ada could see.

The twelfth he kept.

Nine lines. He sat and looked at it for a whole evening. Then he pushed it across the table to Ada.

She read it. Slowly. All nine lines. Her face didn't change. When she got to the end she looked at it a long time.

Then she took the charcoal out of his hand and wrote something at the bottom. Not in his letter. Under it. Small. In her own upright hand.

He read it. It took him a moment.

*Ada says send you this: till the six are one again?*

"Why?"

"Send it," said Ada. "Ask her what she thinks. Ask her reader." She gave him back the charcoal. "And spell it how you like. I've written it how it's said."

He crossed out *again* and wrote *agen* underneath, because that was how he'd have written it, and she looked at him over her folded arms and very nearly laughed.

---

Lisska took it west on the two-hundred-and-seventy-eighth day.

The dark of the moon. He tied it to her foreleg himself, in oilcloth, with a bit of string, on the setters' ledge at dawn, and she held still for it.

*The lion-woman,* she said.

"Yes."

*At the iron place.*

"If she's still there."

*She'll be there,* said Lisska. *She said she'd write. She always says that. And then she does.* She shook her wings out. *Is it a good letter?*

"I don't know."

*Say it twice.*

"I don't know," said Calder. "I don't know."

She went off the ledge and turned west over the moor, small and green and quick, and he watched her till she was gone.

---

Wren's came the next day.

Leif brought it down from the caves. Gunna's tally-boy, the one with the young brown dragon, who'd been teaching Wren her letters all autumn when Ada wasn't there to do it. He came down the path to the hold at noon with his brown dragon walking behind him and a bundle in his hand, and gave it to Calder in the yard.

A bit of slate. Flat, grey, the size of his hand. Wrapped in a cloth.

"Paper's dear," said Leif. "She said."

Calder unwrapped it.

Scratched on the slate, with a nail, in small careful capitals, all the same size, in lines as straight as Ada's.

---

He read it.

Straight through. Standing in the yard. By himself.

He didn't put his finger under it. He didn't have to. He didn't sound out a single word. The letters were small and plain and spelled right, better spelled than his, and they went into his head one after another the way water goes into the ground, and he got to the end, and he'd read it.

The first thing he'd ever read straight through in his life.

*PAPA.*

*OLD GREY COMES WHERE I GO NOW. NOBODY TELLS HIM. HE JUST COMES.*

*THE LITTLE RED ONE STANDS AT THE BACK BY THE WATER AND DOES NOT LOOK AT ANYTHING.*

*I AM GOING TO SIT WITH HER.*

*COME FOR MIDWINTER. COME FOR MIDWINTER.*

*WREN.*

---

He stood in the yard with it.

Leif was looking at him. The brown dragon was looking at him. A man going past with a barrow of mutton looked at him.

*COME FOR MIDWINTER. COME FOR MIDWINTER.*

She'd written it twice. Of course she had. She said everything twice. She made everybody say everything twice. She'd done it since she was three, and nobody knew why, and she'd scratched it twice into a slate with a nail because otherwise he might not believe her.

"Tell her yes," he said to Leif.

Leif waited.

"Yes," said Calder. "Tell her yes."

---

He walked up on midwinter day.

Kharrak flew. Calder walked. Four leagues up the moor from the hold to the caves above Coldtarn, in the snow, on the path the Clan's feet had worn. Ada came with him, on Mabbin, the old grey dray-beast, with a basket.

*Why are you walking?* said Kharrak. From somewhere high up and grey. *I could have you there before noon.*

"She'll want to see me come up the path."

Kharrak thought about this for a while.

*That's stupid,* he said. *That's a very stupid reason.* A pause. *I'll wait at the top.*

---

Old Grey was at the cave mouth.

Calder saw him from the bottom of the last slope. The big grey dray-beast, Joss's, standing in the snow at the mouth of the caves with his head up. Bigger than Mabbin. Old. Scarred, at the neck, where the collar had been. A ring of white hair, all the way round, where the iron had rubbed.

He was standing there on his own. Nobody had led him out. Nobody was holding him.

Then Wren came out of the dark behind him, small, in a coat too big for her, and saw Calder on the slope, and came running down through the snow.

Old Grey turned round and came after her.

Not fast. He was old. Walking. Down the slope behind her through the snow, with his great head low, following. Nobody told him. He just came.

---

"Papa," said Wren. Into his coat. "Papa."

"I got your letter."

"Did you read it?"

"I read it," said Calder. "By myself. All of it."

She pulled back and looked at him. She was taller. She'd been ten since the summer and she looked older than ten. Her face was cold and her nose was running.

"You read it by yourself?"

"Straight through."

"Say it twice."

"Straight through," said Calder. "Straight through."

Old Grey had stopped behind her. He stood in the snow a pace off with his head over her shoulder and breathed on her hair. She put her hand up without looking and held his nose.

---

Bel wasn't there.

He'd known she wouldn't be. She was at the hold, with Pip and Nan, in the rooms over the kitchen. She hadn't come up to the caves since the autumn. Since Old Grey had started following Wren about.

Old Grey's forefoot had come down on Joss, on the night at the camp by the Ewe Beck, with a hand on Old Grey's head that wasn't his own. Bel had buried Joss on the hill above Upper Rush and scrubbed the chains with ash and found Odo's mark and told Calder to bring Old Grey home. And he had. And she couldn't look at him.

And now Old Grey followed her own niece about like a dog, of his own accord, and stood with his head over her shoulder, and breathed on her hair.

Calder didn't say anything to Bel about it. He didn't know what to say. He didn't think there was anything.

---

The little red one was at the back.

Past the rows of stone hatching bowls, past the place where Gunna's three slept, past the unbonded winterers in the dark at the very back. By the warm spring. Where the water came up out of the floor and steamed and ran away down a stone channel to the tarn.

She was standing.

That was the first thing he saw. In the autumn she'd been lying down. With her chin on the stone and her eyes half shut. Now she was standing. On all four feet, by the spring, with her head level and her wings folded and her eyes open.

Looking at nothing.

He stopped at the edge of the light from the cave mouth and looked at her. She didn't look back. She didn't look at anything. Her eyes were open and gold and there was nobody behind them, as there had been nobody behind them in the trench of smoke in the Fells.

"She stands all night," said Gunna, beside him. "Like a horse asleep. Only she isn't asleep." Gunna's old voice was very quiet. "She eats if you put it under her nose. Not otherwise. She drinks from the spring. She does nothing else."

"At Vael she knew where she was," said Calder. "For a bit. On the roof."

"I know," said Gunna. "You said." She looked at the yearling. "It was too much. Out there. She's gone back where it's not too much."

---

Wren sat with her.

He watched it from the edge of the light. Wren went past him and past Gunna, down between the rows of bowls, with Old Grey walking behind her, to the back of the cave. She turned one of the stone hatching bowls upside down with a great scraping effort and sat on it. An arm's length from the yearling. Not touching.

And talked.

He couldn't hear all of it. Her voice went up and down in the warm dark, low, steady. About the hold. About the dogs under the bench, and which one had stolen the cheese. About the snow. About Lisska, who was gone to see the lion-woman. About the lion-woman, who wouldn't come in. About Papa, who'd read her letter by himself, straight through. About Old Grey, who was standing behind her, who used to be like this too, and wasn't now.

She'd done this before. He knew that. He'd heard about it. In the spring before the gate, when Old Grey had been brought home empty with nobody behind his eyes, Wren had sat an arm's length off and talked to him. Every day. About everything. Until one day he'd turned his head to her voice.

Old Grey stood behind her now with his great head lowered, as if he were listening too.

The yearling didn't move.

Wren didn't stop.

---

Midwinter night they sat round the fire at the cave mouth.

Gunna had a fire there, every night, on a flat stone, in the shelter of the rock. Ada lit it. Two fingers and three turns, and the word on the third turn, and it caught before her hand had finished turning, bright and yellow, the way her words always caught on Clan ground. The Scarfell had never been drained. Nothing had drunk it.

They sat round it. Ada on the basket. Gunna with her old dragon's head lying behind her on the warm stone. Leif with his brown. Wren between Calder's knees. Old Grey standing just outside the firelight with his head in.

Aurveth had come up in the afternoon, slowly, along the moor, and lay at the cave mouth with her head inside where the warm came out of the rock. Kharrak was outside in the snow, complaining that there wasn't room.

At the back of the cave the little red one stood by the spring and looked at nothing.

---

"Sing it," said Wren.

"You're too old for it," said Ada.

"Sing it anyway."

"You always say you're too old for it."

"Sing it twice," said Wren.

Ada sang it.

Sitting on the basket with her hands in her lap. In the thin voice that had once been better. The tune carrying itself, low and slow, going somewhere sad without being in a hurry to get there. He'd heard it every winter of his life.

*Before the six there was one,*
*and the one was Winshire,*
*and the old ones walked it unbound*
*by water and by fire.*

*The old ones went up into the light,*
*and the one fell into six,*
*and the last great beast lay down below*
*to sleep in stone and dark.*

*Hush now, the last one's sleeping.*
*Hush now, you'll wake it if you weep.*
*It sleeps under all of us, sleeping,*
*till the six are one again.*

She sang the last verse faster than the others. She always had. Like walking faster past a dog you don't know.

Wren was asleep before the second time.

---

He sat with Ada by the fire after.

Gunna had gone in. Leif had gone in. Wren was asleep against his knee with Ada's shawl over her. Old Grey stood just outside the light with his head down, not asleep.

"The one fell into six," said Calder.

Ada looked at him.

"Everyone says it's the kingdoms. Six kingdoms. Hearthmere and us and the Wyldmarch and Isvaren and Kessmoor and Sarovel. The one realm split into six." He looked at the fire. "That's what the Restoration says. One again. Put the six kingdoms back together under one crown."

"That's what they say."

"The song doesn't say kingdoms."

"No," said Ada.

---

"The reader," said Calder. "At Glasswater. On the frost."

He told her again. He'd told her in the autumn, but he told her again, slowly, by the fire. The round place at the end of the bridge. Six roads. The reader's chart laid over the frost, and the six marks on it, the places where the glass stood high. Six ends. Six temples. Six sleepers under them.

And the great one. The one that had been under the river. Going round them all.

"*It sleeps under all of us,*" said Ada. Quietly.

"Six of them. Under six places." He looked at her. "Under the kingdoms. Under all of us."

Ada was quiet for a long time.

"It's not about kingdoms," she said. "It never was." She looked at the fire. "The kingdoms came after. They're sitting on top of it. Like a house on a well, and nobody remembers the well." She pulled her shawl tighter round her shoulders, and then remembered it was on Wren, and let it go. "*The one fell into six.* The six are them. The sleepers."

"And *one again*?"

Ada stopped.

---

"I don't know," she said.

He'd never heard her say it. Not about a song. She knew more of the old songs than anyone in Harrow's End. She knew which verse went where and which grandmother had sung which line wrong. She'd never once not known.

"I don't know what *one again* is," she said. "If the six are the sleepers. One what?" She looked at the fire. "That's why I sent it. With the mark after it. So she'd ask her reader. He reads the old things." She almost smiled. "Somebody had better."

"You sing it faster," said Calder. "That verse. You always have."

Ada didn't answer for a while.

"My mother did," she said at last. "And hers. I never asked why." She looked at the sleeping child against his knee. "I think somebody once knew why. A long way back. And they sang it faster. And we kept the faster and lost the why."

---

He sat by the fire long after Ada had gone in.

The snow had stopped. The sky over the Scarfell was clear and black and full of stars. He looked for the three. The Ferrymen. Red, green, pale gold. He didn't know where to look. Mira would have.

At the back of the cave the little red one stood by the spring.

He thought of Lisska. Small and green, on the warm ledge by the fire, where she always lay on winter nights at the caves. Saying things twice back to Wren. Humming to the little red one in the dark. Getting in everyone's way.

She'd been gone since the dark of the moon. Three nights.

The caves were too quiet without her.

He sat with Wren asleep against his knee and listened to the quiet, and to the spring running away down its stone channel, and to Old Grey breathing in the dark beyond the firelight, and to the little red one, at the back, not making any sound at all.
