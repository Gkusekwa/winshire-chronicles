---

The glass didn't move for him.

She'd known that since the autumn. Since the first day on the basin, on the hot black floor with the moths ringing round their feet. She'd felt Orrun try. Not on purpose. Just as he tried with any ground, every step, without thinking: reaching into it, feeling for the give, for the long slow patience of stone. And there'd been nothing to reach. The glass had been ground once. It was a window now. You couldn't open a window by leaning on it.

So they'd waited.

Fifty days. From the day the Titan put the egg into its back to midwinter and past it. Waiting for a way down.

---

The Furnace had made one.

On its fourth try. The north. When it had gone down under the edge of the basin toward the third canal and broken the canal's bed, and the chilled water had poured down into its tunnel, and it had come up again in the basin in a column of steam, screaming.

The tunnel was still there.

Going down, under the edge of the basin, from the bed of the third canal into the dark. The way the Furnace had come. And all the way down it, full of chilled water from the canal.

Pike had closed the breach with a dam of iron sheets and clay the next week. And then she'd done something Maren hadn't expected. She'd left the water in. She'd run a pipe down into the tunnel's mouth and another out of it, and pumped the water round, out through the coils in the engine-house to chill it and back down again, all winter.

"To cool it," she said, when Maren asked. "It's glass down there. Hot glass. Hot from the inside of the earth and from that." She nodded at the red glow in the middle of the basin. "You want to go down it. I've seen you looking. So I'm cooling it." She went back to her drawings. "It's a pipe. That's all it is. A very big pipe."

---

On the two-hundred-and-eightieth day her foreman came up out of the tunnel's mouth on a rope, dripping, and said you could hold your hand on the wall for a count of twenty.

On the two-hundred-and-eighty-second day Pike pumped the third canal dry.

Not all of it. A stretch at the north quarter, between two dams of iron sheet, over the tunnel's mouth. All day the pumps ran in the north engine-house, and the grey chilled water went down and down between the brick walls, and the canal's bed came up out of it, wet, black, with weed on it. And in the middle of the bed, a hole.

Round. As wide as a barn door is tall. Going down at a slant into the dark. Its edges smooth and black and rippled, like the inside of a shell.

"There," said Pike.

---

"Somebody else wanted to go down that," said Moss.

He was standing at the top of the bank with his gauge, looking down into the drained canal.

"Twice," he said. "The young man with the ink on his fingers. Lady Aurel's secretary." He shut the gauge. "I've had a man at the tunnel's mouth every night since. Two, since midwinter." He looked at Maren. "I thought you'd want to know you're the first."

She thought of a plain iron ring with six marks scratched inside it, where you'd never see them unless the light caught them.

"Thank you," she said.

---

They went down on the two-hundred-and-eighty-third day.

Four of them. Her, and Idony with his stick and his glasses and the silk sewn into his coat, and Tobin with a lantern. An oil lantern, Kessmoor-made, brass and glass, with a wick and a chimney. No words, no Deep. Just oil and flame. Pike gave it to him and he held it as if it might bite.

And Orrun.

Pike lowered the three of them into the drained canal in the crane's cradle. Orrun stepped down. Over the brick edge, down the wet wall, onto the black bed, as she'd step down a stair.

The field was round them.

She'd thought about that all night. The shimmer, going up from the inner ring of pylons into the sky, that the Furnace had come to and stopped at, four times. *Turn back. Within.* The tunnel's mouth was inside it. In the third canal's bed, inside the inner ring. You had to go through the field to get to it.

"It's not a wall," Pike had said. At the top of the bank, in the morning, with her smoked spectacles up and her hands on her hips. "It's a mirror. For a draw. Anything that pulls on the Deep, it sends the pull back." She looked at Maren. "You're not drawing. You're walking. It'll let you walk."

"And him?"

Pike looked at Orrun for a long time.

"I don't know," she said. "I've never had one of him."

---

It let them walk.

It felt like going into warm water that wasn't wet. That was the nearest she could get. A pressure, all over, soft, like a hand laid flat on every part of her at once. And a sound in her ears, very low, like the cables' hum but inside her head. And then they were through it, on the canal bed, inside the field, and the air was only air, and warm.

Orrun came through after her.

She felt him go through it. Through the bond. A pull, a strange one, as if something had taken hold of the long patience of stone in him and turned it round to look at it, and then let go.

*It looked at me,* he said. *And then it let me go. I was not drawing.* He was quiet. *I am not like the one in the middle. I do not drink.*

Lisska wouldn't come.

She'd come out to the tunnel's mouth with them in the morning, and when they went down into the canal she'd gone up onto the top of the outer bank and lain down there, on the snow, on the basin's rim, with her chin on her forefeet.

*She says it is a mouth,* said Orrun. *She says she will wait.*

---

The tunnel went down at the steepness of a roof.

Round. Smooth. Black. Rippled, like the inside of a shell, where the glass had run and set as the Furnace pushed through it. Their boots were wrapped in sacking again and they slid on it. Tobin went first with the lantern, because he said if anyone fell he'd rather it was on him than on him falling. Idony went next, with his stick, slowly. Then Maren. Then Orrun, filling the tunnel behind them, his back brushing the curve of the roof.

The walls were warm. Not hot. Warm, like a stone in a fire that's gone out in the night.

Down.

Tobin counted. He counted out loud, under his breath, the way he talked to the madmen in the ditches, to keep himself listening. A hundred paces. Two hundred. The lantern's light going ahead of them on the black glass and coming back, and the dark beyond it, and the warm air coming up.

Four hundred.

Then the tunnel opened and there was no wall on one side.

---

The shaft.

She knew it at once. It was the Furnace's own. The hole it had come up through, on the morning of the two-hundred-and-thirty-eighth day, out of the ground under the basin, with the glass running off its plates like water off oil.

A well. That was what it was. Round, and enormous, going down. The tunnel came out into its side, high up, onto a ledge where the glass had slumped and set. Above them, a long way up, very small, round, a grey light: the field's shimmer and the grey sky beyond it, like the sky seen from the bottom of a well. And at the very edge of that circle of light, far off, a dull red glow.

Below them, the shaft went down into the dark.

Tobin held the lantern out over the edge. The light didn't reach the bottom.

"How far?" he said.

Idony dropped a bit of glass. They listened.

A long time.

"Far," said Idony.

---

Orrun took them down.

She didn't ask him how. She felt him look at the shaft's side. Not the glass. Under it. Where the glass was thin, and behind it, the old ground. Rock. Real rock, that the Furnace had melted the face of and not the heart. He put his forepaw against the slumped glass of the ledge and pushed, and it cracked, and behind it was stone, and the stone answered him.

A stair.

Coming out of the shaft's wall. One step under the ledge, then another, then another, going down round the inside of the well in a long slow spiral, each step a slab of grey rock pushed out through the glass skin of the shaft like a shelf through a curtain.

"Oh," said Tobin.

They went down it. Round and round. Orrun first now, making it as he went. Then the three of them behind him in single file, with the lantern, with their backs to the wall and their faces to the dark and the drop. She didn't look down. She looked at the step in front of her, and Orrun's tail, and the light on the stone.

It took an hour.

---

At the bottom, rubble.

Glass and rock and rubble in a great heap on the floor of the shaft. And beyond it, in the side of the shaft, square holes. Doorways. Cut in grey stone. Dressed so close you couldn't see the joins.

The outer chambers.

Idony stood at the foot of the rubble and looked at them with the lantern held up, and his face did something she hadn't seen it do before.

"Orrin," he said. "The engineers. Forty years ago. They sank their shafts down to here. For ore, and to draw the Deep in bulk for the forges. And they broke into these." He went to the nearest doorway and put his hand on its edge. "These rooms. And the sleeper's breath came up the shafts all at once. And in one night the town was glass."

The walls of the chambers were glazed. Not fused like the basin. Glazed. A thin black shine on the grey stone, as if it had been painted with something that had dried hard. Where the Bleed had come through.

They went through them.

Square rooms. Empty. One after another, going in. The lantern's light on the glazed walls. Rubble on the floors. In one, a broken pick, rusted to a red stain on the stone. In another, a boot.

Then a wall.

---

At the end of the last chamber. Grey. Old. Dressed so close you couldn't see the joins. Not glazed. Clean. As if the Bleed had come up to it and stopped.

Cut in it, the hooked script. In rings. Round a shape that looked like a door and wasn't. No hinges. No line where a door would open. Only the shape of one, carved, as a child draws a door on a wall.

"They never got through this," said Idony. "Kessmoor. Forty years. They never knew it was here." He held the lantern up to the script. "It says... it says *the breath is kept*. And something about a door-keeper." He stopped. Then he turned and looked at Orrun.

Orrun came past them. Slowly.

He put his head against the wall. In the middle of the carved door. His forehead to the grey stone.

It opened.

---

She'd seen it before. In the cavern under Greyhold, in the days after the gate, and in the granary wall the night they took the Fenns out. A line going up through the stone, from the floor, thin as a hair. And widening. The stone on either side of it drawing back, as a curtain draws back. Slowly. With no sound at all.

Warm air came out.

Not the warm of the tunnel. Different. The warm of a room where somebody has slept and just got up.

Orrun went through. She went after him.

---

It was the room under the Ring.

That was what she thought first. The same. A round chamber, domed, as big as Greyhold's yard. The walls glowing grey from inside the stone, so that Tobin's lantern was hardly needed. A plinth in the middle. A squat waist-high drum of stone with a hollow in its top the shape of an egg, the size of a cart-wheel's hub. Empty. Warm.

The six on the wall.

She went round them. Slowly. She knew them. She'd seen them under the Ring, in the summer, with Hesper's torch going out at the bottom of the stair. The stag with antlers like a winter wood and a forest lying down with it. The serpent coiled on itself with scales cut like frost. The long low plated beast with its snout in the floor. The one with the single horn and the rays of the sun round its head. The great hump like an island with weed on it and a fin and a shut eye.

The lion. With the broken-rock mane and the plates along its back like the courses of a wall. And wings, folded.

Over each one, a tall figure with no face. Cut to stand out from the wall, so that it caught the glow. One hand held palm-down over the sleeper's head. The other raised.

Up. Toward the dome. Toward the middle.

---

Under the Ring, the dome had fallen. What those hands had held up had fallen with it. There'd been a fragment. An open hand, palm up, fingers curved round something that wasn't there.

Here the dome was whole.

She looked up.

In the middle of the dome. Over the plinth. Carved deep into the stone, so deep that it stood out in shadow against the grey glow. Held up by six hands, one reaching from each of the faceless figures round the wall, six hands meeting in the middle of the dome.

A ring.

Round. Stone. As wide as two hands. And in it, six hollows. Evenly spaced. Round the ring. Like the places where stones would sit in a crown.

She stood under it with her head back and didn't breathe.

The ring of six. On the door under Greyhold. On the coins with the ship and the wave, in the rim. On the collars of the bound young dragons in the trench of smoke in the Fells. On the inside of an iron ring on a secretary's finger. Scratched on the stone over the lock.

Here. Carved in a dome by somebody who had seen the real thing. Held up over six sleepers by six hands with no faces.

---

"There's a line," said Idony.

His voice was very strange.

He was standing at the edge of the plinth with the lantern held up, looking at the base of the dome, where it met the wall. Round it, in a band, all the way round the room over the heads of the six faceless figures, a line of the hooked script.

One line. Going all the way round.

"Read it," said Maren.

He read it.

Slowly. In the old tongue first. She didn't know the words. She heard the shape of them. Then he stopped, and stood very still, and read it again. And then he said it in the common tongue, very quietly, and his voice cracked in the middle.

"*Till the six are one again.*"

---

Nobody said anything.

Tobin had stopped with the lantern halfway up. Orrun was standing at the lion on the wall, with his head lowered, very still.

Maren put her hand inside her coat.

She took out the letter. Grey Clan paper. Charcoal. Nine lines going uphill. And at the bottom, under the farmer's ninth line, in a small upright hand that wasn't his, the line Ada had sent.

*till the six are one again?*

She held it up. Under the dome. Under the carved ring with six hollows. Next to the lantern.

Idony looked at it. Then at the band of script round the dome.

"Word for word," he said.

---

He worked it out standing there. She watched him do it. The way he worked everything out, slowly, a step at a time, with his lips moving. She'd seen him do it at Vael and on the frost and on the tower.

"It's the song," he said. "The old song. *Before the six there was one.* Every grandmother knows it. Every child." He looked up at the ring. "It's this. It's a translation of this. Of the temple. Somebody stood in a room like this a long time ago and read that line and couldn't read the rest of it, and put it in a song." He looked at the six on the wall. "*The one fell into six.* Six sleepers. Not six kingdoms. *The last great beast lay down below.* That one." He didn't point at the lion. He didn't need to. "*It sleeps under all of us. Till the six are one again.*"

"One again," said Tobin. "That's the Restoration's. That's the oath."

"They read it wrong." Idony shook his head. "Everybody's read it wrong. The word there isn't *one* as in *together*. It's not *one again* as in *whole again*." He put his finger in the air, not touching, under a word in the band of script. "It's *one* as in one thing. One will. The six held as one thing, under the ring." He looked up at the six hands holding it. "Commanded as one."

He let his hand drop.

"Somebody saw this," he said. "A long way back. And copied the ring, and didn't understand it, and made it a badge. And somebody else heard the song, and didn't understand it, and made it an oath." He was quiet. "And we've been singing it to children."

---

Orrun had his head against the lion.

The lion on the wall. The winged one. With the broken-rock mane. The one Idony had called the first sleeper, Hearthmere's, the one that was going round the others. His own kin's face. He stood with his forehead to the carved stone of its forehead, as he'd stood with his forehead to the Titan's foreleg on the hot glass in the autumn.

She felt it come back.

What he'd felt then. On the glass. The want. Huge and slow and old and so sure of itself there was no room in it for anything else.

*All of them. Awake.*

*And one.*

She felt it go through him again. And this time, with the ring over them in the dome, and the six hands, and the line round the wall, it meant something.

---

"It is waking them," said Orrun.

He didn't take his head from the stone.

"My kin. The five. It is taking their sleep from the temples and putting it in its back. One. Two. Three." He was very quiet. "When it has all five, it will go to the ring. The real one. Not this. The one this is a picture of." He lifted his head, slowly, and looked up at the dome. "And when it has the ring, they will all be one. Under it. The way they are under the hands here. They will do what it wants. All six."

"And what does it want?" said Maren.

Orrun was quiet for a long time.

"To bring it down," he said.

"Bring what down?"

"All of it." He looked at the walls. At the six. At the faceless figures with their hands raised. "What was made after. After the old ones went up into the light. The roads. The houses. The kingdoms sitting on top." He turned his great head and looked at her. "It was asleep under the river three hundred years with iron in its legs and men singing over it at noon. It woke up in chains. It has been in chains since." He was quiet. "It does not hate. I do not feel hate. I feel... it wants the world to be as it was before there were chains. Before there was anything to put chains on."

"That's everything," said Tobin. His voice had gone small.

"Yes," said Orrun.

He didn't sound angry. He sounded the way he'd sounded at the foot of the cage, lying in the ditch, with his chin on the bank. Grieved.

---

The songs were true.

She stood in the middle of the temple under the Scald with Calder's letter in her hand and thought it.

Ada had sung it every winter of Calder's life. Liss had sung some of it to her, when she was small, before her father died. The grandmothers in Millbrook sang it. The Restoration sang the end of it in the ports like a promise. Everybody knew it. Nobody had ever asked what it meant, because it was a song, and that was the point of a song, Ada had told Wren once in the Fellwood, when Wren asked. *You don't find out.*

It had been true the whole time. All of it. Every word.

*Hush now, you'll wake it if you weep.*

And Ada sang the last verse faster. Like walking faster past a dog you don't know. And her mother had, and hers. And somebody, a long way back, had known why.

---

They went back up the stair in the dark.

Orrun closed the stone behind them. She didn't know why. She didn't ask. He put his head to the grey wall from the outside, and the slit closed, slowly, as a curtain closes, and the carved door was only a carving again.

Round and round. Up the long spiral of steps in the side of the shaft. Up the tunnel at the steepness of a roof, four hundred paces, Tobin counting. Up into the drained canal. Out through the field, warm water that wasn't wet, the soft hand on everything.

It was dusk when they came up. The two-hundred-and-eighty-third day.

---

She wrote it on the back of his letter.

There wasn't any other paper. Idony had used the last of his cream sheets in the autumn. The silk was the silk. So she sat at the top of the bank above the drained canal, in the last light, with the letter on her knee, turned over, and the grey back of the Clan tally-paper blank, and the pencil of Kessmoor graphite in her hand.

Idony gave her the numbers.

He sat beside her with the silk on his knee and his four glasses, and said them, and she wrote them down. Plain, square, big, so the farmer could read them.

*The next one is south. Sarovel. The Sun Stair. Within the month.*

*After that the coast. The reef by Gullhaven. A month after. Your coast.*

Then Orrun's words. She asked him for them. He gave them to her lying on the bank with his chin on his paws.

*It is waking them so it can tell them what to do. All of them as one. Then it will bring it all down.*

Then the line. Idony's reading of it. The ring. The six hands. *One as in one thing.* She wrote it as plainly as she could. She wasn't sure she'd got it right. She wrote it anyway.

*The song is true. Tell Ada. It is in the temple, word for word. Tell her that is why she sings it fast.*

She looked at it.

Then, at the bottom, in the last of the space, smaller:

*Orrun says your red one's too loud.*

---

Lisska was on the rim.

Where she'd been all day. On the top of the outer bank, on the snow, at the north quarter, with her chin on her forefeet, watching the drained canal and the mouth of the tunnel where they'd gone down. She lifted her head when Maren came along the bank with the letter folded small, and sat up, and held out her foreleg.

Tobin tied it on. In the same oilcloth. With the same string.

*She says,* said Orrun, *you were down there a long time.*

"Tell her it was a long way."

*She says, is it a good answer?*

Maren thought about it.

"Tell her I don't know," she said. "Tell her it's a true one."

---

Moss came along the bank in the dark with a lantern.

He stopped beside them and looked at Lisska, with the oilcloth on her leg, and at Maren, and at the drained canal below, where Pike's men were already taking down the first of the dams to let the water back in.

"A guildmaster came back from the south today," he said. "From Solenne. He's been at the Bond-King's court a fortnight, buying unicorn-horn shavings, and he stopped at the camp on his way to Ironhythe." He looked at the basin, at the red glow in the middle of it. "He says the Lady Aurel's house is full of a guest. From the north. A lean man. With a captain with a grey beard. And a box."

Maren didn't say anything.

"He says the Bond-King gives him dinner every night," said Moss. "He says he's called Corvin." He turned the lantern down. "I thought you'd want to know."

He went back along the bank toward the gate.

Lisska went up off the rim at first light, with the answer on her leg, and turned east.
