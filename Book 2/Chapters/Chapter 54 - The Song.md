---

The news came up the Way with the grain-wagons.

On the two-hundred-and-eighty-fourth day. A drover from the Marches, on the box of the last empty wagon, coming back up to the hold for the next load. He'd seen smoke on the Saltreach bank all the way up from the Staithes.

"The companies," he said. In the yard, to Arn, to Calder, to anyone who'd stand still. "At Ashford. They've not been paid since the new moon. Not a coin. The chests stopped coming up the Wend." He spat. "So half of them have broke camp and gone. Back over the river by the fords above Ashford. East. For Cotter's Cross."

"Burning?" said Arn.

"Burning," said the drover. "Upper Rush again. The Lowe. A steading at the Ewe Hollow turn. Taking what they can carry and burning what they can't." He looked at the dragons on the ledges. "They're going home. Or what they call home. The Cross. And they're paying themselves on the way."

"And the knights?"

"Followed them to the river," said the drover. "That knight. The grey-haired woman. She's got them on the fords, on the Hearthmere side. Pikes. They can't go back over." He looked at Calder. "They can only go on."

---

Calder went up onto the setters' ledge and stood there a long time looking south-west.

He couldn't see anything. Moor, and the Fellwood beyond it, black, and the sky. No smoke from here. But he could feel it. Upper Rush. Bel's village. The house she'd lived in with Joss. Burned once already, the night of the fires he'd seen from the yard at Harrow's End, before any of this. And now again.

*Can I?* said Kharrak. From the ledge below. Very low.

"Not yet."

*They're burning it.*

"I know," said Calder. "Not yet. Not alone."

---

Lisska came home on the two-hundred-and-eighty-sixth day at noon.

He'd been on the ledge every noon for three days. He felt her before he saw her, small at the back of his head, tired, and something else. Full. As if she were carrying more than a bit of oilcloth.

She came down beside him on the warm stone and lay flat, and held out her foreleg before he could speak.

*It's a true one,* said Lisska. *She said. Not a good one. A true one. She wrote on the back of yours. There wasn't any other paper.*

---

He read it himself.

On the setters' ledge, in the cold sun, with Lisska asleep against his leg before he'd got the string off. He unfolded it. His own letter. His eight lines going uphill, his *riting* and his *nite* and his backwards D crossed out. He turned it over.

Her hand.

Grey. Pencil. Plain and square and big, pressed into the rough paper. Bigger than she'd written the eleven pages. Written for him. He could see that. Every letter made so he could read it.

He read it a line at a time. Slowly. With his finger.

*The next one is south. Sarovel. The Sun Stair. Within the month.*

*After that the coast. The reef by Gullhaven. A month after. Your coast.*

He stopped there a long time.

*It is waking them so it can tell them what to do. All of them as one. Then it will bring it all down.*

---

There was more. He read it all. The reader's words, which she'd written down as plainly as she could, and which he had to read three times. *One as in one thing. Not together. One will. The six under the ring, as one, commanded.*

He didn't understand all of it. He understood enough.

*The song is true. Tell Ada. It is in the temple, word for word. Tell her that is why she sings it fast.*

And at the bottom, smaller, squeezed in at the edge of the paper:

*Orrun says your red one's too loud.*

He sat on the ledge with it. He read it again. All of it. Then he folded it and got up, carefully, so as not to wake Lisska, and went down the cliff stair to the hold, and through the yard, and into the kitchen, where his mother was.

---

Ada read it at Bel's table.

Slowly. With her finger under the lines, as he did. He'd never seen her do that. She read his eight lines on the front first, though she'd seen them, because she said a letter has to be read in order or not at all. Then she turned it over.

He watched her read Maren's hand.

He watched her get to *The song is true*. He watched her stop.

*Tell Ada. It is in the temple, word for word. Tell her that is why she sings it fast.*

She sat down.

She'd been standing at the table. She sat down on the bench, slowly, with the paper in her hands, the way she'd sat down the night his father died. He hadn't seen her sit down like that since.

---

"Word for word," she said.

"Yes."

"In a temple. Under that place in Kessmoor. On a wall."

"Round a dome," said Calder. "The reader read it. Round the bottom of a dome, all the way round. Over six of them. Carved." He sat down across from her. "And in the middle, a ring. With six holes in it. Held up by six hands."

Ada looked at the paper.

"One as in one thing," she said.

"That's what he says."

She read it again. The reader's lines. *Not together. One will.* She read them three times, as he had.

"My grandmother knew," she said at last. "Some of it. She gave me the verse that says where it sleeps, and kept back what it was for. Or she never had that part either." She put the paper down on the table and smoothed it flat with both hands. "Somebody sang the last verse fast because they knew what it meant. And kept the rest back. And then it was forgotten. And we kept singing it fast." She looked at the fire. "Like walking past a dog."

She didn't sing for the rest of the winter. Not once. Wren asked, in the caves, at the new moon, and Ada said her throat was sore.

---

He thought about it that night on the ledge.

*One again.*

The Restoration's oath. He'd heard it at the Fleece, at Cotter's Cross, in the taverns, said with a hand on the heart. *One again.* Men in the ports singing the last line of Ada's song like a promise. Corvin's ring of six on every coin, in the rim, in the die. On the door under Greyhold. On the collars of the young dragons in the trench of smoke.

All of it off a wall in a temple. Read by somebody who couldn't read the rest. Copied by somebody who didn't understand what he was copying.

A warning. *You'll wake it if you weep.*

And they'd made it a promise. And sung it in the ports. And struck it on their silver.

He sat on the ledge with Lisska asleep beside him and the dragons humming on the cliffs below, and thought of the bird on the Library floor at Vael with three bolts in its breast, saying the last thing it would ever say. *Ask.*

Nobody had asked. Not for three hundred years.

---

He went to Arn the next morning.

In the hall, at the two hearths, with the chief's chair between them under its grey cloth. Arn on one side of it on the bench with his chin on his fist. Ulric on the other, with his arms folded and the scar white through his eyebrow. Both of them had been there since dawn, talking about the companies, and the smoke, and the fords.

He told them.

Not all of it. Not the temple. They didn't need the temple. He told them what was coming. The Sun Stair in Sarovel, within the month. And then the coast. A reef off Gullhaven, where the fishermen wouldn't go and the tide had been doing odd things all autumn. A month after the Sun Stair.

"Two months," he said. "About. And then whatever's under that reef comes up. Like the lake at Vael. Like the thing in Kessmoor." He looked at Arn. "Gullhaven's on a spit. There's no road off it but the spit itself. When the sea comes up there's nowhere to go. I need to warn them. And I need..."

"Yes," said Arn.

---

Calder stopped.

"You don't know what I'm asking."

"You're asking for the ledges," said Arn. "To lift a town off a spit when the sea comes for it." He took his chin off his fist. "Yes."

"I haven't..."

"You'll want baskets," said Arn. "Big ones. Wicker, with straps. And harness. The old riding harness from the war-flights, that's in the tally-house loft, that nobody's used since my grandfather. And every pair on the ledges that can carry." He looked at the chair. "And practice. Two months of it."

"And mine," said Ulric.

Calder looked at him.

"My pairs," said Ulric. "Grey thread. Twenty of them." He unfolded his arms. "You'll want them. You'll want every dragon in the Scarfell for a town that size."

"Why?" said Calder.

Ulric looked at him for a long moment with his hard face.

"Because you asked," he said. "Nobody ever asks the Clan to save anything. They ask it to stop burning things." He folded his arms again. "It'd make a change."

---

"But first," said Arn.

He stood up.

He went to the hall's great door and opened it, and the cold came in, and the light, and the sound of the dragons on the ledges. He stood in the doorway and looked south-west, toward the Fellwood and the Marches and the Saltreach bank and the smoke that wasn't visible from here.

"First the Clan settles a debt," he said.

---

They went up at dawn on the two-hundred-and-eighty-eighth day.

From the Scarfell. All of them. Arn's red-thread pairs off the high ledges of the Roosts, and Ulric's grey-thread pairs from the hold's lower cliffs, together. Thirty-four dragons. Calder counted them as they came off the cliffs into the grey sky, one after another, wheeling, finding each other, red and grey and bronze and black and green, with riders on their necks.

The first time, Gunna told him afterwards, that the two halves had flown together since Teo.

Since before Teo. Since Haldor. Since the quarrel.

He went up on Kharrak in the middle of them. Lisska on his shoulder, because she said she'd done enough flying this month, which she hadn't, and he knew she just wanted to be there.

Aurveth stayed on the ledge. She lifted her head as they went and watched them go, and he felt her watching till they were over the edge of the moor.

---

They found the column on the Cotter's Cross road.

In the Ashlings. The Saltreach scrub, south of the Fellwood, low and grey-green and thorny, going away to the Cross on its hill. A road through it, the old drove-road to the Cross. And on the road, the companies.

A long column. Longer than he'd thought. Men on foot, and men on horses, and carts. Carts piled with things. Sacks. Furniture. A plough. Somebody's door. A cart with a cow tied behind it, lowing. And behind the column, a long way back, on the river side, smoke going up. Black. A steading burning. Their last.

He saw the badges from the air. On their coats, on their cart-cloths, on a flag at the head of the column. The red hand. The green boar. The black ship.

Paid in new silver with a ring in the rim. And not paid now.

---

Arn's word had been plain.

*Break it,* he'd said in the hall. *Not butcher it. Break it. I want it scattered so it never comes back together. I don't want a field of dead men in the Ashlings with the Clan's name on them. I've had enough of that.*

So they broke it.

The first flight went down on the carts.

Fire. Long rushes of it, along the column, over the carts, the sacks, the stolen furniture, the plunder. Not on the men. On the things. The carts went up one after another in a line down the road, yellow, roaring, and the horses screamed and broke their traces and bolted into the scrub, and the men round the carts threw themselves flat or ran.

The second flight went down on the road ahead. A wall of fire across it, in the scrub, so that the head of the column stopped.

The third went down into the column itself.

Not fire. Dragons. Landing. On the road, in among the men, red and grey, wings wide, roaring. Not biting. Not burning. Landing, and roaring, and the men in front of them turning and running.

It broke.

He saw it break. From Kharrak's back, over it all. A long column of men on a road, and then not a column. Men going everywhere. Into the scrub on both sides. Back down the road toward the river, where the smoke was. South, toward the Reedmarsh, the reed-beds below the Cross, where the poachers lived and nobody could follow. North, toward the Marches. In twos and threes and tens. Throwing down what they carried. Running.

Bands. Not an army.

---

*Those,* said Kharrak.

He'd seen them. A band of twenty, maybe, running south through the scrub toward the Reedmarsh. With their badges on. Red hand.

*They burned Upper Rush,* said Kharrak. *Twice. Your sister's village. I can smell it on them.* He was already turning. *Let me.*

"No."

*Calder.*

"Not the ones running," said Calder. "The ones running are done."

*They burned it.*

"I know. Not the ones running." He put his hand flat on Kharrak's neck, on the hot plates. "That's what Arn said. That's what I say. They're done. Look at them. They're running into a marsh with nothing. That's done."

Kharrak didn't turn.

He flew on over them, low, so low that the band of red hands below him threw themselves flat in the thorns as his shadow went over, and he roared, once, so loud that Calder's ears rang. And then he went up, and away, and didn't come back.

*At Crow Scar you wouldn't let me either,* he said. A long time after. Sulking.

"I know."

*You were right then too,* said Kharrak. *I hate it when you're right.*

---

He saw the cart as they went up.

Going fast, west, down a side-track out of the scrub. One horse, a big one, going at a gallop. A thin man on the box, bent over, whipping it. And on the cart behind him, lashed down with ropes, a shape under a cloth.

The cloth had blown back at one corner. He saw what was under it. Iron. A screw. A great flat plate. A press. A printing-press, like the one he'd seen in the back yard of the Fleece at Cotter's Cross in the spring, with a thin man bent over it, inking.

Reede.

He knew him by the shoulders. By the way he sat, bent. The man who'd printed every bill he'd ever seen with the ring of six on it. The one in his coat, that he'd carried since the spring.

Kharrak was turning a grey dragon off a band of boys. Boys, fifteen, sixteen, with the black ship on their sleeves, crying in a ditch. Calder had his hand up, shouting at the grey's rider. When he looked back, the cart was gone into the scrub, and the press with it.

---

Corvin wasn't there.

He looked for him. He knew he wouldn't find him. He looked anyway, all the way along the burning road, for a lean man on a horse, a lean shape at a window. Nobody had seen him in the Marches since the autumn. Since the Halloran fall. He was somewhere south. The three young ones had gone to Sarovel, Maren had written in the autumn. He'd have gone where they went.

The companies had been paid by a man who'd never once come to see them.

---

Rowan's knights were on the fords.

He flew over them on the way back. Low, over the Wend, where the old fords above Ashford went across the river in long shallow stretches of brown water and shingle. On the Hearthmere bank, on every ford, a line of grey. Knights. Not many. A long thin line, with pikes, standing in the shallows and on the bank. Holding. Nobody had come back across. Nobody would.

In the middle of the line, on the biggest ford, a small figure in a grey surcoat on a thin horse.

She looked up as Kharrak's shadow went over.

She lifted her sword. Once. Straight up.

He lifted his hand.

---

They came home on the two-hundred-and-ninetieth day.

All thirty-four. None badly hurt. A grey had a burn on one wing from flying too close to a cart. A red had a bolt in the soft skin under a foreleg that Ivar Leck took out with pincers on the setters' ledge while the dragon swore at him in a way Calder could feel from across the cliff.

Arn and Ulric sat at the two hearths that night. With the chair between them under its grey cloth.

"It's over," said Ulric. "As a war."

"As a war," said Arn.

"There'll be bands. In the Reedmarsh. On the Saltreach bank. For years." Ulric looked at the fire. "Men who've got nothing and learned to burn."

"There'll be bands," said Arn. "There won't be an army."

They looked at the chair.

Neither of them took the cloth off it. It wasn't time. The Halloran house had been named, in a Council room in Corsa, and in a square in Hallowmere, and in a mint-book. The man at the Fleece hadn't been named in any of them. *Every one,* Arn had said. When he was, both halves would pour for the chair at the black water.

Not yet.

---

He read Maren's postscript to Kharrak that night.

On the setters' ledge, by the light of the fire the Clan kept there on cold nights. Kharrak was lying along the ledge with his head on his forefeet, still sulking a little about the band of red hands. Lisska was asleep on Aurveth's back. Aurveth was asleep.

"She says something about you," said Calder.

Kharrak opened one eye.

Calder unfolded the letter. He turned it to the bottom. He put his finger under it, though he didn't need to now, he'd read it so many times.

"*Orrun says,*" he read. Out loud. Slowly. "*Your red one's too loud.*"

Kharrak didn't say anything for a while.

*Who's Orrun?* he said.

"The lion."

There was a very long silence on the ledge.

*He's too big,* said Kharrak.

From Aurveth's back, without opening her eyes, Lisska said: *That's what I said.*
