---

There were lions on the doors.

She saw them on the morning she left. All down the street of the low quarter where the stable was. On the mud-brick walls, on the doors, on the shutters. Painted. In mud, mostly, brown on brown. In ochre where somebody had it, yellow-red. One in black soot from a cooking-pot. One, on the door of the wine-inn where the Saltreach captain had shouted, in gold leaf, scraped very carefully off something that had been gold before.

Lions. With manes like rocks. Lying down, mostly. With their heads up.

"They've been doing it all week," said Tobin. He was standing in the stable door with a piece of bread. "At night. Nobody says who."

Maren looked at the gold one for a long time.

"Who taught them what he looks like?"

"Nobody," said Tobin. "They've seen him. Every one of them's seen him. He lies across the end of the street with his head in a stable." He ate the bread. "You can't miss him, Maren."

---

Tobin stayed.

She'd known he would. The old man who owned the stable had taken to him. The unchosen grooms who worked the beast-stables up in the white city had taken to him. He talked to everyone. He sat in the wine-inn at night and talked to dock-workers and fish-wives and stable-hands about Greyhold, and the ditches at the Scald, and being unchosen, loud, as he'd been loud about it at the back of every line at home, and they listened to him as nobody at home ever had.

"Somebody should be here," he said. "When Idony comes. If he comes."

"He'll come."

"Say it twice," said Tobin.

She didn't. She put her hand on his shoulder and went out to where Orrun lay.

---

The Elderwood was a day north.

Up the west side of the Gilt's valley, out of the vineyards, out of the olive terraces, into steep hills where nobody farmed. The king's forest. At its edge, where the road ended, a gibbet. Two of them. Old, grey wood, with the iron still on them. And on one, in its iron, bones.

*Poachers,* the old man had said. *The king's foresters hang them. They've not hanged one in a year because nobody's been fool enough.*

Orrun stopped by the gibbet and looked at the bones.

He didn't say anything. He went on.

Into the trees.

---

Old trees. She'd thought the Thornwood was old. This was older. Evergreen oaks, enormous, with grey trunks twisted like rope and leaves dark and hard and shining. Cork-trees with their bark like a sponge. Yews, black-trunked, so old they'd split down the middle and grown round themselves. On steep hills, with no paths, the ground soft with a hundred years of leaves.

And bright.

The Grace was in it. Thick. Thicker than on the Sun Road. Every leaf too green. The air sweet, so sweet it made her teeth ache. The light coming down through the oaks in long bars, gold, so bright it hurt her eyes, in the middle of winter.

Orrun went in slowly. With his head low. As if he were walking into deep water.

"They are here," he said, at noon.

"Where?"

"All round." He didn't look. "Watching. They have been watching since the gibbet."

She looked. She saw trees.

"They kill hunters," she said. "The old man said."

"I am not a hunter," said Orrun. "They know."

---

They slept in a clearing that night. A round space among the oaks where one had fallen a long time ago and left a hole in the canopy. She lay against Orrun's flank with the stars in the hole above her, and the sweet air, and the silence.

Not silence. She could hear them. Very faint. A step, in the leaves, a long way off. And another. Cloven hooves, very careful. Going round.

She didn't sleep much.

---

Lisska came at first light.

Maren heard her before she saw her. A sound in the canopy, a crash of leaves, and somebody swearing in a way that only Orrun could hear and that made Orrun lift his head and blink. And then the small green dragon came down through the hole in the oaks into the clearing, badly, scraping a branch, and landed in the leaves beside them, and lay there.

Thin. Tired. Frost on her, from the night flying.

*She says,* said Orrun, *she found the city by the lions.*

"By the..."

*On the gates. On the doors. She says there are lions painted everywhere and she followed them down to the low quarter, and the skinny one saw her on the stable roof and pointed north and shouted a word at her over and over.* Orrun was quiet. *She says the skinny one is very loud.*

"He is."

*She says she has a letter.*

---

It was on her foreleg. In oilcloth. With string.

Maren untied it. Lisska held still with her eyes shut.

A page. A whole page. Grey paper, the back of something printed, she could see the print through it. Charcoal. His hand. Better. The lines didn't go uphill so much. The letters were more the same size. He'd written more.

She unfolded it in the bright cold light of the clearing.

She didn't mean to see the bottom.

She couldn't help it. It was the biggest thing on the page. Bigger than anything else he'd written. Letters as tall as her thumb. Pressed so hard the charcoal had gone through.

*LISS SAYS COME HOME.*

And under it, smaller, on its own:

*LISS.*

---

She stood in the clearing with the letter in her hand.

She hadn't heard her mother's name said aloud since the autumn. She hadn't seen it written ever. Her mother couldn't write. Somebody had written it for her. A farmer, with charcoal, who could hardly write himself.

*Come home.*

Two words. She could hear them. In her mother's voice. Not asking. Liss never asked. Saying it, the way she said *eat* or *sit down before you fall down* or *that coat's not warm enough.*

*Come home.*

She didn't read the rest.

She looked at it. She saw that it was lines, a lot of lines, his lines. She saw a word she knew without reading it, because it was a name, *Corvin*, and another, *Blackwater*. She didn't read them.

She folded it.

---

"She says he says read it," said Orrun.

"Not yet."

*She says why.*

Maren put the letter inside her coat. In the pocket. Behind the black book. With the other one, his first, with her own words on its back. She put her hand flat on her coat over them.

"Because if I read it I'll go," she said.

She didn't know until she'd said it. She heard herself say it in the bright clearing and knew it was true.

"If I read it I'll go home. And it's on the hill. Over the city. And the stair's under the palace. And Idony's in the palace." She looked at the small green dragon lying in the leaves. "I can't go home yet."

Lisska looked at her for a long time with her pale green eyes.

*She says she'll wait,* said Orrun. *For an answer. She says she always waits. She says you always say you'll write.*

"Tell her I will."

*She says she knows.*

Lisska went up into the oak above the clearing and lay along a great grey branch with her tail hanging down and went to sleep.

---

The unicorns came out on the fourth evening.

They'd been round all the time. Three days. Watching. Going round the clearing at a distance in the trees with their careful cloven feet. She'd seen one, once, on the second afternoon. A shape between two oaks. Grey. Gone.

At dusk on the three-hundred-and-eighth day, they came out.

---

Eleven.

Out of the trees on the far side of the clearing, one after another, into the last of the light. Slowly. Very quietly. She didn't hear their feet on the leaves at all.

Not white. Not horses. She'd seen the painted signs on the stage-houses on the Sun Road, white horses with gold horns, and the one on the palace stair chalked white for the feast. These were nothing like the signs. Lean. Deer-boned. With long fine legs and narrow heads and cloven hooves. Grey, some of them. Silver-grey. Two almost black, a black with silver in it, like charcoal with ash on it. And each one with a single horn, long, spiralled, the colour of old ivory. Bone. Living bone.

They stopped at the edge of the clearing.

The one in front was old. She could see it. Nearly black, gone grey round the muzzle and the eyes. Thin, with her ribs showing under the dark coat. And her horn was broken. The tip of it gone, a hand's length, clean, as if it had been cut, long ago, and worn smooth since.

She came forward alone. Into the clearing.

Ten paces from Orrun, she stopped.

---

Ten paces.

The old tusker cow had stopped at ten paces, in the high bowl under the snowline. Orrun had stopped at ten paces from the Titan at the Ring, and lain down. It was the distance, she thought. The one you stopped at, if you were old enough to know.

Orrun got up.

Slowly. To his full height. He'd grown so much in the winter that he stood up out of the clearing like a tower, and the oak branches brushed his back. The old mare looked up at him. Her ears went back, and then forward.

He put his head down.

Low. Lower. Till his chin was almost on the leaves. As Gunna had bowed to him on the edge, the first day, the old way.

The mare put her head down too.

---

They talked all night.

Maren didn't hear any of it. There wasn't anything to hear. The old mare stood at ten paces with her broken horn lowered and Orrun lay with his chin on the leaves, and neither of them made a sound, and the other ten stood at the edge of the clearing in the dark, and the stars came out in the hole in the canopy.

But she felt it. Through Orrun. Not the words. The weight of them. Something old being handed to him, piece by piece, very carefully, as you'd hand someone a thing you'd carried a long way and were afraid to drop.

He told her at dawn.

---

"They remember," said Orrun.

He hadn't moved all night. His chin on the leaves. His eyes open.

"The old realm. As I do. The road. The white bridge. The cat on the steps." He was quiet. "Her grandmother's grandmother's grandmother walked on the road. To the bridge. With the people. Before."

"Before what?"

"Before the kings," said Orrun.

---

"Nine," he said.

The sun was coming up. Gold, through the oaks. Too bright.

"Nine kings ago. The first Bond-King came into this wood. With smoke. With sweet smoke, in pots, on poles. And men." He was very still. "And took a foal. From her grandmother's grandmother's... from a mare of the herd. Small. A year old. Too small to run fast in the smoke."

Maren didn't say anything.

"They put gold round its neck," said Orrun. "And a hand on its head. And it never came back to the trees. And the people below the walls were told that the king and the unicorn had chosen each other. That it was a bond. The first unicorn bond in all the world." He breathed out, long. "And they believed it. And the king was the Bond-King. And his son. And when it died, the son came into the wood with the smoke again."

"Nine times," said Maren.

"Nine foals. Nine kings. The one on the stair now is the ninth." He lifted his head a little off the leaves. "They have a word for it. In the herd. For the one on the stair. It means the one who was taken in the snow. It was taken in a snow, twelve winters ago. The mare remembers. It was her daughter's."

---

The old mare was standing at ten paces.

She hadn't moved either. In the gold light. With her broken horn lowered and her dark eyes on Maren. Not on Orrun now. On her.

"There has never been a king with a unicorn," said Orrun. "She says. In all nine. There has never been one. There have been nine kings with a prisoner."

Maren looked at the old mare.

She thought of the stair. The procession. The silver-grey beast chalked white, stepping down the white stone with its cloven feet ringing, every step perfect, the gilded horn, the gold collar, the crowned man on its back, the crowd on the walls cheering. And its eyes.

Nobody behind them.

Twelve winters. Taken in the snow. Her daughter's.

"Her horn," said Maren. "The tip."

Orrun was quiet a moment.

"The fourth king," he said. "She was young. She went into the smoke after a foal. They cut it off her with a saw while she lay in the smoke, because the horn of a unicorn is worth a farm. She came back out of the smoke and the foal didn't."

---

"There's more," said Orrun.

"Tell me."

"Their kin," he said. "Under the palace."

She waited.

"Under the stair. Under the place at the bottom of the stair, where the crown goes to kneel. The shrine. The one the stage-houses sing to at dusk." He looked south, through the trees, toward the city she couldn't see. "The Sun on the Stair."

"It's a sleeper," said Maren.

"The one with the horn," said Orrun. "On the wall. Under the Ring, and under the Scald. The one with one horn and the sun round its head. It is their kin. Far back. Very far. As Kharrak is mine." He was quiet. "They have always known. The herd. Since before the kings. It is why they never leave the wood and the hills above the bay. They are keeping watch on it."

"And the crown kneels on top of it."

"And calls it the Sun on the Stair," said Orrun. "And says it lit the first lamp and gave it to the crown." He put his chin back down on the leaves. "She says the crown has never known what it kneels on. Nine kings. Not one has asked."

*And I will not ask what I keep.*

She stood in the gold light and thought of a noon oath, in a yard, facing a wall.

---

"They're frightened," said Maren.

"Yes."

"Of the one on the hill."

"It is going to take the sleep," said Orrun. "Out of the stair. As it took it from the Scald and from Vael and from the Ring. Soon. She can feel it, as I can. The stone over the stair is filling. Like a cup." He was quiet. "And when the sleep is taken, their kin will wake."

"And the one on the stair..."

"Will still be on the stair," said Orrun. "With gold round its neck. With a hand on its head. When its kin wakes and stands up out of the cliff."

He didn't say anything else for a long time.

She felt him grieve. Kin to kin. For the one taken in the snow. For the foal the old mare had gone into the smoke after. For nine of them. For a thing under a cliff that had slept since before the kings and was about to wake up to this.

---

The old mare came forward on the second morning.

The ten paces. Slowly. Through the leaves, with her cloven feet making no sound, to Orrun lying with his chin on the ground. She put her head down. And touched the broken end of her horn to his mane. Once. Where the broken-rock mane was thickest, on his neck.

Then she turned and went back across the clearing.

The others went with her. One after another. Into the trees. Grey and silver-black. Gone.

Orrun lay where she'd left him and didn't move for a long time.

---

They stayed in the clearing one more night.

She didn't know why. Orrun didn't want to go. He lay with his chin on the leaves where the mare had touched him, and looked at the place in the trees where they'd gone, and she sat against him.

Lisska was asleep in the oak above them. Along the great grey branch, with her tail hanging down. Waiting. She'd been waiting four days. She'd wait, she said, as long as it took.

Maren took out the letter once. In the dark. She held it.

She didn't unfold it. She knew what was at the bottom. She could feel the letters through the paper with her thumb, the charcoal gone through, the biggest letters on the page.

*Come home.*

She put it back behind the black book.

"Tomorrow," she said. To Orrun. To the dark. "We go back to the city tomorrow."

"Yes," said Orrun.

"Idony said two weeks. Perhaps less. It's been ten days."

"Yes," said Orrun. "It is filling. I can feel it from here."

She lay down against him and looked up through the hole in the canopy at the stars, too bright, and the dark shape of the small green dragon on the branch, asleep, waiting for an answer she hadn't written.
