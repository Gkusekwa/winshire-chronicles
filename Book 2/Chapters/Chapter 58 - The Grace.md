---

The carriage didn't go to the front of the palace.

He'd thought it would. White and gold, four white horses, a man in livery on the box. He'd sat in it on the white leather seat with his stick across his knees and the case of glasses on his lap and watched the white city go past the window, the domes, the colonnades, the bond-crests over the noble doors, the people stepping out of the road for the royal horses. He'd thought: they're taking me in at the great gate.

They took him round the back.

Along the cliff, below the palace walls. Through a gate in a wall with no crest over it. Down a ramp, cut into the rock, into the cliff itself. The carriage stopped at the bottom in a yard full of carts and barrels and men in brown carrying things, and the man in livery got down and opened the door.

"The hands' hall," he said. "Below."

Idony got out.

He looked up. At the white wall of the palace going up and up over his head, with its hundred windows. And at the ramp going down. And at the men in brown.

He thought: of course.

---

The hands' hall was under the west wing.

Cut into the cliff. Long, vaulted rooms, one after another, going back into the rock, with benches down them and tables, and at the end of each a window cut through the cliff-face to the sea, small, deep, like a window in a castle wall. And lamps.

He stopped in the doorway of the first room and looked at the lamps.

Plain glass. Globes of plain glass on iron brackets on the walls, with nothing in them. No oil. No wick. No rune that he could see. And they burned. White. Steady. So bright the vaulted ceiling was as clear as noon.

"The Grace," said a voice. "It's that bright here. You put a glass on a bracket and it fills. Like a cup under a spring."

An old man was sitting at the nearest bench. Brown robe. Thin white hair. Eyes gone milky, both of them, so that he looked at Idony's left shoulder instead of his face.

"Sebold," he said. "Master of Hands. Sixty years below." He held out a hand in Idony's direction. "And you'll be the Kessmoor measurer. Who isn't from Kessmoor."

---

There were forty of them. Fifty.

He learned it in the first days. The court's hands. Men and women in brown robes, unbonded, every one, with the knack. They sat at the benches in the vaulted rooms under the palace and they did the court's workings.

All of them.

The warming of the rooms upstairs, in the winter: a hand below each room, with her palms on the floor of the room above. The lighting of the great hall. The singing of the fountains in the palace gardens, which the court said sang because the Grace loved the Bond-King: a hand in a cellar under each fountain, humming into a pipe. The healing of the nobles' headaches in the mornings. The gleam on the bonded lords' beasts in the procession: a hand in the stable at dawn with her hands on each beast's coat.

And at the banquets, when a chosen lord rose from his chair and lifted his hand and fire went up over the tables in a crown, to applause: a hand behind a screen at the back of the hall, drawing.

"We are the Grace, Reader," said Sebold. "They are the gratitude."

---

He knew himself in them.

He didn't say so. He sat at the bench Sebold gave him with his glasses and his silk, among the brown robes, and watched them work, and knew them.

Eleven years at a reading-desk at the foot of a perch. Taking the readings. Doing the sums. Drawing the charts. Correcting the College's great chart of the tides, every winter, line by line. And carrying them up the hill to a Fifth ring in a blue collar, who signed them. Whose name went on them. Whose readings they were, in the Hall of Rings.

A Second ring who read like a Sixth.

*Reader Vael.* A hand.

He thought: I was below stairs the whole time. I just had a better stair.

---

The Lady Aurel received him once.

On the second day, upstairs, in a white room with tall windows on the bay and a gilded ceiling. She sat in a white chair and smiled at him with her bright interested eyes, and asked him about the Scald, and the cage, and the inspector with the dry face, and how the lion was. She said she was so glad he'd come. She said the Bond-King had been so worried about his collars.

She didn't mention her secretary.

Then a man in livery took him back down the stair, and he was on the household's books, Sebold told him, as *the Kessmoor measurer, a hand*.

---

Sebold took him to the stables on the third day.

The royal stables. On the cliff, east of the palace, a palace of their own. Marble. Columns. A roof of gilded tiles. Inside, stalls the size of houses, each with a crest over it in gold, and clean straw, and a hand in brown in every one, grooming.

The bonded beasts of the court.

He walked down the long marble aisle between them with Sebold's hand on his arm. Gold-coated hounds the size of ponies. Great spotted cats on chains of gold. A bear, white, with a jewelled collar. Horses that weren't horses, too-long in the leg, with eyes like coals. Olifants, at the end, in a stall like a barn, three of them, grey, enormous, with their trunks swinging and their tusks gilded at the tips.

And at the very end, alone, in a stall of white marble with a gilded sun over its gate, the unicorn.

---

It was standing.

Lean. Deer-boned. Silver-grey, without the chalk now, its coat like ash. Its horn spiralled up from its forehead, the colour of old ivory, gilded at the tip. Its cloven feet in the straw.

It was looking at the wall.

At nothing. Its eyes open, dark, with nobody behind them.

And round its neck the collar. Gold. A hand's breadth wide. Engraved on the outside with suns and crowns and the Bond-King's crest. Very beautiful.

"Since the beast lay down on the hill," said Sebold. Quietly. "They've been cracking. Six of them. The gold." He held his milky eyes on the unicorn's stall, or near it. "And two in the iron. Underneath." He was quiet. "His is one."

---

Idony read it.

Not the gold. The gold was for show. Under the gold, inside it, against the beast's neck, iron. A band of plain dark iron, and on its inner face, where you'd never see it unless you took it off, the hooked script.

Very fine. Very small. Sarovel's court hand, close and elegant, with long tails on the hooks. He'd never seen it. He read it anyway.

He had to have the collar off the beast to see the inside. He couldn't. He read what he could see, at the edge, where the iron showed under the gold at the clasp. A line. Two. And the crack, a hair-line, running across the iron.

Not *hold*, nor *turn back*, nor *within*.

A shaping.

---

He knew it from the grammar.

Not from the chapter on binding. Nobody in Vael would ever have written a chapter on binding. From the chapter on shaping. The greater shapings, the ones the College didn't teach, the ones that changed a living thing's nature, that the grammar listed in a long dry paragraph at the end of the chapter with a note that said *forbidden everywhere but Sarovel's court* and nothing else.

A binding, at bottom, was a greater shaping done on a beast's self.

That was what this was. Cut into the iron. Fed by the Grace, which here was everywhere, in everything, in plain glass on a bracket. A working that took the beast's self out of it, and kept it out, and left the shape behind to be ridden down a stair.

Nine generations of it.

*Kept out*, he thought. Not destroyed. Kept out. Like a breath held.

---

And he knew the unmaking.

That was the thing. He stood in the white marble stall in front of the silver-grey beast with the gilded horn and the cracked collar, with Sebold's hand on his arm, and he knew it.

Every shaping has its unmaking. It's the first page of the grammar's chapter on shaping. The same rune, read backward, with the closing word turned. Every first-year learns that there is one. It's the last thing anyone is ever taught to say, because nobody ever unmakes anything. The colleges make. They don't unmake.

He'd read the page at nineteen. For a citation. He'd never said the word.

He looked at the line of court hand at the clasp, and read it backward in his head, and turned the closing word.

He didn't say it.

He didn't write it down.

"Well?" said Sebold.

"It's cracking," said Idony. "Because the Grace is too bright. Because of the beast on the hill. It's filling everything. Even the collars." He looked at the unicorn. "They weren't made for this much."

"Can you mend them?"

Idony looked at the dark eyes with nobody behind them.

"I'm a reader," he said. "I don't mend."

---

He saw Dane on the fourth day.

At a distance. In a gallery upstairs, where Sebold had sent him with a message for the chamberlain. A long gallery of white marble with portraits of nine Bond-Kings on unicorns. And at the end of it, by a window, the chamberlain in gold and white, and with him, a tall man in grey with a blue collar, very straight, with gloves on his hands and a roll of drawings under his arm.

Selling the College's chain-craft. Again. To another crown.

Dane looked up and saw him.

Idony waited.

Dane looked at him for a long moment down the length of the gallery, past the nine kings. Then he looked away, and unrolled his drawings on the windowsill for the chamberlain, and didn't look back.

---

Corvin found him on the eighth night.

In the stables. At the unicorn's stall. Where Idony went every night, now, after the hands' hall had emptied, to look at the cracked collar. To see if the crack had grown. It had. Every night. A little.

He was standing at the white marble gate with a lantern when he heard someone come down the aisle behind him.

Not a groom. Not a hand. He knew their feet now. This was a different walk. Quiet. Even. Unhurried.

He turned round.

---

A tall man. Thin. Very still, even walking. A long pale face. Short dark hair. Grey eyes. Neither young nor old. In a plain dark coat with nothing on it, no crest, no chain. No guards.

At the far end of the aisle, by the stable door, a man with a grey beard stood with his hands behind his back.

"Reader Vael," said the tall man.

His voice was quiet. It didn't need to be anything else.

"Corvin," said Idony.

---

"You know me."

"Your coin," said Idony. "With a ring of six in the rim. Your captain." He nodded down the aisle. "He shot the bird."

"He did," said Corvin. He didn't look round at the captain. "I didn't ask him to. I asked him for the case. The bird wouldn't give it." He came and stood beside Idony at the white gate and looked at the unicorn. "I'm told it died very well."

Idony didn't say anything.

"I know who you are," said Corvin. "The Long Library's reader. Second ring, broken. The one who walked out of the colleges' binding in the middle of the form." He looked at the unicorn. "The one who sat at a desk at the foot of a perch one night in the summer with his palm on a roll of vellum, and read it, all night."

"Who told you?"

"Somebody in your college's own household," said Corvin. "Who keeps a lantern owl." He said it without any weight at all. "The College keeps its letters, Reader. And its servants keep copies."

---

He had the case with him.

Under his arm. Dark wood. Tarnished silver. The College of Tides' mark on the lid, the wave over the glass. He knelt in the straw outside the unicorn's stall, carefully, and set it down, and opened it.

The roll. The appendix, folded under it.

"I've had it since the autumn," said Corvin. "I can't read a word of it." He looked up at Idony from the straw. "Nobody in Sarovel can. Nobody in Saltreach. Your two old men in the Sixth-ring house won't leave the house. I sent to ask. They sent my man back with his letter unopened."

He took the appendix out. He unfolded it on the straw. The rune of holding, drawn stroke by stroke. And under it, the working-words.

"These," he said. "The words. For speaking to the iron. Teach me."

---

"Why?" said Idony.

Corvin looked at him.

"You've worked it out," he said. "I can see you have. You worked it out at Kessmoor, on that tower, I expect. The chain-ends on its legs still say *hold*. They still feed. A working that's fed answers its words." He laid his long hand flat on the appendix. "Whoever can say these over the chain-ends has a hand on it."

"Yes."

"You want to speak to a Titan," said Corvin. Quietly. "So do I."

---

"What for?" said Idony.

"To ask it something."

"What?"

Corvin was quiet for a long time. He looked at the unicorn standing in its stall with its empty eyes and its cracked collar.

"That's mine," he said.

He stood up. He didn't close the case.

"I'll give you this," he said. "The roll. To read. All of it. To keep. And every book in the Bond-King's library, which is better than you'd think. And a room. And people to ask you questions. A college of your own, if you want one." He looked at Idony. "You were never let ask anything in Vael. You were told not to. Here you could ask everything."

*Ask, ferryman. Ask everything.*

---

He wanted to say yes.

He stood at the unicorn's gate with the lantern in his hand and wanted it so much his chest hurt.

The roll. To read again. All of it. Not in one night with a bird's eye on him. Slowly. Every line. The contract and the appendix and whatever else the College had cut out of its own index. And a room. And books. And someone, for once in his life, coming to him and asking what he knew instead of telling him not to know it.

He looked at the open case in the straw.

He thought of the bird. Sitting down on the case with three bolts in its breast. Putting its head down. Not lifting it.

He thought of a hundred magisters on a terrace saying *and do not ask* with their bare feet on the stone.

He thought of a crate of hearts in the straw at the Scald.

"No," said Idony.

---

Corvin didn't seem surprised.

He bent and closed the case. Carefully. He tucked it under his arm.

"You'll say yes," he said. "Not tonight." He looked at the unicorn once more. "But you'll want to know what it says, when I speak to it. You won't be able to bear not knowing." He turned to go. "You're a reader. That's what readers are."

He went back up the aisle, past the gilded stalls, past the hounds and the cats and the white bear, to where the man with the grey beard was waiting, and they went out into the night.

Idony stood at the gate.

The unicorn hadn't moved. It stood looking at the wall.

The crack in its collar had grown a hair's breadth since the evening.

---

The Grace grew brighter every day.

He measured it. From the window in the hands' hall, the one at the end of the long vaulted room, cut through the cliff. It faced north. Toward the hills. He'd asked Sebold for that bench and Sebold had given it to him without asking why.

Every evening he set his four glasses on the deep stone sill and watched them lean.

North. Up. Toward the ridge of the Sunward Hills above the palace, where the grey shape lay with its wings folded like a roof. All four. Harder every day.

The lamps in the hands' hall began to crack.

The plain glass globes on their brackets, filling and filling with the Grace until it was too much for them. On the ninth day one burst, in the third room, with a sound like a bell, and a hand screamed. On the tenth, three. Sebold had them taken down. The hands worked by daylight from the windows after that, and the cliff-rooms went dim.

The fountains sang by themselves at night. In the palace gardens. With no hand under them. He heard them from his bench, through the window. High. Wordless. Like the glass moths at the Scald.

The collars cracked. Ten of them now, Sebold said. The unicorn's crack had gone right across the iron.

---

The drawer burned on the ninth night.

At a banquet in the great hall. Idony saw it. Sebold had sent him up with a message and he'd stood at the back of the hall by the screens, where the hands stood, in his brown robe, unnoticed, and watched.

A long hall. White. Gilded. Tables down it under a hundred lamps burning so white they hurt. The court in silk with their bonded beasts lying under the tables. The Bond-King at the high table in white and gold. And beside him, a guest in a plain dark coat with nothing on it.

A chosen lord stood up from his chair. A handsome man in green with a bonded cat at his feet. He lifted his hand.

Behind the screen at the back of the hall, a hand drew.

Idony felt it. Through the floor. A pull, from the ground under the palace, up through the man behind the screen. Too much. Far too much. The ground was too full. The man behind the screen had drawn as he always drew, for a crown of fire over the tables, and the Grace had come up through him like a river through a broken sluice.

Fire went up over the tables.

Not a crown. A wall. A sheet of it, white, roaring, up to the gilded ceiling. And it didn't stop.

The court applauded.

They didn't understand. Idony watched them not understand. They clapped, in their silk, at the white fire going up over their tables, the best show the lord in green had ever given. They clapped for the space of ten breaths. The lord in green stood with his hand up, smiling, not knowing why it wouldn't stop.

Behind the screen, the man was screaming.

Idony went round the screen.

He was on his knees. A hand. Young. In brown. With his palms flat on the floor and fire going up out of him into the hall, and his hair going white. Idony watched it go. From the roots out. Black to grey to white in the time it took to run three steps. As Pask's had gone, on the stone at the Ring.

Then it stopped.

The fire over the tables went out. The court stopped clapping. Somebody laughed, uncertainly.

The young hand fell forward on his face behind the screen, grey, shaking, his hands smoking, and didn't get up.

Sebold had him carried below.

The lord in green bowed to the applause.

---

He gave it two weeks.

He'd known it on the first night, at the window, with the glasses leaning. He wrote it on the tenth, in the silk, in the smallest hand he had.

*Two weeks from the day it lay down. The three-hundred-and-fifteenth. Near noon. Every time, less.*

At the Ring, three months. At Vael, seven weeks. At the Scald, a month. Here, two weeks. The cup filling faster every time. The beast thirstier every time, with every relic it took into its back.

He looked at the line. He thought of the stair. The long white stair down the cliff from the palace to the sea. And at the bottom of it, the shrine. The Sun on the Stair, who lit the first lamp. The one the hands sang to at dusk in the cliff-rooms, in their brown robes, with their eyes on the floor.

He hadn't been down there. Hands weren't let.

He'd find a way.

---

He went down to the low quarter on the twelfth night.

He'd said he would. *I'll come. I'll come.* Twice, because the loud one had made him.

Out of the hands' hall by the ramp, after dark, in his brown robe, with his stick. Through the gate in the wall with no crest. Down through the white city, through the lamps burning so bright they cast shadows at midnight, and out through the landward gate into the mud-brick quarter below the walls.

The doors had lions on them.

He hadn't seen them. He stopped in the street and looked. On every door. On the shutters. Mud and ochre and soot. Lying down, with their heads up. One in gold leaf.

He found the stable at the end of the street.

---

A stable built for a horse.

Mud-brick. A thatched roof. One door. And lying along the whole length of its front wall in the street, as long as the stable and longer, a grey lion with a broken-rock mane, asleep, with his head in through the door.

On the thatch, small and green, a dragon. Asleep too. With her tail hanging down over the eaves.

He went to the door and looked in.

Inside, on the straw, against the lion's great head, curled up, with her coat pulled round her and her arm over the lion's nose, Maren. Asleep. Her face turned into his mane. Her boots still on. Mud on them to the knee, from the Elderwood.

He stood in the doorway and looked at her for a long time.

---

"Don't wake her," said Tobin.

He'd come down the ladder from the loft. Quietly, for him. He stood beside Idony in the doorway.

"She came back this afternoon," he said. "From the wood. She's been walking since dawn. She's got two letters in her coat she won't read." He looked at her. "Don't wake her."

Idony didn't.

He told Tobin instead. Standing in the street outside the stable, in the bright cold night, low. The day. The three-hundred-and-fifteenth, near noon. The collars. The drawer behind the screen. Dane in the gallery. The tall man with the grey eyes in the stables at night, with the case, kneeling in the straw.

"He asked you," said Tobin.

"He asked me."

"What did you say?"

"No."

Tobin looked at him for a long moment in the light from the lamps on the wall above.

"Say it twice," he said.

Idony opened his mouth.

He didn't say anything.

He went back up through the white city to the palace before dawn, and sat at his bench under the window in the dim cliff-room, and set out his four glasses on the sill, and watched them lean.
