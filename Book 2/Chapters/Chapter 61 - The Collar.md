---

At noon the windows went.

He was at his bench at the end of the third room in the hands' hall, with his four glasses on the deep stone sill of the window cut through the cliff, watching them lean. They'd leaned all morning. Harder than they'd ever leaned. North, up, toward the ridge. Then, an hour before noon, not toward the ridge. Toward the palace. Toward him. Toward the stone under his feet.

Then they stopped leaning and stood straight up in their throats, all four, the quicksilver pressed to the very top, trembling.

The peak.

He heard the bell ring noon, far up. He heard the court begin to sing. He heard the windows go.

---

All of them. Above him. Every window in the palace at once. A long high shattering roar coming down through the cliff, through the stone, through the vaulted ceiling of the hands' hall, so that dust came down off the vaults in sheets.

In the room above the third room, where a hand lay every day with her palms on the floor to warm a lady's bedchamber, the floor blazed.

He saw it. Through the vault. Through the stone. A white light, coming down through the ceiling in the shape of two hands, two palms, spread, burning through the rock. Then a scream from above. Then the light went out.

Sebold was on his feet at the end of the room.

"Out," he said. His blind eyes on the doorway. "All of you. Out. By the ramp. Now."

The hands went.

Idony didn't. He picked up his stick and went the other way.

---

Hands weren't let on the stair.

Nobody was stopping anybody. He went up the ramp out of the hands' hall, through the yard of carts and barrels, through the gate with no crest, along the cliff-top path under the palace walls with the glass still falling from the windows above, a glittering rain on the path, crunching under his boots. Past the gardens, where the fountains were throwing water higher than the trees and screaming like kettles. Past the chapel, where the court was coming out of the doors in silk, bleeding from the glass, with their bonded beasts howling round them.

To the head of the Sun Stair.

He looked down it.

---

White steps. Zigzagging down the face of the cliff to the sea. Hundreds of them.

And at the bottom, at the landing, where the shrine was, something grey.

The Titan's head. Coming up out of the shrine. Out of a hole where the shrine's dome had been. Up, slowly, through dust and broken marble, with the broken chain-ends on its forelegs swinging against the cliff, and in its jaws, small, grey, an egg.

He watched it turn its head over its own shoulder. He watched it lay the egg in its back.

Four, he thought. Four.

Then it opened its wings and went up off the cliff, past him, so close that the wind of it knocked him off his feet onto the white steps, and its shadow went over him cold, and it was gone. North-west. Over the hills. Away.

He got up.

He went down the stair.

---

He ran.

He hadn't run since Vael. Since Rope Street, the day the Blue tower fell. His back screamed at him on every step. He didn't stop. Down the zigzags of the Sun Stair, with the stick in one hand and the other on the white rail, past the first landing, past the second, down toward the broken shrine at the foot of the cliff where the light was coming up white out of the hole the Titan had left.

The surge. Coming up.

He could feel it in the steps. In the rail under his hand. Going from full to flood.

He stopped at the middle landing. Halfway. He couldn't go further. The steps below the landing had cracked across, and the next flight was hanging off the cliff by its iron pins.

He looked down at the shrine.

Somewhere down there. In the white. Under the stone. In the temple, with the empty plinth.

She was down there. He knew she was. Where else would she be, with the lion?

---

He shouted.

Not her name. Her name was a Hearthmere name, a common-tongue name, and the stone wouldn't carry it, and the lion wouldn't care. He shouted the old tongue's word for what the bird had called the lion. On the first day. In the white light of the Long Library, with the readers backing away and the lion lying down at ten paces.

The one cut on the door under the Scald, round the carved door that wasn't a door.

*Door-keeper.*

He shouted it down the cliff into the white light. As loud as he could. His voice cracked in the middle.

He shouted it again.

Twice. So he'd believe it.

---

The lion came up.

Out of the shrine. Out of the white. Up onto the landing at the cliff's foot, with the sea boiling white round it, and up the stair. The steps that were cracked held for him. The flight that was hanging off the cliff by its pins held for him. Idony didn't know how. He thought perhaps the stone of the stair was old stone, under the white, and it knew him.

On his back, between his shoulders, holding on to the broken-rock mane with both hands, Maren.

The lion came up to the middle landing and stopped and looked at Idony.

Maren looked at him too. Her face was white. Dust in her hair.

"It took it," she said. "He stood in front of it. And it took it."

"I know," said Idony. "I saw."

"What did you shout?"

"His name," said Idony. "The bird's name for him."

The lion looked at him with his gold eyes for a long moment.

Then he went on up the stair, and at the top turned along the cliff-path, east, away from the palace, toward the low quarter. Idony watched them go.

He went back up to the palace.

---

The surge came up for two days.

Every lamp in Solenne went white. The palace and the whole white city. The domed halls, the colonnades, the bond-crested noble houses. The low quarter too. Every dish of oil, every rush, every tallow end, burning white and too bright and cracking its holder.

The bonded nobles' gifts blazed.

He saw it in the palace corridors. A lord in green, the one from the banquet, with his bonded cat at his heels, and the cat was shedding sparks. Gold sparks, off its spotted coat, falling on the marble, and the lord was screaming because the cat's gift was his gift, and it had come up through him, and he couldn't stop it. A lady with a gold hound, and the hound burning. Not on fire. Burning. Gold light coming off its coat like heat off a road, and the lady on her knees beside it holding it, with her hands blistering.

The hands burned out.

One after another. In the cliff-rooms. In the stables. Wherever they were. Sebold had sent them all out by the ramp, and some had come back, because the court needed its rooms warm and its lamps lit and its beasts calm, and the court screamed at them to come. And they came. And they knelt and put their palms to the floor, as they had every day of their lives. And the Grace came up through them and didn't stop.

He stopped counting at eleven.

---

The cliff was cracking.

From the shrine up. He could see it from the palace's broken lower terrace. A crack, running up the face of the cliff from the hole where the shrine had been. Wide. Wider every hour. With the white light coming out of it. And the sea at the cliff's foot boiling white, as if something huge were turning over under it.

The hands knew what it was. They'd whispered it in the cliff-rooms for days. Before the Titan came down.

*The Sun on the Stair.*

*It's waking up.*

---

The Bond-King knew too.

Corvin had told him. Idony heard it from Sebold, who heard it from a hand who'd served at the high table on the ninth night. At dinner. Pleasantly. Between the fish and the fowl. *Your Majesty's shrine is over a sleeping thing. It will wake when the great one takes what's under your altar. When it wakes it will be yours, if you're ready.*

The Bond-King was ready.

---

He set up on the terrace on the three-hundred-and-sixteenth day.

The great upper terrace. Above the cliff. Above the bay. The broken one, where the Titan had come down; half of its balustrade gone into the sea, and the other half cracked. The Bond-King came out onto it at dawn on his unicorn. In white and gold. With the crown on his head. With the silver-grey beast under him in its cracked gold collar, stepping carefully over the broken stone, every step perfect, its eyes empty.

And behind him, the binders.

Forty of them. The court's. In white, not the hands' brown. Chosen lords' binder-hands, the ones who did the court's binding, that nobody in Sarovel admitted the court did. Each one with a honey-pot on a gold chain, smouldering. They came out onto the terrace in a long white line and made a ring.

And on a cart, drawn by six bonded horses that weren't horses, a collar.

---

Gold. Enormous. As big round as a cart-wheel, as big round as a gate. Hinged. Cut on the outside with suns and crowns and the Bond-King's crest.

He knew what was inside it before he saw.

Iron. A band of plain dark iron on the inner face. And on the iron, the hooked script. Not Sarovel's court hand, long-tailed and elegant. The College's. Square and spare. The rune of holding, and the runes of binding round it.

Dane's drawings.

The court smiths had been making it since the Magister came. Since the autumn. Selling the College's chain-craft. Again. To another crown.

Dane wasn't there. Idony looked for him. A hand who'd been at the harbour said a tall man in grey with gloves had taken ship that morning for the north, on the first ship out, with his drawings under his arm.

---

He understood it on the terrace, watching them set up.

He'd read the collars in the stables. He'd known what they were. A greater shaping, cut into a beast's self, taking it out, keeping it out. Nine generations.

He hadn't understood what the king's "bond" was for.

It was a channel.

The whole court's binding ran through it. The forty binders, with their pots and their words, didn't each bind the thing they bound. They poured into the king. Into the king's hand. On the unicorn's head. Through the unicorn's collar. The royal bond was the place all the court's binding went through, as all the Deep went through the bridge on the frost-map. One hand. One will. Nine generations of kings riding a prisoner down a stair, and every binding in Sarovel running through the prisoner's collar into the king's palm.

And now they meant to run it into the thing in the bay.

Through the gold collar on the cart.

He stood at the back of the terrace in his brown robe and nobody looked at him.

---

It woke on the three-hundred-and-seventeenth day.

In the morning. He was on the terrace. He'd slept there, or not slept, against the cracked balustrade at the back, with his glasses. They'd stood straight up in their throats all night.

The cliff broke.

From the shrine upward. With a sound he'd never heard before and never wanted to hear again. Bigger than the Blue tower had been, or the ice at Glasswater. A sound like the end of the world. The whole face of the cliff below the palace, from the sea to the terrace's foot, cracking away from the land along the white-lit crack, and leaning out, and going.

Into the bay.

A cliff. Falling into the sea. Rock and the white stair and the shrine's broken portico and the gilded barge and everything. Going down into the water in a roar and a wall of white spray that went up higher than the palace, higher than the dome, and came down on the terrace like rain.

And out of it, something rose.

---

White-gold.

He saw the horn first. Coming up out of the spray and the falling rock. A spire. Long, spiralled, shining, going up and up, higher than the palace, higher than the hills. Living bone, white and gold, with the light coming off it.

Then the head.

Long. Narrow. Deer-shaped. Like the unicorns in the stables, like the silver-grey one under the king, but white-gold and the size of a hill. And round the head, from behind it, from around the base of the horn, light. Rays. Going out in every direction, white and gold, as the rays go out from the sun at noon on a clear day.

It rose out of the bay with the sea pouring off it in sheets. Rock pouring off its shoulders. Its long fine legs coming up under it. Cloven hooves the size of houses.

It stood in the bay with the sea round its knees.

The Horned Sun.

*The one with one horn and the sun round its head.* On the wall under the Ring. On the wall under the Scald. On the wall of a temple that was falling into the sea behind it in pieces.

It lifted its head and looked at the white city.

---

The binders worked on it.

He saw them start. The forty in their ring on the terrace. The honey-pots on their gold chains, swung out over the broken balustrade, toward the bay. The smoke going out. White, sweet, a great rolling cloud of it, going out over the water toward the thing standing in the sea. He could smell it from the back of the terrace. Sweet. So sweet.

The forty spoke. Together. The binding-words. In the old tongue, the court's version of it, *you're chosen now*, over and over, low, the sound of it going out over the bay with the smoke like the sound of the many-voice working on the terrace at Vael.

And at the edge of the terrace, over the bay, the Bond-King on his unicorn, with his hand on its head. Pouring.

Idony felt it take.

---

In the surge everything worked.

He'd learned that at Vael. At the Scald. Every charm took too hard. Every word answered. A Second-ring reader could cut a rune that held. A boatman's word could have turned a ferry, if there'd been live ground under it.

The binding took.

He felt it go out over the water, through the smoke, into the thing in the bay. He saw the Horned Sun's head dip. Its light dim. The rays round its head going grey, going pale, going in. It stood in the white sweet smoke with the sea round its knees and swayed. Its head going down. Its eyes...

He knew that look. He'd seen it on a yearling on a roof at Vael, standing on the ridge in the frost, not knowing where it was.

It was working.

The Bond-King of Sarovel was binding a waking sleeper, a thing the size of a hill, through his unicorn's collar, from a broken terrace, with forty binders and a cartload of the College's iron. And it was working.

And when it worked, it would be his. Its sleep and its waking and its will. In a gold collar the size of a gate.

---

He went to the unicorn.

He didn't decide. Three times now he hadn't decided. He felt his feet go. Across the terrace. Through the ring of binders. They didn't look at him. They were binding. They had their eyes shut and their pots swinging and their words going out. He was a hand in a brown robe with a stick. Nobody in Sarovel had ever looked at a hand.

He went through the ring to the edge of the terrace, to the broken balustrade, to the king on his unicorn.

The Bond-King didn't look down. He was pouring. His hand on the unicorn's head. His eyes on the bay.

The unicorn stood with its eyes empty.

The gold collar on its neck was cracked right across. Gold and iron. He could see the iron through the crack. The fine long-tailed court hand. The greater shaping, cut into its self. Fed by the Grace. Feeding the king.

He put his mouth to the crack.

---

As Pell had put his mouth to the grey's neck, in the yard at Greyhold, that Maren had told him about on the canal. *You say it into the beast. Not at it. Into it.*

He said the unmaking.

The rune, read backward. The closing word, turned. The first page of the grammar's chapter on shaping, that every first-year knew was there and nobody was ever taught to say. He'd read it at twenty for a citation. He'd read the collar's working in the stables, at the clasp, and turned it in his head, and not said it, and not written it down.

He said it.

Into the iron. Into the crack. Into the beast.

In the surge, everything answered.

---

The collar broke.

He heard it go. Like a bell cracking. The gold, and the iron under it, parting along the crack, in two, and the two halves sliding apart and falling off the unicorn's neck onto the broken stone of the terrace with a sound like dropped plates.

The unicorn's eyes filled.

He was a hand's breadth from them. He saw it. Somebody coming up from the bottom of a well. Fast. Faster than the yearling at Vael. All at once. A self, coming back into a place it had been kept out of for twelve winters, since it was taken in the snow.

It stood still for one breath.

Then it knew where it was.

---

It reared.

Straight up. On its hind legs, on the edge of the broken terrace over the bay, with its forelegs pawing the air and its horn going up. The Bond-King came off its back. Backwards. His hand torn off its head. His crown going one way and his body another.

Onto the stair.

The great upper stair. The white one. The royal one, that came down from the palace's terrace to the city's main square, that the king rode down on feast-days with the crowd on the walls cheering. He landed on its top steps in his white and gold and slid down three of them and lay there.

The unicorn came down off its hind legs.

It turned. It looked at the man on the stair.

Twelve winters. The one taken in the snow.

It put its horn through him.

On his own stair. On the third step. Once. Through the white and gold, through the chest, into the stone. And drew it out. And stood over him, with the blood on its horn of living bone, and looked at him, with somebody behind its eyes.

---

The binding broke.

He felt it go. The whole of it. The forty. All their binding had run through the collar into the king's hand, and the collar was in two pieces on the terrace and the hand was on the stair with nobody behind it. The working that had been going out over the bay into the Horned Sun had nowhere to come from.

It broke like a rope cut.

He heard two of the binders scream. He turned and saw them on their knees in the ring, with their pots on their chains still smoking, and their hair going white. From the roots out. Grey. White. As the young hand's had gone behind the screen at the banquet.

The rest ran.

Three of them, at the terrace's edge, nearest the unicorn when it reared, weren't on the terrace any more. He looked over the broken balustrade. The cliff went down to the sea. The sea was white.

---

The unicorn went to its kin.

Off the terrace. Over the broken balustrade. Down the face of what was left of the cliff, the raw new rock where the old cliff had broken away, going down it like a goat, cloven feet on ledges he couldn't see, leaping. Into the bay. Into the white water. Wading. Then swimming. Lean and silver-grey in the white sea, with its horn up, going out toward the thing standing in the smoke.

The smoke was clearing.

With the binders gone, the pots were burning out on their chains on the terrace, and the wind off the sea was taking the sweet cloud away. And the Horned Sun lifted its head.

He saw its light come back.

The rays. Round its head. Pale, grey, then white, then gold. Going out again. Brighter. Its head coming up. Its eyes.

The silver-grey unicorn came out of the water at its knee, onto a ledge of fallen cliff, and stood there. Small. Very small against it. Like a foal at its mother's leg.

The Horned Sun put its great head down.

---

Corvin had gone.

Idony looked for him afterwards, on the terrace. He'd been there at the start. Beside the king, a little behind him, in his plain dark coat, at the edge of the terrace, watching the binding take. Idony had seen him there.

He'd gone when it began to work. When the Horned Sun's head first dipped. Idony remembered it now. A tall still shape going away along the terrace toward the harbour stair, unhurried. As if he'd seen what he'd come to see.

He hadn't stayed to see it break.

---

Idony stood on the terrace and counted.

He always counted.

The Bond-King of Sarovel. On his own stair, on the third step, with a hole in his chest and his crown gone into the sea.

Two binders. Burned grey. Kneeling in the ring with their pots smoking.

Three more. Off the edge.

Six.

He'd known the word. He'd read it at twenty. He'd turned it in his head in the stables and not said it. He'd said it today, into a crack in a collar, in a surge, because a thing the size of a hill was being bound through a prisoner's neck into a king's hand, and he could stop it.

He had stopped it.

Six.

*My knowledge has a body count now,* he thought.

He stood on the broken terrace with his stick in his hand and his back screaming.

---

He didn't sleep that night either.

He sat against the broken balustrade with his glasses. They'd come down off the top of their throats. A little. The surge going over. Turning toward the drain. And out in the bay, in the moonlight, the Horned Sun stood in the sea with its light round its head, white and gold, and the small silver-grey unicorn at its knee, and didn't move.

In the morning he looked east.

Along the bay. Along the white city on its terraces, with its lamps still white, to the low quarter below the walls, brown, and the shingle below it where the unchosen kept their boats. And the boats. Dozens of them. More. Pulled down to the water. People on the shingle in a crowd, loading them. Children being handed in. And above the shingle, the old fishing-stair, cut in the low cliff under the low quarter, going down from the mud-brick houses to the boats.

The cliff above the stair was sliding.

He saw it go. A whole shoulder of the low cliff, cracked in the surge, sliding down toward the shingle, toward the boats, toward the crowd. Rock and earth and the mud-brick houses on top of it.

And in the slide, a lion.

Grey. With his head down. Standing in the falling earth on the stair with his forefeet braced against it, holding it. Holding it off the boats.

And beside him, another. And another.

Three lions. On the old fishing stair, in the morning light, holding a cliff.
