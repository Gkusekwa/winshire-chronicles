---

For three days the white city burned.

Not all at once. A roof. Then another. She watched it from the low quarter's shore, from the shingle by the boats, from the stable door with Orrun's head against her back. The lamps in the white city burning white and bursting, and something catching where they burst. A stable-roof. A noble house's shutters. The bonded lords' gifts blazing in the streets, a cat shedding gold sparks into straw, a hound burning in a doorway. And the fire going from roof to roof down the terraces toward the walls.

The cliff under the palace cracked. Wider every hour, with white light in the crack.

The sea in the bay heaved. Every hour or so. A long slow swell coming in across the bay out of nowhere, with no wind behind it, and running up the low quarter's shingle further than any tide, and pulling back.

Orrun lay along the stable wall with his head in the door and didn't eat.

"It is turning over," he said. On the second night. "Under the stair. My kin. Like the pale one at Vael."

"How long?"

"Not long," said Orrun.

---

It woke on the three-hundred-and-seventeenth day.

She saw it from the shingle. The horn first. White-gold, spiralled, going up out of the falling cliff and the spray, higher than the palace. Then the head, long and narrow, and the light round it. Rays. Going out in every direction, white and gold, like the sun at noon.

The low quarter came out of its doors and stood in the street and looked at it.

Nobody said anything.

Then an old woman beside Maren, a fish-wife with a basket on her hip, said, very quietly, "The Sun on the Stair," and put her basket down on the stones and knelt.

---

The unicorns came down that afternoon.

Out of the hills. Out of the Elderwood. She'd never known there were so many. Hundreds. Grey and silver-black, lean and deer-boned, over the hills behind the city and down through the olive terraces and over the walls. Through the streets. Through the white city, through the burning, past the noble houses with their bond-crests, down the stairs and the steps and the lanes to the water.

Into the bay.

They waded out. Swam. Hundreds of them, in the white water, toward the thing standing in the sea. And stood round it in the shallows, in a great ring, with their horns up.

She saw the old mare among them. Nearly black. Grey at the muzzle. The tip of her horn gone.

She saw a small silver-grey one at the Horned Sun's knee, on a ledge of fallen cliff, apart from the rest. With no collar.

Orrun stood on the shingle beside her and watched them for a long time.

"They have come to it," he said. "Their kin. Awake." His voice was very low. "I came to mine at the Ring and lay at ten paces, and it did not turn its head."

---

Mother Ysa came in the night.

From Sorrel Isle. The little rock off the bay's eastern horn, where the lighthouse was. Where the unchosen were sent to serve the light, the old stable-owner said, or to be forgotten, and it came to the same thing. She rowed in alone across the heaving bay in the dark in a boat with a lantern in the bow, and came up the shingle, and walked into the wine-inn in the low quarter, where Tobin was, and everybody, and stood in the door.

Old. Small. Brown and wrinkled as a walnut. In a fisher's widow's black, with a shawl over her head.

"The cliff's going to come down," she said. "On the stair. On the shingle. On you." She looked round the room. "And the white city's burning down through the gates. And every time that thing in the bay moves, the sea comes up the shingle further."

Nobody said anything.

"The island's rock," said Mother Ysa. "It's high. It doesn't burn. It doesn't slide." She pulled her shawl tighter. "Come over. All of you. In the boats. At first light."

"All of us?" said somebody. "There's thousands."

"There's boats," said Mother Ysa.

---

They loaded at dawn.

On the shingle below the old fishing-stair. The stair the low quarter had cut into the low cliff over the shore, generations ago, from the mud-brick streets at the top down to where the boats were pulled up. A narrow stair. Steep. Worn.

Every boat in the low quarter. Fishing boats. Rowing boats. The flat-bottomed salt-barges from the pans at the bay's end. A dozen of them. Fifty. More. Pulled down into the water and held there while people climbed in.

Tobin ran the shingle.

She'd seen him do it at the Scald, in the ditches, talking. He did it here. Up and down the stones, loud, in his bad Sarovel and his good common tongue, shouting, pointing, picking up children and putting them into boats and telling their mothers to get in after. Everybody listened to him. She didn't know why. They'd listened to him in the wine-inn every night for two weeks, and now they listened to him on the shingle.

"Children in the middle! Old ones in the bow! Don't stand up! Don't stand up in it! Sit! That's it! That's grand!"

She worked beside him with the old stable-owner and his nephew. Getting people down the stair. Getting them into boats. Pushing the boats off.

---

People came down the harbour road from the white city.

She saw them at sunrise. The harbour road ran along the shore under the city walls, from the white city's sea-gate down to the low quarter's shingle. And down it, in the smoke, people. From the burning terraces. Nobles in silk, with their bonded beasts. Merchants. Servants. Running. Pushing. A crowd, coming down the road toward the shingle, toward the boats, in the smoke, with their beasts screaming.

Too many. Too fast.

She saw what would happen. The road came onto the shingle through a gap in the low cliff, narrow, where the stair was. If that crowd came through the gap at a run, with frightened beasts, it would go over the people on the shingle like a flood. The boats. The children. Everything.

She started toward the gap.

She didn't know what she'd do when she got there.

---

The olifants came down the road behind the crowd.

Three of them. Grey. Enormous. Bigger than Orrun had been in the spring. With their tusks gilded at the tips and painted cloths on their backs, scorched now, and on each one's neck, a rider. In the royal livery. Bonded. She could see it in the way they sat, as the knights at Greyhold had sat on their beasts, as if they'd grown there.

The royal olifants. From the court's stables.

They came down the harbour road through the smoke at a walking pace, and the crowd parted in front of them. And at the gap in the cliff, where the road came onto the shingle, they turned.

Across the road.

Side to side. Shoulder to shoulder. Three grey walls of olifant across the gap, with their trunks up and their ears spread wide and their gilded tusks forward. And stood.

The crowd from the white city stopped against them.

"In war," said the old stable-owner, beside her. Very quietly. "That's what they do. In every war. They stand. The army goes behind them." He looked at them. "I've not seen it. My grandfather saw it."

---

The riders let them through.

A few at a time. One of the riders, an old man on the middle olifant with a white beard and a whip he didn't use, called to the crowd in a voice like a bell, and the olifant on the end took one step back, and a gap opened at the cliff's edge, a man's width. And people came through it. Ten. Twenty. Then the olifant stepped forward and the gap closed.

Then again.

Ten. Twenty. A trickle. Onto the shingle. To the boats. Not a flood.

She stood on the shingle and watched three olifants hold a road.

---

The knights wouldn't get in the boats.

A dozen of them, chosen, bonded, from the court. They'd come through the olifants' gap on their bond-steeds, the long-legged coal-eyed beasts that weren't quite horses, and stood on the shingle in their armour, in the smoke, with their beasts plunging and screaming at the sea.

"Not in those," one of them said. Looking at the fishing boats. "Not with him." He had his hand on his steed's neck. "He won't. He'll drown."

The grooms took them.

Unchosen. Stable-boys from the royal stables, in brown, who'd come down the road with the crowd. She saw them come across the shingle. Not to the knights. To the beasts. A boy took one steed's head. Just took it, by the bridle, and talked to it, low, and walked it down the shingle into the water. To one of the flat salt-barges, wallowing in the shallows. And up its side, over the rail, onto its flat deck, with the steed's coal eyes rolling and its hooves slipping on the wet planks.

And the knight went after it.

He had to. The bond pulled. He couldn't stand on the shingle with his beast on a barge. He went down the shingle into the water to his waist in his armour and climbed up over the barge's rail after it.

The grooms led every one. Every steed. Down into the water and onto the barges.

And every knight followed his beast.

---

The Horned Sun moved at mid-morning.

She felt it before she saw it. Through Orrun. Through the stones. A shift. Like a sleeper turning over, but awake. It turned in the bay. Slowly. Its long legs moving under the white water, its horn swinging round against the sky. Toward the east. Toward the mouth of the Gilt, where the river came down into the bay from the hills.

The sea heaved.

Bigger than all the others. A long grey swell coming across the bay from the thing as it turned, lifting the boats, lifting the barges with the steeds on them, running up the shingle in a rush of white, past her knees, past her waist.

And pulling back.

The low cliff above the fishing-stair cracked.

---

She heard it go. Over the sea. A long grinding crack, above her. And the cliff over the stair began to slide.

Not all of it. A shoulder. A whole shoulder of the low cliff, with the top of the stair on it, and the mud-brick houses at the top of the stair, and the street, sliding. Down. Slowly. Earth and stone and brick. Toward the stair. Toward the shingle. Toward the boats, loading below it, full of children.

Orrun went up the stair.

---

She felt him go. Up the narrow stair, into the slide, with the earth coming down round him. She felt him stop. Brace. His forefeet against the sliding ground, his head down, his whole weight leaning into it.

It slowed.

Not enough. She felt it not be enough. One lion against a cliff.

Then the cliff-rock beside him broke open.

And a lion stood up out of it.

Grey. As big as him. The broken-rock mane, the plates. With the stone falling off its shoulders. It put its head down beside him and braced.

And on his other side, the rock broke again.

Another.

---

Three lions on the old fishing-stair, in the dawn, holding a cliff.

She saw them from the shingle. Side by side across the stair, in the slide, with the earth and the brick and the stone coming down round them and stopping against them. Heads down. Forefeet braced. Holding it. Off the stair. Off the shingle. Off the boats.

She felt herself go into them.

Not like at the Scald. She didn't lose the hour this time. She stayed. She felt it. All of her. Everything she was, going up the stair, into the three of them. Into the weight and the slowness and the long patience of stone, three times over. Stretched across three bodies. Holding.

She knelt on the shingle.

She put her hands in the stones. Both of them. Flat. Into the wet shingle below the stair, below the slide, as she'd put them into the bank at the Scald. She had the word. Pell's word. The knights' stand-word, for a frightened horse. *Into it, not at it.*

She said it into the ground.

Nothing.

---

She felt it go out of her mouth and die.

Not dead ground. The ground was alive. The surge was still in it, under her hands, warm and buzzing. Running water? No. It was the shingle. It was stone.

It was her.

There was nothing behind the word. Nothing in her to put into it. Everything she had was up the stair, in three lions, holding a cliff. She was kneeling on the shingle with her hands in the stones and a word in her mouth and nothing behind it at all. Empty. Like a jug.

She said it again.

Nothing.

She stayed on her knees with her hands in the stones and let the lions hold it.

---

She saw the ship through the smoke.

She didn't know how long after. She was still kneeling. The three lions were still on the stair. The boats were still loading below them, and going off, one after another, across the heaving bay toward the rock of Sorrel Isle with its white tower.

The smoke was coming down off the white city. Drifting across the harbour. Thick, grey, in sheets. And through a gap in it, across the bay, under the palace cliff, at its western end, she saw the palace's harbour stair.

Not the Sun Stair. That was gone into the sea. The other one. The private one, that went down from the palace's west wing to a deep-water quay under the cliff, where the court's own ships tied up.

A ship at the quay.

White-hulled. Two masts. Smoke-blackened. Its sails going up.

And men on the quay. Dragging someone.

---

A man in a brown robe.

Bent. Grey at the temples. Fighting them. Not well. He'd never fought anyone in his life. Pulling back against them, with his feet on the stone of the quay, and they had him by the arms, two of them, and a third behind with a hand in the back of his robe.

A stick went into the water off the quay's edge.

Idony.

She was on her feet. She didn't remember getting up.

"Idony!"

Across half a bay. Through the smoke. Over the heaving water and the boats and the noise of the cliff sliding behind her and three lions holding it. He couldn't have heard. Nobody could have heard.

They dragged him up the plank onto the ship.

---

She couldn't reach him.

Half a bay. A crowd. The shingle. And everything she had up the stair behind her, in three lions holding a cliff over fifty boats full of children.

She stood on the shingle and watched.

Lisska saw it too.

She'd been overhead since dawn. On watch. Circling the shingle, small and green against the smoke. Waiting, still, for an answer. She saw. Maren saw her see it. Saw her turn in the air, sharp, and go. Low. Fast. Out over the harbour toward the ship, toward the quay, toward the plank and the men on it.

The crossbows came up on the ship's deck.

She saw them loose. A dozen bolts. Up at the small green shape coming in low over the water. Lisska twisted in the air. Turned. A bolt went past her. Another. She went up, out of range, and came round, and went in again.

They loosed again.

She went up again. And stayed up. Circling. Over the ship. Out of range. Screaming. Maren could hear her, very faint, through the smoke. Screaming at the ship.

The ship cast off.

It went out from under the palace cliff on the morning tide, with its sails filling. Past the end of the bay. Past Sorrel Isle and its white tower. Out onto the open sea. East.

Lisska followed it to the end of the bay.

Then she came back.

---

Mother Ysa rowed the last boat.

When the slide was held. When the three lions on the stair had stood long enough, and the earth had stopped coming down, and settled against them, and stayed. Twelve children in it, and two old women. She rowed it off the shingle herself, because there was nobody else left to row, and she said she'd rowed across that bay every day for thirty years and she'd row it once more.

The Horned Sun moved again as she reached the island.

Another swell. Not so big. Big enough. It took the boat on the rocks below Sorrel Isle's landing and turned it over.

The island's people pulled the children out. On ropes, from the rocks. All twelve. The two old women.

Not Mother Ysa.

They found her the next morning, on the island's shingle, below the lighthouse. With her shawl still over her head.

---

The heir came down the harbour road at noon.

She saw them come. Through the olifants' gap, the last of the crowd from the white city. A dozen people. Unchosen. Palace grooms, in brown. Kitchen-women. Two hands in brown robes. An old blind man with milky eyes, led by a boy.

Carrying someone.

A boy. Nine years old, perhaps. In a white shirt with blood on it, not his own. Carried in a groom's arms, with his face turned into the groom's shoulder.

"The prince," said the old stable-owner. He'd gone very still. "Seraphin. The king's son."

They'd carried him out of the burning palace. The chosen hadn't. The court hadn't. The grooms and the kitchen-women and the hands had gone up through the fire and brought him down. Through the smoke, down the white city's terraces, along the harbour road, through the olifants.

They put him in a boat for Sorrel Isle.

---

The street-tribunes came down to the shingle.

The low quarter's own. She'd heard of them from Tobin. The elected heads of the mud-brick streets, one for every street, who settled quarrels and kept the wells and decided who got the water first in a drought. Unchosen. Old men and old women, mostly. Fish-wives and stable-hands and a cooper.

They stood on the shingle round the boat with the boy in it.

"Somebody's got to hold the city," said one. A fish-wife. The one who'd knelt that morning. "Till he's grown. The chosen won't. They're on the barges with their horses."

"In his name," said another.

"In his name," said the fish-wife. She looked at the boy. "Somebody's got to."

Tobin was with them.

She saw him. Standing among the street-tribunes on the shingle, loud, thin, all bones and eyes, talking. They listened to him. They'd listened to him for two weeks. They were listening now.

---

The Horned Sun went up the Gilt in the afternoon.

Out of the bay. Slowly. At the river's mouth. Wading up out of the sea into the river, with the water pouring off its flanks, and up the river's valley, north. Toward the hills. Toward the Elderwood. With the unicorns round it, hundreds, grey and silver-black, going with it up the valley in a great slow river of their own.

And at its knee, small, silver-grey, the one with no collar.

The Grace went with it.

She felt it go. Through the shingle. Through Orrun on the stair. The ground going from flood to full to thin to nothing, in an hour, as the Horned Sun went up the valley out of sight. Like water going out of a basin when someone pulls the plug.

Every white lamp in Solenne went out.

All of them. The ones still burning in the white city, on the terraces, in the noble houses that weren't on fire. Out. Glass. And the blazing gifts in the streets stopped. The gold sparks off a lord's cat went dark. A hound stopped burning.

---

The olifants' riders felt it.

She saw it. The old man with the white beard on the middle olifant put his hand on its neck, and his face changed. Something going out of it. Not grief. Puzzlement. Like a man who's lost his place in a book.

The bond hadn't broken. The olifant was still under him, still his. But dull. The gift gone out of it. Whatever the Grace had given them, the riders and the beasts, that made them more than a man on an olifant: gone.

They were ordinary people. On frightened beasts.

All over Solenne. All the chosen. Ordinary people, on frightened beasts, on burning terraces, in the smoke.

The olifants stood in the road anyway.

They didn't move till the last of the crowd was through.

---

The shadows went back into the cliff.

She felt them go. On the stair. The two that had stood up out of the rock, sinking back into it, slowly, as the water goes into sand. And everything they'd been holding coming back into her. Into him.

Orrun came down the stair on three legs.

His left foreleg held up. He came down the narrow worn steps slowly, with the slide settled against the cliff above him and the mud-brick houses at the top of the stair leaning out over nothing, and lay down on the shingle at the bottom, and put his chin on the stones.

"I am lame," he said.

She knelt by him.

"I made two of me again," he said. "And me. Three of us." He shut his eyes. "It cost again. The leg." He was quiet. "You said the word."

"It didn't work."

"No," said Orrun. "You had nothing. I had it all."

---

They went over to Sorrel Isle at dusk.

The last boat but one. Orrun swam, on three legs, slowly, with his lame foreleg held up out of the water, and she sat in the stern of the old stable-owner's nephew's boat and watched him and didn't breathe the whole way.

The island was rock. High. With a white tower on the top of it and a light in the tower that didn't burn now, because the Grace had gone. Somebody had lit an oil lamp in it instead. Small. Yellow.

The low quarter was on the rock. Thousands. Sitting on the stones in the dusk, in families, round fires made with flint. Children asleep. The boy in the bloodied shirt asleep in a fish-wife's lap.

Tobin wasn't there.

He'd stayed. With the street-tribunes. On the shingle. In the low quarter, under the walls of the burning white city, holding what was left.

"Somebody's got to," he'd said. When she'd tried to make him get in a boat. "You said. *I will stand between the weak and what would harm them.*" He'd grinned at her. Thin, and tired, and filthy. "I've said it now. In a dead town. I can't take it back."

---

She took the letter out when the boats were off.

On the rock of Sorrel Isle, below the lighthouse, in the dusk, by a fire made with flint. With Orrun lying on the stones beside her with his lame leg stretched out and Lisska on the rock above them, thin and silent, looking east at the open sea where the ship had gone.

The page she wouldn't read in the Elderwood.

She unfolded it. She read it.

All of it. This time. Slowly. His hand, better, the lines level. The vinegar cloths. The shed on the black water. The black and the red with their collars off, and their eyes at the edge. The bronze, and the knife.

*The man who pays them is at Solenne with you. He is selling our young ones to the court. He has a buyer there.*

*Be careful of him. I know you will be. I am writing it anyway.*

She looked east. At the dark sea.

*LISS SAYS COME HOME.*

*LISS.*

She read it twice.
