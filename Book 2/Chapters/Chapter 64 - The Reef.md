---

Ada came down to the coast in a basket.

She'd said she would. At Bel's table, on the three-hundred-and-seventeenth night, after the dragons had turned their heads in the caves and hummed till dawn. She'd said it with her arms folded.

"If you're writing to the Tide Council," she said, "you'll want me."

"It's a long way."

"I've never seen the sea," said Ada.

He looked at her.

"Sixty-one years," said Ada. "On the river. And never once the sea." She unfolded her arms and put her hands flat on the table. "Get me a basket."

---

Gunna flew her down on Tamsin.

The steadiest flier on the ledges. Three hundred and twelve years old and never once dropped anything, Gunna said. They put one of the big wicker baskets on Tamsin's back in the war-harness, and Ada climbed into it on the setters' ledge with her shawl and her bag of pens and her mouth shut tight, and sat down in the bottom of it, and held onto the sides.

Not a word, the whole way. A day's flying, south, over the moor and the Fellwood and the Marches and the Ashlings. He flew beside Tamsin on Kharrak and looked across at the basket every few minutes and saw his mother sitting in the bottom of it with her knuckles white on the wicker and her eyes shut.

She was furious. He could tell. She'd never say so.

Then they came over the last of the Ashlings and the sea was there.

---

Ada opened her eyes.

He saw her do it. She'd felt the air change, perhaps. The smell. She opened her eyes and looked over the side of the basket, and there it was. All of it. Grey and silver and going on forever, with the light on it, and the long curve of Gullhaven's spit going out into it like an arm.

They came down on the spit. Gunna got her out of the basket. She stood on the shingle with her shawl round her and her bag in her hand and looked at the sea.

She didn't say anything for an hour.

Then she said: "Well." And sat down on an upturned boat and asked somebody for tea.

---

The spit was busier than when he'd left.

Arn's twelve pairs had become twenty. The baskets forty. Every day, from first light, dragons going up off the spit with baskets on their backs and out over the harbour and back. Sacks. Barrels. Salt-boys, who'd stopped screaming and started queuing. A salt-wife, once, who'd climbed into a basket on a dare and come back white and said never again and gone again the next day.

The town still thought the Clan was mad.

But the town had started standing on its roofs to watch.

---

The salt-wives poured the brine at dawn.

He saw it on the first morning. He'd gone out to the end of the spit before light to look at the reef. And they came past him in a line. Twenty of them, thirty, in their aprons, with their arms white to the elbow from the pans. Each one carrying a cup. Clay, or tin, or wood. Full.

They went down to the water's edge at the very end of the spit, where the shingle ran out into the sea, and stood in a row.

The eldest stood in the middle. Very old. Bent nearly double. With a tin cup in both hands.

"*Lady,*" she said. In a cracked high voice, out over the grey water. "*We give you back your salt. Keep the stones.*"

And poured her cup into the sea.

And all along the line, they poured. One cup each. Into the grey water, at the end of the spit, as the light came up.

Then they turned round and went back to the pans.

---

"Every morning," said the white-armed one.

She'd stopped by him on the way back. The one who'd asked him what the baskets were for, on the first day. Hessa Pane, her name was. The eldest's daughter.

"Every morning since there's been a Gullhaven," she said. "Brine from the pans. One cup. Back to the Lady. So she'll keep the spit."

"Does she?"

Hessa Pane looked at him as if he were simple.

"Look at it," she said. She waved her white arm at the spit. A league of shingle, going out into the sea. "Every storm since my grandmother's grandmother. The big one, forty years back, that took the harbour wall at Corsa. Took ships off the Saltings. Took the whole of Little Brine down the coast." She looked at the spit. "Didn't take one stone off this. Not one." She folded her arms. "She keeps the stones. She always has."

"The Drowned Lady."

"She's under the water," said Hessa Pane. "Everyone knows that."

---

They wouldn't leave the pans.

He'd known they wouldn't. He tried anyway. He stood in the square at the root of the spit, where the harbour-master's door still had the Tide Council's notice nailed to it, and told them. The reef. The bell. The breath he'd felt under the rock, waking. What had happened at Vael, at the lake, when the thing under it woke. A wall of water down a valley.

They listened. Politely. Because of the dragons.

Then the eldest salt-wife, the bent one with the tin cup, said, "The Lady keeps the spit."

"She's kept it since before the Council," said Hessa Pane. "She'll keep it after."

And they went back to the pans.

---

"Then we'll mark the roofs," said Arn.

At dusk, on the shingle, with the dragons coming in for the night. He'd listened to it all from the edge of the square with his chin on his fist.

"They won't leave," said Arn. "Fine. They won't leave. Then when it comes they'll go up. Onto their roofs. And we'll come for them." He looked along the spit. At the houses. Stone, some. Timber, most. Thatch, some. "But not every roof'll take a dragon. A dragon lands on a thatched cottage, it goes through to the cellar." He looked at Calder. "So we find the ones that will. And we mark them. And we tell every house which roof is theirs."

---

The riders marked them.

All the next day and the day after. Along the spit. Every roof. Arn's riders and Ulric's, walking the ridges, testing. Stamping on the slates. Tapping the beams. Getting a small dragon to land on it, Leif's brown, the lightest on the ledges, and seeing if it held.

If it held, a white cross. Lime. On the ridge. Big enough to see from the air.

The smoke-houses were best. Stone-walled, with flat roofs of slab for the smoke to come through, built to take a weight of fish. The net-sheds were good. The chapel by the harbour-master's was good. The salt-store at the root of the spit, which had a roof like a fortress.

Four hundred and twelve roofs.

He walked the spit at dusk on the three-hundred-and-twentieth day and looked at them. White crosses on the ridges, all the way out along the shingle to the end, in the last light.

People had come out of their houses to look at the crosses on their roofs.

Some of them had started asking which was theirs.

---

He wrote to the Tide Council on the three-hundred-and-twentieth night.

At a table in the Anchor-and-Net. Gullhaven's inn. Low, dark, with fish-nets on the ceiling and the worst wine in Winshire in a jug in front of him that Ada had tasted once and pushed away. Ada across the table. Pens. The Clan's grey paper.

He wrote it himself.

What Aurveth had felt. On the reef. The breath, under the rock, under the whole coast. Going in. Held. Going out. Getting shorter. Waking. The bell. The reader's mark on his chart. The Strand house's fleet wouldn't hold it. Please tell Gullhaven to go.

He pushed it across the table.

Ada read it.

She read it. She put it down.

"They'll say a farmer had a feeling on a rock," she said.

---

"It's true."

"I know it's true." Ada folded her arms. "They won't care. They'll read it in Corsa at their long black table and say, *a farmer had a feeling on a rock, and his dragon told him so,* and they'll laugh, and put it in the fire." She looked at him. "You've got to write it so they can't pretend not to understand."

"How?"

"Things they can't wave away," said Ada. "Things with names on. Numbers. Things that happened where people saw." She pushed the paper back. "Again."

---

He wrote it again.

It took him all of the next day. She stopped him every few lines.

He wrote the reader's numbers. From the frost at Glasswater, and from Maren's letters. The Ring: the great one took what was under it, and seven days after, the thing under it woke. Vael: five days. The Scald: five days. The Sun Stair: *within the month*, the reader had said, from Kessmoor, a month before. And it had been.

He wrote Vael. The lake. The lower city drowned. The meltflood down the Wend that took the Eelweir and nearly took Greyhold's ferry with forty people on it. He'd been there. He wrote that he'd been there.

He wrote the Scald. With names. Master Engineer Agathe Pike. Inspector Harl Moss of the Assembly's Office of the Cordon. A cage a league across, of iron and three canals of cold water and four engines, forty years in the building, burning the hearts of six hundred beasts a day. On dry land.

"Now this," said Ada.

He wrote what the Council's own notice said. He copied it out. *As Kessmoor has taken and holds the thing beneath the Scald.*

He wrote: *Kessmoor's cage is on land. The Strand house cannot build a league of iron and canals on a reef in the sea. Ask Master Pike. Ask Inspector Moss. They will tell you.*

---

"Better," said Ada. "Now the last bit."

"What last bit?"

"The bit they'll lie awake over." She tapped the table. "What they'll be asked. After."

He looked at her.

"Four thousand people on a spit," said Ada. "In the Council's own League. With the Council's own notice nailed to the harbour-master's door. And something comes up out of the sea, and they drown." She looked at him across the bad wine. "And somebody stands up at the long black table in Corsa. Afterwards. And says: *Who knew?*"

He wrote it.

*When it comes, and the spit drowns, somebody will ask the Council: who knew? This letter is so the answer is: you did.*

"Plainer than that," said Ada.

He crossed it out. He wrote it plainer.

*You know now.*

---

The third one he signed.

CALDER FENN. At the bottom. The D the right way round. He didn't even have to think about it now.

Ada read it all. Slowly. With her finger under the lines. Then she nodded, once.

"They can't pretend not to understand that," she said.

"They'll still say no."

"Very likely," said Ada. "But they'll have to say it knowing." She stood up. "That's the most you can do with the Tide Council. Make them say no knowing."

A Clan rider took it to Corsa on the three-hundred-and-twenty-second day. On one of Ulric's greys. To the Tidehall's sea-steps, with orders to wait for an answer.

---

Lisska had been gone three weeks.

Since the three-hundredth day. Since the shingle at Blackwater, at dawn, with his letter on her leg and Liss's two words at the bottom, turning west.

He felt for her every night.

The bond. The thread. He could feel it was there. That was all. It was there, going out of his chest, from the green mark, the smallest of the three, west and south, out along the coast. A long way. Further than he'd ever felt it go. So far that he couldn't feel her at the end of it. Only that the thread was still there. Still whole. Going out into the dark and not breaking.

An empty place. Where she should be. With a thread going through it to somewhere he couldn't reach.

She wasn't dead. He'd know. He'd have felt it go, as Aurveth had felt Ennis go, and Ragnald, and Sigrun.

She was just very far away. And she hadn't come back.

She was waiting for an answer, he told himself. She always waited.

---

Aurveth woke on the three-hundred-and-twenty-third night.

He was asleep against her side on the shingle at the end of the spit, where she'd lain since the reef, with her wound packed and Ivar Leck's orders on her not to fly. He felt her wake. Felt her lift her head.

He woke too.

She was looking south-west. Along the coast. Toward the far end of everything, where it went away west past the Wend's mouth into a dark he'd never flown.

She was very still.

"What is it?"

She didn't answer.

"Aurveth. What did you feel?"

She didn't answer. She lay on the shingle with her great old head lifted and turned south-west, looking along the dark coast, and he felt something come off her through the bond. Not words. Not a picture. A weight. Like something heavy put down somewhere very far off.

"Is it Lisska?"

*She's alive,* said Aurveth. At last. Very low.

"What, then?"

*She's turned,* said Aurveth. *A long way off. She's coming.* She didn't put her head down. *I can feel her feeling it. At the end of the thread. Very far.* A long pause. *I won't say what. I don't know what. She saw something bad, a long way off, and she's coming now.*

She didn't say anything else.

In the morning the salt-wives came past them in a line to the end of the spit with their cups, and poured the brine back into the sea for the Lady.
