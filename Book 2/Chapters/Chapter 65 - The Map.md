---

He'd written it at the stage-house above the Gilt.

The night before the household's man came. She'd forgotten. She found it on the rock of Sorrel Isle, by the flint fire, in the dark, looking for the letters in her coat. Folded small. Behind the black book. On the back of one of the paper forms Moss had given them at the Scald, *the Office of the Cordon*, printed across the top.

She unfolded it.

His hand. Small. Neat. In columns. Down one side, the hooked script, a word at a time, square and spare, with the hooks at the ends of the strokes. Down the other, the common tongue.

*Sleep. Breath. Door. Keep. Hold. Road. End. Bridge. First. Chain. Beyond. Below.*

And on.

Sixty words. More. And at the bottom, the shapes of the little hooks that turned a word into *of*, or *under*, or *toward*, with a note beside them she remembered him saying aloud, at the stage-house kitchen table, while he wrote. *These are the ones that matter. Everything else is nouns.*

At the very top, over the columns, in the same small hand:

*The words you'll need. In case.*

---

In case.

She sat on the rock with it in her hands.

He'd known. At the stage-house above the Gilt, the night before the household's man came asking for the Kessmoor measurer. He'd known he might not come back down from the palace, and he'd sat at the long table in the servants' kitchen with a pencil and written her a list of words.

*Sleep. Breath. Door.*

She folded it again very carefully and held it.

---

The temple was in the sea.

She knew that. She'd felt it go. From the shingle, on the morning of the waking, through Orrun: the cliff below the palace breaking away and leaning out and going into the bay, with the shrine and the stair and the temple under it, the plinth and the dome and the six and the ring and the long band of carving below the six that Corvin had looked at and couldn't read. All of it. Into the water.

She hadn't looked at the band long. In the temple. She'd looked at the egg.

She wanted to look at it now.

---

The old stable-owner's nephew rowed her on the three-hundred-and-nineteenth day.

At low water. From Sorrel Isle across the bay to the foot of the cliff under the ruined palace. The new cliff, raw, yellow-grey, where the old one had broken away. And at its foot, in the shallows, the old one.

Slabs. Great slabs of it. Lying in the water, tumbled, heaped, the size of houses and the size of carts. The white limestone of the stair, smashed. The gilded portico of the shrine, a column standing up out of the water at a slant. Marble. Rubble.

And grey. Old stone. Dressed so close you couldn't see the joins. In pieces, among the white.

Orrun swam beside the boat. On three legs. With his lame foreleg held up out of the water, as he'd held it all the way across to the island. He wouldn't stay on the rock. She'd told him to. He'd come anyway.

"It is here," he said. When they came in under the cliff. "The old stone. My kin's house. In pieces." He put his head down to the water. "It still knows me."

---

They found it in the afternoon.

Face-up. In a hand's depth of water, in the lee of a fallen column. A slab of grey stone as long as a cart and half as wide. Carved. She saw the hooks before she saw anything else. And lines. Long lines, cut deep, going across the slab and curving. And small shapes along the lines. And at one end, a round.

She knelt in the water beside it.

"Can you lift it?"

Orrun looked at it.

He was lame. He'd held a cliff for an hour on the fishing-stair with two shadows, and it had cost him the leg, and he hadn't shaped stone since. She didn't know if he could.

He put his head down. Into the water. His forehead against the edge of the slab.

The slab rose.

Not lifted. Rose. The water going off it in sheets, the grey stone coming up out of the shallows on its edge, slowly, as if the sea floor under it were pushing it up. Up. Until it stood on its end against the raw new cliff, upright, with the water running down the carving and the light on it.

Orrun lay down in the shallows beside it with his chin on a rock and shut his eyes.

"It knew me," he said. "Dead ground. It still knew me."

---

She read it by a lamp.

That day the light went, and the tide came, and they rowed back. The next, at low water again, in the evening, with an oil lamp the nephew had brought, a fisherman's lamp, brass, hung on a pole stuck in the rubble. And Idony's list on her knee, in the boat, wrapped in oilcloth against the wet.

She'd read the hooked script a little all her life. Her father's milestone, when she was small. Hale, in the archive. The verse under the arch at the Old Ruin, that she'd copied into a notebook in the rain. The lock's sentence, that she'd copied in charcoal into the back of the black book on the third day after the gate.

A little. Enough to know a hook when she saw one.

With the list it was more.

---

It was the frost-map.

She saw that first. Before she'd read a word. The round at one end of the slab. A river cut through it, wavy, splitting round it and joining below. An island.

And six lines going out from the round.

Six roads. At the angles she'd seen on the frost at Glasswater, in the first grey light, walking them with her eyes down. The farmer had drawn them with a knife on a stick. Somebody had cut them in the stone of a temple wall, a long time before there were farmers.

At the end of each line, a shape.

She knew them. A stag with a forest. A coil. A long low burrower with its snout down. A beast with one horn and rays. A hump like an island, with a fin.

And a lion. With wings. At the end of the shortest line. Close to the round. Down the river.

---

Under five of them, the same sign.

An egg. A small carved oval. And a word under it. She found it on the list. Near the top. Idony had put it first.

*Sleep.*

Under the stag, the coil, the burrower, the horn, the island. Five times. *Sleep.*

Under the lion, no egg.

Something else. A line of hooked marks, short. She went down the list with her finger in the lamplight. She found it.

*Chains.*

And before it another word, with a hook on its end that she found at the bottom of the list, among the ones that mattered. The hook that meant *of*, or *the*, or something between. And the word itself, a little way down the column.

*First.*

*The first chains.*

---

She knelt in the boat and looked at it.

The first chains. The lion with wings. Down the river from the round. Three and a half leagues down the river from the bridge at the Old Ruin, the reader had said on the frost, to the river hill. Where Greyhold was. Where the cavern was, under the school, with six iron rings in its walls and the stumps of chain on them and the black river standing half-way up.

Where the Last Titan had lain three hundred years.

The first chains. Somebody had cut that in a temple wall in Sarovel. Before Sarovel. Before the chains were forged.

*Before.*

She didn't know how. She didn't know what it meant, for something to be carved before it happened. She put it away. She went on.

---

Past the lion, there was more.

The line from the round to the lion went on. Past it. Not along the river. Down. She saw it now in the lamplight: the carving didn't stop at the lion's shape. A short cut went on beyond it, deeper, under the round. Under the island. Under the bridge.

And at the end of it, another sign.

A ring. Round. With six small hollows cut in it. Evenly spaced.

The ring. The one the six hands held up in the domes under the Scald and under the stair. The one on the door under Greyhold, and in the rim of every coin, and on the collars of the young dragons in the Fells. The real one. The one this was a picture of.

Beside it, words. A line. She went down Idony's list with her finger, word by word, in the yellow lamplight, with the sea lapping at the side of the boat.

*Beyond.*

*The first chains.*

*The one.*

*Is kept.*

She read it twice.

*Beyond the first chains, the one is kept.*

---

And one word more, small, cut inside the ring itself, in the middle, where the six hollows went round.

She knew it before she found it on the list. She'd seen it carved round a dome under the Ring, in the summer, by Hesper's dying torch, and Idony had read it to her.

*Breath.*

*Here the breath is kept.*

---

She sat in the boat for a long time.

The tide was turning. The nephew said so, quietly, from the oars. The water was coming back in round the slab, round the fallen column, round Orrun lying in the shallows.

She didn't move.

The held breath.

Idony had found it on the morning after the gate. On his glass, in Vael, before he'd ever met her. He'd told her about it on the road south from the Ring, walking: the hole under Hearthmere, and a line of dips going north-east at the speed of a bird, the walker, and under the river hill, at Greyhold, something else. *Like a breath held.* A dead-still place, where the glass didn't go up and didn't go down.

On the frost at Glasswater he'd found it again. On the College's great chart. The one place where the tide didn't move. Under the river hill. Where all the lines went in.

Nobody knew what it was.

It was the ring.

---

Not a sleeper. Not another beast. The ring. The thing the six hands held up. The thing that would make the six one. *One as in one thing. One will.* Kept under the bridge, under the river, beyond the first chains.

Below the cavern. Below where the Titan had lain in its chains for three hundred years. Down. Deeper. In the dark under Greyhold's hill.

A vault.

And Orrun had said it, on the second night after the river, in the cavern, by the lock, with the black river standing half-way up the walls. She'd copied the lock's sentence into the back of the black book by the light of the burning words and he'd stood beside her and said:

*Something is shut there. Like a breath held.*

She'd written it down. She hadn't understood it.

And the bird. At the Library. With its feathers rising and not settling, looking at the lock's sentence in her charcoal. *The lock was made for two hands. So was the other thing.*

*Together.*

---

"Corvin knows," she said.

To Orrun. To the dark. The lamp was guttering.

"The bird said nobody knew where the ring went. After the lord broke the egg. It was on the plinth with the egg, and then it was gone." She looked at the carving. "Idony didn't believe it. He said the bird knew more than it said."

Orrun opened his eyes.

"Whoever had the chains made knew where the ring was put," said Maren. "The lord. And the College. Who sold him the chains." She put her hand on her coat, over the black book. "The roll. The contract. The one in the case. Between the College and the lord. Whatever they agreed. Where to put the ring."

"He could not read it," said Orrun.

"He's got Idony now."

---

The ship had gone east.

Past Sorrel Isle. Out onto the open sea. East along the coast, toward everything she knew. Toward the river. Toward home.

Idony could read the roll. All of it. Idony had the chain-words in his head, that the bird had told him to keep there unwritten. *If they're only in my head, the only way to get them is to get me.*

They'd got him.

*Whoever can speak to the chain-ends has a hand on it.* And whoever had the ring...

She didn't finish the thought. She didn't need to.

---

She decided on the rock.

The next night. The three-hundred-and-twenty-first. On Sorrel Isle, under the lighthouse, with its small yellow oil-lamp in the top of the white tower where the Grace-light had been. The low quarter's people asleep on the stones round their fires. The heir asleep in the fish-wife's lap, as he'd slept every night since. Orrun lying along the rock beside her with his lame leg stretched out.

"It's going to the Bell," she said. "Next. The reef. Calder's coast."

"Yes."

"Calder's there. With the Clan. With baskets." She looked east. "We'd be a week behind it. More. On foot. With your leg."

"Yes."

"And Corvin's going to the door."

Orrun didn't say anything.

---

"We're going home," said Maren.

He lifted his head.

"To Greyhold. To the cavern. To the river." She looked at him. "To the door. Below the chains. Where it's kept."

"To keep it," said Orrun.

"To keep it."

He looked at her with his gold eyes in the firelight.

"I kept a door once," he said. "On a bridge. In the sun. With people walking over it. And men came with fire on poles. A hundred of them. And pitch." He was very quiet. "I could not hold it. I had fire on my side and I could not hold it. I ran." He put his chin back on the rock. "And they took the witness out of the bronze. And broke the egg. And put chains on my kin. And I lay in the dark three hundred years and forgot I was anything."

"I know," said Maren. "The bird told us."

"I failed it," said Orrun. "The door."

"Then we'll keep it this time."

"We will be too late again, perhaps."

"Then we'll be too late at the door," said Maren. "Not somewhere else."

---

He didn't say anything else.

After a while he put his head down on her knee. Not his chin. His whole head. Heavy. Warm. The broken-rock mane against her leg. He'd never done it before.

She put her hand on it.

*I will keep the watch,* she thought.

She didn't say it. She didn't have to.

---

She told Tobin on the three-hundred-and-twenty-second day.

In the low quarter. The nephew rowed her over in the morning. The low quarter was half empty and half full, with the low cliff slid down against the fishing-stair and three grey shapes in the rubble where the lions had stood, pressed into the earth, the shape of them, as if something had lain there.

She walked up through it to find him.

People sat in their doorways under the lions painted on the doors, with nothing in their hands. There was nothing to work at. The boats were on Sorrel Isle or broken on the shingle, and the white city's kitchens, where half the quarter had carried water and scrubbed pots, were black shells on the terraces above.

The fountain at the fish-market was dry. It had run every day she'd been in Solenne, a jet out of a bronze fish's mouth, sweet and cold and higher than a man. Now there was a skin of green in the basin, and two children kneeling at the rim, scooping it up in their hands.

Behind the wine-inn a groom was sharing out a sack of barley into hats. A line of women held the hats out. He counted under his breath, and she counted with him. Twenty-two. When he got to the end of the line the sack was flat, and the last three hats had a handful each. He went back along the line and took a little out of the first hats and put it in the last ones. Nobody said anything.

On the inn step a boy of six sat with a heel of bread held in both hands against his chest, uneaten, and watched the line.

The street-tribunes were in the wine-inn. Tobin was with them.

He came out when he saw her.

"You're going," he said.

"Home."

He nodded. He didn't argue. He looked at her for a long moment, thin and filthy and tired, with the gold lion on the wine-inn's door behind him.

"Keep the watch," he said.

"You too."

"I am," said Tobin. He jerked his head at the inn. "In there. Lot of old fish-wives wanting to know where the water's coming from." He grinned. "Harder than a cliff."

---

She wrote her answer on the rock at noon.

On the back of Calder's page. The Blackwater one. Under his lines, his vinegar cloths and his shed and the bronze and the knife and *Be careful of him*. Under *LISS SAYS COME HOME*. On the other side. There was no other paper.

In her square hand. Big. Plain. So he could read it.

*Corvin's men took Idony onto a ship at Solenne. Going east. He knows the words.*

*The ring is under Greyhold. Below the chains. That is the held breath. It is carved in the temple. Corvin knows where.*

She stopped. She looked at it. She thought of him on his coast, with the Clan, waiting for something none of them had seen yet.

She wrote the last line.

*Going home. Keep your coast.*

---

Lisska was on the lighthouse rail.

Where she'd been since the shingle. Since the ship. Thin, and silent, and looking east at the open sea where it had gone. Sixteen days she'd waited for an answer. Since the Elderwood. Since dawn in the clearing with a letter on her leg.

She looked round when Maren came up the lighthouse stair.

Maren held out the letter.

Lisska held out her foreleg.

Maren tied it on herself. In the oilcloth. With the string Tobin had used before, that she'd kept. She tied it carefully, the knot tight, and put her hand flat on the small green dragon's neck for a moment.

She couldn't hear her. She never could. But Orrun could, from the rock below. And Maren could hear Orrun.

*She says,* said Orrun, *is it a good answer?*

Maren thought about it.

"Tell her it's the only one I've got."

*She says that will do.*

Lisska went off the rail at dusk on the three-hundred-and-twenty-second day.

East. Out over the sea. Along the coast. Toward the reef, and the spit, and the farmer.

Maren watched her go until she couldn't see her. In the morning she went down the lighthouse stair, and down to the boats, and over to the shore, with the lion swimming beside her on three legs, and they went up through the burned white city and out by the landward gate onto the Sun Road, north-east, toward home.
