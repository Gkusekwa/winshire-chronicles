---

The lions were still on the gates.

All the way up the Sun Road. North-east, out of Sarovel, back the way she'd come. Every stage-house. Painted on the gates, in gold where the stage-keepers had gold, in yellow ochre where they hadn't. A lion with a mane like rocks, lying down, with its head up.

The Grace was gone.

She felt it go on the first day out of Solenne. Not the slow thinning she'd felt coming in. Gone. The vineyards grey, the olives grey, the white walls grey in a grey light. Every stage-chapel's gilded sun dull over its door. At dusk nobody sang. The stage-keepers stood in their doorways at the hour when they'd have sung, and didn't, and went back in.

They still knelt when Orrun came through the gate.

She still went in at the servants' gate. It was open for her at every stage now. Grooms waiting at it. Unchosen. Who'd heard.

---

The vineyards had beasts in them.

She saw the first on the second day. A big brindled hound in a studded leather collar, alone between the rows on the slope above the road, nosing at something in the black stumps. It lifted its head at Orrun and ran. By the fourth day she'd seen nineteen. Hounds in twos and threes. A cat on a stage-house wall. A bond-steed standing in a terrace with its reins caught on the vine-stakes, too frightened to pull free, and nobody going up to it.

At a stage-house in the hills the keeper's wife showed her the hen-house. Feathers. The door torn off its leather hinges.

"Cats," she said. "The lords' cats. They come down out of the vines at night." She had a hay-fork by the kitchen door, and she didn't put it away while Orrun was in the yard.

And people. Families with handcarts, going up the road toward the hill towns. Unchosen, most of them, by their brown. Thin. At the foot of an olive terrace a woman and two children went along the rows on their knees in the wet grass, picking windfalls out of it one at a time into a shawl. The olives were black and shrivelled. The woman ate one as she picked, and spat the stone, and went on.

A cat watched them from the wall at the top of the terrace. Tawny. Its silk cloth gone. Thin too.

Orrun looked at it, and it went.

---

Orrun's leg mended on the road.

Slowly. He walked on three legs for five days. Then on four, with the left foreleg barely touching. Then on four. By the time they crossed the border into Hearthmere's dead downs, on the three-hundred-and-thirty-first day, he was walking sound. He didn't say anything about it. She saw him put the foot down on the first morning of the downs, flat, and stand on it, and look at it, and go on.

Hearthmere was the same.

Dead. Grey. She'd known. She'd crossed it in the winter the other way. Downs the colour of ash. Villages with their doors open. A crossroads shrine with its bowl empty and its three-faced figure grey with dust.

Then the King's Road. Then Hallowmere.

---

The capital was dead ground.

She'd never been. She'd heard of it all her life. The royal city on the north shore of the Mere, in terraces, every street looking down on the water. The lamps that burned without oil in the better streets. The long hall of chapter-houses where the knightly orders sat with the crown.

She came up the King's Road into it on the three-hundred-and-thirty-fourth day and it was dead.

The lamps were glass. On their iron posts, in rows, up every terrace. Black. Hundreds of them. She'd seen the lamps at Vael go out, one after another down the hill, in the drain after the Coil. These had been out for a year. There was dust on them.

The wells had gone sour.

A woman told her, at the first well inside the gate, where people were queuing with buckets anyway. The wells of Hallowmere had been charmed sweet for two hundred years, by the licensed keeping-wives, a word said over every well every spring. The word was only a word now. The water came up brown and tasted of iron and made the children sick.

"We boil it," said the woman. "When there's wood."

---

There was a bread riot at the corn exchange.

She heard it before she saw it. Shouting. A crash. She came round a corner into a square and it was full. People. Thin. Hundreds. Pressing toward a long grey building with columns along its front and its doors shut and barred, and men on the steps in crown blue with halberds, and stones going up at the windows.

"No grain," said a man beside her. A thin man with a licence pinned to his coat. A square of parchment with the crown's seal on it, and writing. *Licensed to tend the lamps of the Fourth Terrace.* "No grain since the new moon. The Clan's grain goes to the Marches. The crown's grain went in the spring. They say there's a ship coming from Corsa." He laughed, without anything in it. "They say."

He held out his hand.

She looked at it. Then at the licence on his coat.

She gave him what she had. A copper. Two. He looked at them in his palm as if he'd forgotten what they were for.

There were others. All through the city. Licensed workers, begging. A keeping-wife with her licence on her shawl, *for the keeping of milk*. A bone-setter's, on the coat of an old man sitting against a wall. Licences to say words that were only words, pinned to coats like the badges of a company that had broken.

---

The paper was on every door.

She saw it first on the gate. Then everywhere. On posts. On doors. On the walls of the corn exchange. On the black glass lamps themselves, pasted round the posts. Cheap grey paper, printed in black, smudged, the same paper over and over.

THE CROWN'S CRIME.

She stopped in front of one on the corn exchange's wall and read it. Slowly. It was printed small.

*For three hundred years the crown's lamps burned stolen light.*

*Under Greyhold, in a cavern beneath the river, the first knights chained a great beast. A god of the old realm. They bound it in iron and sold its light. Every lamp in Hearthmere was fed from its chains.*

*The crown knew. The crown hid it.*

*The oath you said at noon was the chain.*

*Now the light is gone, and the beast is loose, and the crown that stole from it would chain it again.*

*Ask what you kept.*

And at the bottom, printed, small, a ring of six.

---

She read it again.

Half of it was true. More than half. The cavern, the chains, the lamps, the oath. She'd read it in a black book in an archive, and heard it from a bird, and written it on eleven pages in Kessmoor graphite. *Keep the watch below.*

The rest was the Restoration.

*A god of the old realm.* *The crown that stole from it.* *Ask what you kept.* Turned. Twisted, a little. Enough. Made into a bill, for people to read on corners, from the same press that had printed the bills for the war.

Reede's press. On a cart. Saved, a man at the gate had said, out of the scrub at Cotter's Cross when the Clan came down on the companies.

People were reading it aloud. On the corners. On the corn exchange steps. To people who couldn't read. *The oath you said at noon was the chain.*

She thought of the chapel in Orrin's ruins, and three voices saying it by a flint fire, with the floor and the gag taken out.

She went on.

---

The old women were in the chapel.

Off the corn exchange. Down a lane, a small chapel, grey stone, with a slate roof and a door that stood open. She went in because it was quiet.

They were kneeling at the hearthstone.

Every chapel in Hearthmere had one. She'd knelt at Greyhold's as a child, with Liss. A flat grey stone, round, set in the floor in front of the altar, where the hearth-fire had been kept in the old days, before there were lamps. Nobody kept a fire on them now. People knelt at them anyway.

Six old women. In shawls. On their knees round the grey stone, with their hands flat on the floor beside it.

"*Mother,*" one was saying. Very low. "*Keep the fire. Keep it small and keep it in.*"

The others said it after her.

*Keep the fire. Keep it small and keep it in.*

No lamp in the chapel was lit. No lamp in the city would light. The hearthstone was cold. They knelt at it anyway, and asked.

---

She knelt with them.

She didn't know why. She hadn't knelt at a hearthstone since she was fourteen. Since the week her father died, when Liss had taken her to the chapel at Greyhold every evening and they'd knelt there and said it, and it hadn't kept anything.

She knelt beside an old woman with a shawl over her head who looked like Mother Ysa, and put her hand flat on the cold floor by the grey stone, and said it.

*Keep the fire. Keep it small and keep it in.*

The old woman beside her looked at her sideways.

"Who's the mother?" said Maren.

The old woman looked at her as if she'd asked who the sun was.

"The hearth-mother," she said. "The grey one. Under the hearth." She turned back to the stone. "She keeps the fire. She always has."

Maren knelt there till they'd finished.

Then she got up and went out to where Orrun lay in the lane with his chin on the cobbles, and put her hand on his neck, and didn't say anything.

---

Davin found her at the corn exchange.

On the steps. In the evening, after the riot had broken up and the crown's men had gone inside and the stones had stopped. She was standing at the foot of the steps with Orrun lying in the square behind her and the crowd keeping its distance from him. A thin young man came down the steps toward her with a clerk's ink on his cuffs and a cap too big for him.

He stopped.

"You're Liss's girl," he said. "From Greyhold. With the lion."

She looked at him.

"Davin," he said. "From the Kettle. At Millbrook. I used to read the papers on the jetty." He looked at Orrun. "I read the farmer's account here. In the autumn. On these steps. To the whole square."

"I heard."

"I'm clerk to the street tribunes now," said Davin. "We run the bread-lines. What there is. And the wells." He pushed his cap back. "The council lets us because it's got to. There'd be nobody in the bread-lines if we didn't."

---

He read Reede's paper on the steps every evening, he said.

Aloud. To the crowd. Because they'd read it anyway, on the corners, and he'd rather they heard it from somebody who'd tell them after.

"Tell them what?"

"What's true in it," said Davin. "And what isn't." He looked at the paper pasted on the corn exchange's wall behind him. *THE CROWN'S CRIME.* "Most of it's true. That's what makes it work. The chains are true. The lamps. The oath." He shook his head. "It's the end that's not. *A god.* *Ask what you kept.* They want you to ask so you'll come to them for the answer. And their answer's a ring of six." He looked at her. "I tell them that. Every night. Some of them listen."

"Where did they get it?" said Maren. "The true part."

Davin looked at her for a long moment.

"I don't know," he said. "Somebody told them. Somebody who knew."

---

The council heard her on the three-hundred-and-thirty-fourth day.

In the long hall of the crown's chapter-house. At the top of the city, on the last terrace, above the Crown's Square where the farmer had landed in the autumn with his red dragon. Grey stone and dark wood and banners of the orders hanging from the beams, faded. A long table. Old men and old women in the colours of the orders, with chains and badges. And at the head, a thin old man with a heavy gold chain on his chest.

"Lord Thale," said a clerk. "President of the Council."

She told them.

Plainly. Standing at the foot of the long table, with her coat on and the black book in it, and Orrun lying in the square outside the tall windows where they could all see him.

---

The Titan had four.

Four of the five sleeps, in its back, under its plates, closed over like water. The Ring. Vael. The Scald. The Sun Stair. She'd seen two of them go in.

The fifth was the reef off Gullhaven. The Drowned Bell. Within the month.

After that it would come home.

"Home," said Thale.

"Here," said Maren. "To the river. To Greyhold. To the cavern where it was chained." She looked down the table. "And under the cavern. Below the chains. Beyond them. There's a vault. And in the vault, a ring. Stone. With six hollows."

She told them what she'd read on the fallen slab in the shallows under Solenne's broken cliff. *Beyond the first chains, the one is kept.* What the six hands held up in the domes. What the reader had called the held breath.

"It's what the five relics are for," she said. "When it has all five, it goes for the ring. And when it has the ring, they're one. All six. Under it. They do what it wants." She stopped. "And what it wants is to bring it all down."

Nobody at the table said anything.

"And Corvin knows where the ring is," she said. "He has the roll the College wrote for the first lord. He has the one man alive who can read it. He has the words for the chain-ends." She looked at Thale. "He's coming here too."

---

They thanked her.

Thale did. In his thin careful voice, from the head of the table. The council was grateful. The council had wondered for a year what the beast wanted. The council now knew.

"And the council is ready," he said.

She waited.

"The crown has bought a cage," said Thale. "As you'll know. From Kessmoor. You were at the Scald, I'm told, when theirs was closed." He folded his thin hands on the table. "Ours is being set now. In the cavern under Greyhold. Where the chains were. Kessmoor's engines, and the crown's chain, and the College's runes on every link, the rune of holding, drawn by Magister Dane himself. He's at Greyhold now. With Kessmoor's engineers. Setting the anchors."

"It won't hold," said Maren.

Thale went on as if she hadn't spoken.

"The beast is coming home," he said. "To the cavern. You've told us so. It'll come into the cage. And the cage will close." He looked at her down the long table. "But the council would like to be sure it comes in."

---

She knew before he said it.

She'd stood in a room like this before. Not this room. A small room at the top of the north tower at Greyhold, with a knight-captain behind a desk. In the spring before the gate. Told to take her lion down into a trap. So the Restoration couldn't have him.

"The lion," said Thale.

"No."

"It lay at ten paces from him at the Ring, I'm told. It looked at him under the Scald. It took what it wanted at Sarovel and left him standing." Thale's voice didn't change. "It comes to him. The council would like the lion in the cavern when it comes home. In the cage. A little way inside. So that it comes all the way in."

Bait, she thought.

He didn't say the word. He didn't need to.

---

Rowan was in the hall.

Maren saw her. At the side of the long table, standing, among the knights of the orders. Thin. Iron-grey. In a surcoat that wasn't mended now. New. White. With the badge of a Knight-Captain on it.

She wasn't looking at Maren.

"Knight-Captain Rowan will command the cage," said Thale. "Her lances are restored to her for it. She knows the cavern. She knows Greyhold." A pause. "She knows you, Ashdown."

Rowan didn't look up.

---

"No," said Maren.

She said it plainly. As she'd said it in the small room at the top of the north tower.

"The crown doesn't ask, Ashdown," said Thale.

"Then it can try to chain him itself."

She turned round. She walked down the long hall, past the banners of the orders, past the knights, past Rowan, who didn't look up. To the doors. Out onto the top of the steps. Into the evening. Down the steps into the Crown's Square, where Orrun was lying on the paving with his chin on his paws and the crowd standing back from him.

She put her hand on his neck.

"They want you for bait," she said. "In the cavern. In the cage. So it comes in."

Orrun lifted his head.

"I have been in that cavern," he said. "Three hundred years. Under the river. In the dark."

"I know."

"I will not be bait," said Orrun.

"I know," said Maren. "I said no."

---

Rowan came out after her.

Alone. Down the steps of the chapter-house in her new white surcoat, in the dusk, to the square. She stopped a few paces off. At the edge of the space the crowd had left round the lion.

She looked at Orrun for a long time. Then at Maren.

"I told them it wouldn't hold," she said.

Maren didn't say anything.

"In there. Before you came. I told them. I was at the Ring with the great chain. I told them what it did with the chain." Rowan looked up at the chapter-house's tall windows. "They gave me my lances back and told me to command it anyway."

"Then why are you?"

Rowan was quiet for a moment.

"Because if somebody else does," she said, "they'll do it worse."

---

She was going to Greyhold in the morning.

"Come to the yard," she said. "When you're there. Not for them." She looked at Orrun. "Not for that." She looked back at Maren. "For the yard."

She turned and went back up the steps.

Maren watched her go. Small. Neat. In a white surcoat she didn't seem to want. The woman who'd sent her down into a trap in the spring before the gate, and turned her back on the River Road at noon on the day after.

*For the yard.*

She didn't know what that meant. She thought perhaps Rowan didn't either.

---

The herald came on the three-hundred-and-thirty-fifth day.

She was at the corn exchange in the morning. Davin had asked her to come. The street tribunes wanted to see the lion-woman. They wanted to ask her about the wells.

A horse came into the square. With a blue wave on its saddle-cloth.

She knew it. The Tide Council's mark. A herald, in blue, with a satchel. He rode across the square through the bread-line, which parted for his horse without looking at him, and stopped at the corn exchange's doors, and got down, and took a hammer and a nail out of his satchel.

He nailed a notice to the door. Over Reede's paper. Printed. Black.

Then he got back on his horse and rode away.

---

Davin read it aloud.

From the steps. To the bread-line. To her. Slowly, loud, as he read everything.

"*By order of the Tide Council of the Saltreach League. To all the Leagues and kingdoms of Winshire. Notice.*"

The bread-line turned to listen.

"*The Council has sold, to the house of Strand, a contract for the taking and holding of the thing beneath the reef called the Drowned Bell. The house of Strand has assigned its execution to the company of Captain Idris Vey.*"

Davin stopped. He looked at the next line. Then he read it.

"*The thing beneath the reef is the property of the Council. Let no kingdom interfere.*"

He lowered the paper.

"The Bell," said somebody in the bread-line. "What's the Bell?"

Nobody answered.

Maren stood at the foot of the corn exchange's steps with her hand on Orrun's neck and thought of a spit of stones a league long, with four thousand people on it. And a farmer on it, if he'd done what she'd written on the back of his own page.

*Keep your coast.*

Sold. To the highest bidder. *Let no kingdom interfere.*

She turned round and went to find the street tribunes, and their wells.
