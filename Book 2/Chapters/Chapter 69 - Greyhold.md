---

Cassian was still on the gate.

She saw him from the River Road. A mile off. A shape under the charter, on the gatehouse, in the grey noon light. Hanging in his chains, where the council had hung him in the spring, with the board on his chest. She couldn't read the board from the road. She didn't need to.

*THE RESTORATION'S LEADER.*

The crows had had most of him. The chains had kept the rest.

Orrun stopped in the road and looked at it.

*They have left him.*

"Yes."

*Why?*

She thought about it. Walking. With her hand on his neck. The River Road under her boots, the same road, the same ruts, the river on her right going down grey and fast to the sea.

"So people will see him," she said.

*They see him,* said Orrun. *They don't look.*

---

The crown's blue was over the gate.

A crown company on the walls. Not knights. Men in blue coats with crossbows, bored, leaning on the merlons, who stood up straight when they saw what was coming up the road at the girl's side.

Orrun was sixteen feet at the shoulder now, and more. She'd stopped measuring him against doorways. She measured him against walls.

They let her in. Nobody asked her name.

---

The yard was full of iron.

Carts. Bars. Chain in heaps as high as a man, each link as tall as one, black and new and smelling of the quench. Men in leather aprons with Kessmoor accents going in and out of the cellar doors under the inner wall with lamps and drills and long iron rods. A winch on a timber frame over the old well-head, groaning.

And in the corner by the north tower, from the old forge, the sound of a hammer.

She knew the hammer.

---

Tam had got bigger.

In the shoulders. In the arms. He was at the anvil in the forge under the north tower with his shirt off and a leather apron on and a link of chain on the horn of the anvil the size of a cartwheel, glowing orange, and he was striking it, steady, and he didn't look up.

Odo Marl was in a chair by the forge door.

His legs had healed. Crooked. Two sticks leaned against the chair beside him. He had a blanket over his knees and his big burned hands on the arms of the chair, and he was watching every stroke.

"That one," said Odo. "Cold shut. You can see it from here. Strike it again."

Tam struck it again.

"Again."

Tam struck it again. Then he looked up and saw her in the forge door, and the lion behind her filling the yard, and the hammer stopped in the air.

"Maren," he said.

---

"They took every smith on this bank," said Tam.

Later. Sitting on the forge step with a cup of water, with the link cooling on the anvil behind him. His hands were black to the wrist. "Every smith in the Marches. The council's writ. Conscripted. For the crown's cage." He drank. "To a Kessmoor pattern. Link after link. With the runes struck inside the curve, so the thing won't see them." He looked at his hands. "Like the first ones."

"I know."

"I know you know," said Tam. "You've seen the first ones."

"And the third ones. At the Scald."

"Did they hold?"

"Yes," said Maren. "On dry land. With six hundred hearts a day."

Tam looked at her. Then at Odo.

"It's not the links," said Odo. From his chair. He hadn't looked away from the anvil. "The links are good. Most of them. I see to that." He lifted one big hand off the arm of the chair and pointed. Across the yard. At the cellar doors under the inner wall, where the Kessmoor men were going in and out with their drills. "It's what you hang them on."

---

He'd rebuilt that wall.

He told her. In the forge, while Tam struck the next link and the noise made it hard to hear. Twenty years ago. The granary's back wall, where the inner wall was thinnest. Old stone. Old-realm stone, grey and fine-grained, that the first knights had found here and built on. He'd faced the inside of it with rubble and put three iron ties through it, and the ties had held, and the old stone had crumbled round them like stale bread.

"Even then," said Odo. "Twenty years ago. It was going. Like chalk. You could scratch it with a nail." He looked at the cellar doors. "And they're drilling it. For eyes. To hang the biggest chain in the world on."

"Have you told them?"

"I've told them," said Odo. "I've told everyone. I've told the Kessmoor man and I've told the magister and I've told your captain." He put his hand back on the chair. "Ask the Kessmoor man. He's not a fool. He knows."

---

The Kessmoor man was in the cellar.

Under the inner wall. Where the Watch had kept its stores, long ago, before the stair. The cellars ran the length of the wall in a row of low vaults, and they'd knocked through between them, and the whole length was lamps and scaffolds and men, and the sound of drills going into stone.

He was square and quiet and grey at the temples, in a leather coat with Kessmoor's iron wheel on the shoulder. He was standing at the back wall of the cellar with a long iron rod in his hands, holding the end of it against the stone, with his eyes shut.

She knew what the rod was. She'd seen Pike's people use them at the Scald.

He opened his eyes.

"It reads empty," he said. To nobody. Then he saw her. And the lion in the cellar door behind her, who couldn't come in, whose head filled the doorway. He didn't jump. Kessmoor people didn't. "You're Ashdown."

"Yes."

"Isak Thurl. Master Engineer Pike's man." He held out a hand. It was rough and dry. "She said you'd come. She said to tell you she remembers."

---

"It reads empty," said Thurl.

He showed her. He put the end of the rod against the old stone and put her hand on the rod above his, and she felt nothing. Not cold. Not warm. Nothing. Like the glass at the Scald before the Titan stood up on it. A stone with nothing in it.

"Three hundred years," said Thurl. "Of the lamps drinking out of it. The bleed went up through this stone to every lamp in the kingdom. And took the Deep out of the stone as it went." He took the rod away. "It's the same stone as the first anchors. I've read those too. In the cavern. They're worse."

"Then why are you drilling it?"

"Because the crown pays Kessmoor to drill it," said Thurl. "And Kessmoor doesn't refuse the crown." He looked at the wall. "And because the magister says it'll hold."

---

The magister was behind her.

She hadn't heard him come. Very straight, in grey with the Blue College's blue at the cuffs, and gloves on both hands, and a long face that never moved much. Magister Hollis Dane. She'd seen him at the Scald, at the envoys' camp, standing on the bank in the steam with his gloved hands folded, watching the Titan lie on the glass.

"Ashdown," he said.

"Magister."

"The lion's in the doorway."

"He can't fit in."

Dane looked at Orrun's head in the doorway. Orrun looked back. Dane looked away first.

"It held three hundred years," he said. To Thurl. Not to her. As if he'd said it many times. "The stone. It held the first chains for three hundred years."

"It held something asleep," said Thurl.

Dane didn't answer him. He went past them along the cellar to where the drills were going, very straight, with his gloved hands behind his back.

Thurl watched him go.

"I've written it to Ironhythe," he said quietly. "Twice. So has Pike." He put the rod back on its hooks on the scaffold. "When it goes, there'll be a paper with my name on it saying I said so. That's what Kessmoor does." He looked at her. "It's not much."

"No," said Maren.

---

She slept at the ferry house.

In her old bed. Under the river wall, in the stone house at the bottom of the jetty road, with the guide-rope going out across the water from the post outside the door and Liss's boat drawn up on the slip.

Liss had opened the door before she knocked.

Older. Her mother was older. Greyer in her plait. The same big rough hands. She looked at Maren in the doorway, and then over her shoulder at the lion lying in the road with his chin on the jetty's end and the river going past his nose, and she didn't say anything for a while.

"You came home," said Liss.

"Yes."

"With a lion."

"He's bigger."

"I can see he's bigger," said Liss. "I can see him from the kitchen." She stood back from the door. "Come in. Wipe your feet."

---

The rope was new.

Liss told her that night. At the table, with the old lamp on it unlit, and a tallow candle instead, and bread. The old rope had worn through in the autumn and she'd spliced a new one with Tam, the two of them out on the river in the cold with the boat on a line. It was running every day now. Back and forth. People going east.

"Why?"

"Rumour," said Liss. "Your farmer's lot are taking anyone who'll come, over the Way. The Clan's grain." She broke the bread. "And they say something's coming home. Up the river. The old women in the chapel say it. The boatmen say it. Reede's paper says it." She looked at Maren. "Is it?"

"Yes."

Liss put the bread down.

"When?"

"After the sea," said Maren. "After the farmer's coast. A month. Less."

Liss looked at the window. At the river, black, going past in the dark. At the guide-rope going out over it into nothing.

"I'll need another rope," she said.

---

Rowan was in the practice yard at dawn.

Alone. In the small yard behind the south tower where the students had drilled, where Maren had drilled, a hundred mornings, with Tobin beside her complaining. Sand. A wooden rail. A rack of blunt swords.

Rowan had one of them. She was going through the forms, slowly, by herself, in her shirt, with her white surcoat folded on the rail. First form. Second. The cuts every student learned in their first week.

Maren stood at the gate.

Rowan saw her. She didn't stop. She went on through the forms to the end of the sixth, and then she lowered the blunt sword and stood there breathing, and looked at Maren across the sand.

Neither of them said anything.

---

For a long time.

The sun came up over the Saltreach bank and lit the top of the north tower. Somewhere in the yard Tam's hammer started. A Kessmoor drill. The winch.

Rowan put the blunt sword back on the rack.

"I sent you into a trap," she said.

She said it to the rack. Not to Maren.

"In the spring. Before the gate. The council told me to bait it with the lion and I did it. I put you at the end of it." She took her hands off the rack. "Because the council told me to. I always did what I was told. It's the only thing I was ever good at."

Maren didn't say anything.

"And on the River Road," said Rowan. "The day after. When the farmer walked down it with his dragons." She turned round. "I turned my back. Because I couldn't look at him. A farmer. Who'd done what three hundred years of knights hadn't. With nothing. Without being asked." She looked at Maren. "I couldn't look at him. So I turned round. And everyone saw."

---

Maren came in through the gate.

She sat down on the rail. Beside the white surcoat. The sand was cold through her boots.

"I broke Callis's arm," she said. "On the island. In the snow. With Tobin's practice sword."

"I know."

"I killed the one at the school." She looked at the sand. "I've never said that to anyone. Not to the farmer. I told him he was dead. I didn't tell him how."

Rowan didn't say anything.

"Thorne died at the river gate," said Maren. "With her boar. I was on the stair. I didn't get there."

"Nobody got there."

"I didn't get there," said Maren.

---

They talked until noon.

She didn't know how. She didn't know what about, afterwards, most of it. The yard. Hale. Haverly and his sword, which was on her hip. The trial. The stair. Tobin. The Sun Stair. The Scald. What Rowan had been told at Hallowmere in the autumn: the lamps going one by one on the morning after the river, from the gate inward, until the whole city was dark.

At noon the bell didn't ring.

They both looked up. At the bell tower over the inner wall, where the bell had rung for the oath every noon for three hundred years. Nobody said the oath now. The rope had been cut in the spring. The bell hung there in the cold with the light on it.

"It won't hold," said Rowan.

She was looking at the inner wall. At the cellar doors.

"The cage. I've written it to the council. Odo told me. The Kessmoor man told me. I've been down there with his rod." She looked at her hands. "They read the letters and they thank me and they send more iron."

"Then why command it?"

"I told you why."

"Tell me again."

Rowan looked at her.

"Because I'll hold it as long as it holds," she said. "And when it doesn't, I'll let it go. And get the people off the hill." She stood up off the rail. "Somebody else won't. Somebody else will hold on to the chain till the hill comes down on them. Because they were told to."

She picked up her white surcoat.

"And I won't chain your lion," she said. "I'll tell them he couldn't be taken. They'll believe it. They've seen him."

---

"Callis is out," said Rowan.

At the yard gate. Going. She stopped and said it over her shoulder, as if she'd nearly forgotten, and Maren knew she hadn't.

"Out of the cells at Hallowmere. In the autumn. Bought out." She put the surcoat on. "The gaoler's book shows his fine paid in full. In Saltreach gold. By a friend of the family." She did up the buckle. "The gaoler's gone too."

Maren looked at her.

"I don't know whose friend," said Rowan.

"I do," said Maren.

---

A boatman told her about the dragons.

That afternoon. At the jetty. A Saltreach man, up from Wendmouth with salt in a flat-bottomed barge, tying up at the Staithe and then crossing on Liss's boat for the Hearthmere market because he'd heard there was a lion. He stood on the jetty and looked at Orrun with his mouth open, and then he told her, because he had to tell someone.

"Dragons over Gullhaven," he said. "Forty of them. More. Red thread and grey. The Clan's colours." He made a lifting motion with his hand in the air. "Taking sacks off the roofs of the spit in baskets. Over the harbour and back. Every day. My cousin saw it from the salt pans." He shook his head. "Never seen on that coast. Never. Not in my grandfather's time."

"Why?"

"Nobody knows," said the boatman. "Practising, my cousin says. He can't say what for." He looked at the lion again. "Strange times."

---

She went down that night.

To the held breath. With Orrun. Down the water-gate stair, which was wide enough for him now only just, his shoulders brushing the walls on both sides, his mane full of old dust. Into the cavern under the river.

It was as she'd left it.

Half full of black water, still, that didn't move although the river ran over it. The six iron rings in the walls with the stumps of chain hanging from them, rusted, each link as tall as a man. The knight's lock, still lit, the rings of its hooked script glowing faint gold in the dark, the sentence she'd copied into the back of the black book in charcoal in the spring.

And at the near end, new: scaffolds. Lamps. Kessmoor drills standing in the dark, left for the night. Holes in the old stone of the walls, as wide as her waist, where the iron eyes would go.

Orrun went past them. Along the wet ledge at the edge of the black water, to the cavern's far end. And lay down.

---

The shut place.

She'd never been this close. A wall of the old stone at the end of the cavern, plain, without a carving on it, without a mark. Just stone. Fine and grey and smooth, as if something had smoothed it from the other side.

Orrun put his chin on the floor in front of it.

She put her hand on it.

It was warm.

Not the warmth of the Scald's glass. Not the heat of a fire. The warmth of something alive, a long way in. She stood with her palm flat on it in the dark and felt it, and didn't hear anything, and heard it anyway.

A breath. Held.

*It is waiting,* said Orrun.

"For what?"

He was quiet for a long time. His eyes were half shut. His chin on the stone floor of the cavern, at the foot of the shut place, as he'd lain at the foot of the arch for three hundred years.

*For the one who comes home,* he said. *And for us.*

---

Lisska came the next day at dusk.

In over the river from the south, low, thin. She'd come by the Wend's mouth and up the river, looking for the lion, and slept two nights on the Wendmouth roofs on the way. Thinner than Maren had ever seen her, her pale green eyes too big for her head. She came in over the Saltreach bank and the Staithe and the ferry rope, and landed on the ferry-house roof with a sound like a barn coming down, and lay there with her wings spread out over the slates and her sides going in and out.

Liss came out and looked at her.

"She'll want fish," said Liss. And went back in for it.

---

Two pages. In oilcloth. On her leg.

Maren read them in Liss's kitchen by the tallow candle with Liss reading over her shoulder.

The first one she'd seen the like of before. His big square careful letters, getting smaller to fit. A spit of stones. White crosses. Forty baskets. And at the top, in the biggest letters she'd seen him make:

*WHEN THE COAST IS DONE THE CLAN WILL COME TO THE RIVER.*

The second was short. Six lines. The Council, the oysters, Vey's harpoons, the Clan over Corsa. And the last one.

*Keep the door.*

"He writes like a child," said Liss.

"He writes," said Maren.

Liss looked at the page a while longer.

"He does," she said.

---

Lisska talked to Orrun.

Up on the roof, with her head hanging over the eaves and Orrun lying in the road below with his head up, the two of them looking at each other, not making a sound. A long time. Maren sat on the step and waited.

*The coast,* said Orrun at last. *The spit. Four thousand. The sea-council would not. The Clan went over their city and they said they would not hinder.* He was quiet. *He was afraid, in their hall. He read anyway. She says he read every line.*

"Yes," said Maren.

*She says he says keep the door.*

"I read it."

*She says he says it every night,* said Orrun. *Before he sleeps. To her. She says to tell you.*

---

She wrote the answer the next day.

On the back of his Corsa page, because there was no other paper in the house that wasn't Liss's ferry-book, and Liss wouldn't give her a page of that. In her square hand. At the kitchen table, with Odo in his chair by the fire, carried down from the forge by Tam that morning to see the dragon, and Tam on the step, and Liss at the window.

*The cage won't hold.*

*They are hanging it on the inner wall. The inner wall is the same stone as the first anchors and it's empty. Odo says so. The Kessmoor engineer says so. Rowan says so. Dane says it held three hundred years.*

*The crown wants Orrun for bait. No.*

*Callis is out. Corvin bought him.*

She stopped. She looked at the candle.

*Keep your spit. Then come.*

She read it over. She didn't cross anything out.

"That's not much of a letter," said Odo, from the fire.

"It's what's true," said Maren.

Odo thought about it.

"It's a good letter," he said.

---

Lisska went at dawn.

Fed. Fish from Liss's barrels and a goat from the Staithe that Liss paid for out of the ferry-box without being asked. She stood up on the roof in the grey light with the page in oilcloth on her leg and looked down at Orrun.

Then she went off the roof over the river and turned south-east, low, toward the sea.

Maren stood on the jetty and watched her go until she was gone.

Behind her, up on the hill, the Kessmoor drills started. Into the old stone. One after another, going in.
