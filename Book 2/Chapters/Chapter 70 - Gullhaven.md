---

The town knew before they landed.

A Corsa boat had come in on the morning tide. Fast. With the news. The Council had said no. The Council had said it was oyster season. And then forty dragons had gone round and round over the Tidehall like gulls over a gutting-table, and the Council had said it wouldn't hinder.

They were on their roofs when the Clan came in over the spit.

Calder saw them from Kharrak's back. Every white cross had somebody standing on it. Looking up. Watching the flights come down along the shingle, pair after pair, red and grey, onto the stones between the smoke-houses and the sea.

---

Arn stood in the square by the harbour-master's door.

Where the Council's notice was still nailed up. He stood under it, with his red behind him filling the end of the square, and said it once, loud enough for the roofs.

"Anyone who'll go now," he said, "the Clan will fly to the hills. Today. With what you can carry."

Nobody moved.

"The old," said Arn. "The sick. Mothers with babies. Anyone who can't climb onto a roof in the dark with the sea coming in the door." He looked round the square. "Today. Not that night. Today."

---

Some went.

He'd thought none would. Some did. An old man first, with a stick and a bundle, who came across the square and stood in front of Arn and said "I'm eighty-four and I've never been off this spit and I'd like to see the hills before I die," and Arn put him in a basket himself.

Then a woman with twins. Then a boy with his grandmother, who didn't want to go and went because the boy was crying. Then more.

Two hundred and some. Over four days. The baskets going back and forth over the harbour to the hill above the salt-store and the farms behind the pans, all day, from first light, with the riders swapping dragons at noon so nobody flew tired.

The rest stayed.

Oyster season. The beds. The smoke-houses full and the barrels waiting for the Corsa boats. And under all of it, said in the square, said on the roofs, said by Hessa Pane at the water's edge at dawn with a cup of brine in her white hand:

"The Lady keeps the spit."

---

Ulric carried Nan Gorrie.

On the third day. Calder didn't see how it started. He came down the spit road from the salt-store with an empty basket and found a crowd at a net-shed door near the spit's middle. Ulric's grey lying in the road. Ulric on one knee on the shingle in front of the door, with his arms folded on his knee, listening.

An old woman in the doorway. Very old. Small. With white eyes. Blind. A net-needle still in her hand.

"I'm not salt," she was saying, "and I'm not a child, and I'm not going in a basket like a herring."

"No," said Ulric.

"My son used to carry me," said the old woman. "When I was sick. On his back. Up the spit road to the chapel. Every week." Her white eyes looked at nothing over Ulric's head. "He drowned. Twenty years back. Off the Bell."

Ulric looked at her for a while.

Then he turned round, on one knee, on the shingle, with his back to her.

---

She climbed on.

Somebody helped her. Hessa Pane, with her white arms. The old woman got her arms round Ulric's neck and her knees round his sides and Ulric stood up with her on his back, slowly, as if she weighed nothing, and she didn't.

He walked her off the spit.

The whole league of it. On foot. Down the spit road between the smoke-houses and the net-sheds and the chapel, with his grey walking behind him in the road like a dog. People came out of their doors to watch. The old woman talked the whole way. Calder flew over them twice on Kharrak and he could hear her from the air. About her son. About the nets. About the spit when she was a girl, when the oysters were so thick you could walk on them at low water.

Ulric didn't say anything. He walked.

At the spit's root, at the salt-store, he put her down on a bench in the sun.

She felt for his face with both hands. Found it. The scar through his eyebrow. She felt it with her thumb.

"You're not him," she said. "You're a good boy, though."

Ulric was quiet all evening.

---

The reefwyrms came in on the fourth day.

A few first. Calder saw them from the spit's end in the morning: long grey-green backs rolling in the harbour inside the spit's arm, where the water was calm. Three. Five. Each as long as a fishing boat. The oyster-men pointed at them and laughed and swore and went out anyway.

By noon there were fifty.

By the next morning, hundreds.

---

He'd never seen anything like it.

Nobody had. The whole harbour inside the spit was them. Packed. Grey-green bodies, eel-long, thick as a man, lying against each other and over each other in the shallows along the shingle until the water boiled with them. Not hunting. Not feeding. They didn't go for the boats. They didn't go for anything. They lay packed against the stones of the spit with their blunt heads in under the jetties, as close to the land as they could get.

"Hiding," said Ada. On the shingle. With her shawl round her. Looking at them.

The oyster-men couldn't get their boats out. The beds were out beyond the harbour mouth and the harbour was full of reefwyrms.

"They come in in a storm," said Hessa Pane. Standing beside them with her arms folded. Frowning at the water. "Sometimes. A few. When there's a big sea coming."

Calder looked at the sky. Blue. A light wind from the west. The sea outside the spit flat and grey and quiet, all the way to the line of breakers on the Bell.

"There's no storm," he said.

"No," said Hessa Pane.

---

They came from the Bell.

Everyone knew it. The reef was thick with them. Always had been. It was why the oyster-men wouldn't go near it, why the pirates used it for a landmark and never anchored there. A ship that went too close to the Bell at low water came back with its oars bitten through.

They'd left it. All of them. Every reefwyrm on the Bell had come in two leagues over the open sea to lie against the stones of Gullhaven.

Calder stood on the spit's end that evening with his feet on the shingle and felt it.

He could, now. Since the reef. Without Aurveth. A little. A pull, a long way down, under the stones, under the sea. A breath. Going in. Held. Going out.

Quicker every day.

---

The Strand fleet came on the seventh day.

From the east. Along the coast from Corsa, past the Saltings. Green sails. He counted them from the spit's end with Arn. Eleven ships. Four of them towing barges, low, broad, and on each barge an iron engine the size of a house, black, with a chimney, smoking. Kessmoor engines. The kind he'd seen in Maren's letters, drawn small in the margins.

Five harpoon-ships. With great bolt-throwers on their bows, wound back, and racks of harpoons along their sides as long as a mast, every one cut with runes from the point to the barbs.

And a small black ship with a single mast, neat, quick, with two hooded shapes on its deck. Wyverns. Black-green. Sable and Hask.

Vey's.

The fleet anchored in the bay outside the line of breakers. Between the Bell and the open sea. In a long line, with the engine-barges in the middle.

"Well," said Arn. "There's the Council."

---

Vey came ashore once.

On the eighth day. In a rowing boat, with two oarsmen, through the packed reefwyrms in the harbour, which moved for the boat slowly and closed again behind it. He stepped onto the jetty in his grey gloves and spectacles and looked at the town on its roofs, and the forty dragons on the shingle, and Calder.

"Master Fenn."

"Captain."

"I've come to count the sheds," said Vey. "Since I'm being paid for them."

---

Arn took him round.

Calder went with them. Off the spit, across to the mainland behind the harbour. The salt pans. Square, grey, in rows, with the low sheds along their edges where the salt was raked and dried and bagged. Wooden sheds. Thin roofs. Nothing a dragon could land on without going through.

The children worked there. Raking. Bagging. Every child in Gullhaven over seven.

Vey walked the rows with his hands behind his back and counted.

"Fourteen sheds," he said. "Two wyverns. A wyvern can lift three children. Four if they're small." He looked at the sheds. "Seven trips each. If the sheds stand that long."

"Will they?"

"I don't know," said Vey. "I've never seen what's coming." He looked out over the pans at the sea. "Neither have you."

He got back in his boat and went out through the reefwyrms to his black ship.

---

The gull-hawks wouldn't fly on the ninth day.

The harbour-master's birds. Gullhaven's messages to Corsa and the boats. They sat on their long perch on the harbour-master's roof in a row, a dozen of them, grey and white and hook-beaked, all appetite, and wouldn't go.

The harbour-master's boy threw one off the roof with a message on its leg for the Strand flagship. It went up, and turned, and came back to the perch.

He threw it again. It came back.

He threw another. It went out over the harbour, over the reefwyrms, out through the harbour mouth toward the fleet and the Bell, low, and then it turned in the air as if it had flown into a wall, and came back, and landed on the perch with its feathers wet and stood there shaking.

None of them would go out over the bay again.

---

Aurveth's month was up.

Ivar Leck said so, and let her fly. Short. Once round the harbour and down. She did it, slowly, with her crooked wing, and landed on the shingle at the spit's end beside Calder, heavily, and lay there.

*It's closer,* she said.

"I know."

*You know a little,* said Aurveth. *Lie down.*

He lay down on the stones against her side with his hand behind her jaw, where her neck plates met, and she opened it for him a little. Not the deep sight. A crack. Enough.

The breath. Under the Bell. Quick now. In, held, out. In. Held. Out.

Like a sleeper at the very end of sleep. When you can hear them come up out of it, and you know the next breath they'll open their eyes.

---

Lisska came in from the west on the tenth day.

Along the coast. Low. Thin. From Greyhold, down the Wend to its mouth and along the shore, two days. She came in over the reefwyrms in the harbour, and they didn't move for her, and she landed on the shingle by Kharrak and put her head down on the stones.

*Calder.*

"Liss."

*They're building a cage,* she said. *At the lion-woman's home. Iron in the yard. Men drilling the hill. Chain as tall as a man, link after link.* She lifted her head. *And the lion-woman says it won't hold.*

---

One page. On the back of his own.

He read it on the shingle. Without his finger. Her square hand.

*The cage won't hold.*

*They are hanging it on the inner wall. The inner wall is the same stone as the first anchors and it's empty. Odo says so. The Kessmoor engineer says so. Rowan says so. Dane says it held three hundred years.*

*The crown wants Orrun for bait. No.*

*Callis is out. Corvin bought him.*

*Keep your spit. Then come.*

He read it twice.

*Then come.*

He folded it and put it in his coat with the others. Her answer page. The five-line letter. The eleven pages. Rook's book. Ada's account. They made a lump in his coat over his ribs now, the size of a fist.

---

The stir came that night.

He was asleep against Aurveth on the shingle. She woke him. She lifted her head, and he felt it through her before he was awake: something coming up under the sea. All at once. Everywhere.

Then the shouting started.

---

The boats were burning.

Burning without flame. He ran down the spit's end to the shingle where the fishing boats were drawn up in rows, and saw it. Every boat. Every hull. Every oar laid across every thwart.

Saltreach's sea-charms were runes. They had to be. A spoken charm broke on running water, every child on the coast knew it, and the sea was the biggest running water there was. So the warranted charm-cutters in every port cut them into the wood. Keel-runes for steady. Bow-runes for true. Oar-runes on the blades for pull. Every boat in Gullhaven had them. Every oar.

They were glowing.

Every cut. White. In lines along the keels. In rings round the bows. On every oar-blade on every boat on the shingle. White, then brighter, and then the wood round the cuts began to smoke.

---

Men were running out of the houses.

Fishermen. In their shirts. Dragging their boats down the shingle to the water, throwing water on them with buckets, with their hands, with their hats. The runes hissed where the water hit them and went on burning. A man stood up to his knees in the harbour among the reefwyrms holding an oar under the water, and it boiled in his hands, and he wouldn't let go.

Out in the bay, the Strand fleet was glowing.

He could see it from the shingle. Eleven ships in a line in the dark beyond the breakers, and along every hull, in lines, white. Their runes. The harpoon-ships' runes. The racks of harpoons along their sides, every one lit from point to barb, white in the dark like a row of candles.

---

He knew what it was.

He knew it now. He'd felt it at Vael, coming up the Wend's valley from the lake. He'd felt it through Lisska at the Scald, coming up through the glass. He'd stood in it on the reef with his face on the barnacles.

He could feel it through Aurveth, beside him on the shingle with her head up. The Deep. Coming up under the sea. All at once. Under the Bell, under the bay, under the spit, under his feet. Up through every rune cut into every piece of wood on the coast and setting it on fire.

"It's the stir," he said.

Ada was beside him. In her nightgown, with her shawl. She'd come down from the Anchor-and-Net with the rest of the town.

"What does that mean?"

"It means it's coming," said Calder. "The big one. The one with the others in its back." He looked out at the bay, at the fleet glowing in the dark. "It came up like this at Vael, after. I saw the sky go white from a long way off. And at the Scald, through her."

---

It came the next day.

At dusk. He was on the spit's end with Aurveth and Arn and Ulric. The burnt boats along the shingle behind them, black at the runes, every one. The reefwyrms in the harbour. The town on its roofs again, all of it, looking out to sea.

Aurveth lifted her head.

*There,* she said. *East. A day behind the stir. It's come.*

He couldn't see anything. The sea. Grey. Flat. The line of breakers on the Bell. The fleet beyond it with its runes gone dull now, scorched along the hulls. The sun going down red behind the spit.

Then he could.

---

On the sea's rim. To the east, where the Saltings came down to the shore. Low. Long. Darker than the water.

Like an island. Where there'd never been an island.

It came in through the dusk. Slowly. Not swimming. Not flying. He couldn't tell what it was doing. It came in over the sea toward them, and got bigger, and bigger, and didn't stop getting bigger, until it was the size of the reef, and then bigger than the reef.

It stopped in the deep water beyond the Bell's breakers. Two leagues off. Between the reef and the fleet.

And lay down.

---

The fleet's lamps came on. One after another. Along the line. He heard horns, very faint, across the water.

The thing in the sea didn't move.

"Why's it stopped?" said Arn.

Aurveth was watching it with her old gold eyes. Her chin on the shingle. The wind off the sea moving the fringe of her neck plates.

*It's waiting,* she said.

"For what?"

*For the tide,* said Aurveth. *The bell rings at the lowest tide. When the reef comes out of the water.* She shut her eyes. *Three days.*

---

He stood on the spit's end in the dark until the moon came up.

Out in the bay, the shape lay on the sea. Dark. Long. Still. With the moon on it. Bigger than the reef.

Behind him, four hundred and twelve white crosses on four hundred and twelve roofs, and people on every one, standing in the moonlight, looking at it.

Nobody went in.
