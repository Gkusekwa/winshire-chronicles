---

They came north on the wyverns.

He didn't remember much of it. The Saltings' mouth, at dusk, with the bell still ringing in his teeth. Corvin giving the ship to Mauger: *Wendmouth. Wait.* The hoods coming off the two wyverns on the deck, and their eyes, with nothing in them. A harness of Halloran leather. Being strapped into it behind a binder, with Corvin behind him, Corvin's good arm round his chest.

Then the air.

Two days of it. North over the Saltings and the scrub of the Ashlings, low, fast, with the wind tearing at his coat and his back screaming at every wingbeat. Down at noon in the scrub to rest the beasts. Up. Down at dusk. He lay on the ground both nights and didn't sleep, and couldn't stand in the mornings, and they lifted him back into the harness like a sack.

On the second day at dusk they came down in a wood.

---

The Brackenwood.

A royal hunting ground, Callis said. Beside Greyhold. An hour's walk from the river wall. Oak and holly and bracken, brown with winter, and in a hollow among the oaks, a camp.

Forty men. Rings on their fingers, iron. Broken men, with old Restoration coats and no banner. Five binders with their pots. A pen of oxen made of hurdles, the oxen standing in it with their heads low and nothing in their eyes. Horses. A pile of Saltreach gold in a cloth on a stump, with a man sitting beside it with a crossbow.

They looked at Corvin when he came down off the wyvern.

As men look at the one who pays them.

---

They took Cassian off the gate the next night.

He didn't see it. He heard it. He lay by the fire in the Brackenwood, and Callis sat on a log across from him with his crooked arms held against his body, and told the band where the gate-watch stood.

He'd been a knight of the gate. He knew.

Ten men on the gate, he said. Crown men, not knights. Watching the river. Watching the cage. Nobody watched the charter. Nobody had looked at what hung under it since the spring.

They came back before dawn with it on a hurdle.

Still in its chains. He didn't look. He heard Corvin get up from the fire and go to it, and stand there, and not say anything.

---

Corvin cut the chains off himself.

The next day. In the hollow. With a smith's chisel and a hammer from the band's cart. Link by link. It took him the whole morning, with one good arm, kneeling on the ground beside the hurdle with the chisel held in his bad hand and the hammer in his good one. Striking. Every link.

A man from the band came to help him.

Corvin looked up at him. The man went away.

Nobody else came.

---

In the afternoon Corvin wrote a note.

At the stump with the gold on it. On a leaf from his black book. Idony saw him write it. He didn't hide it.

*The eddy, tonight, when the moon's up. An hour. Come alone. I'll bring the reader. C.V.*

He gave it to a boy from the band. Fourteen or so. A ring too big for him on his finger, on a string round his neck.

"The ferry house under the river wall," said Corvin. "Give it to the woman with the lion. Nobody else."

The boy looked at him with his mouth open.

"She won't hurt you," said Corvin. "The lion won't, while she's there."

---

The eddy was where the island had been.

He'd seen the island on his charts. The Old Ruin. On its rock in the river. Where the walker had come from, the day the glasses broke in Vael. The Marches' oldest thing.

It was gone.

It had fallen. In the spring, when the chains went, and the lock, and the thing under it stood up. The river ran over it now. And where it had been, the river turned. Slowly. In a wide brown eddy, round and round, over the island's drowned stones. He could see them under the water in the moonlight. Grey. Square. Cut.

A shingle bar on the Hearthmere bank.

A boat on it. A broad flat river-boat, borrowed from Ferriby's jetty, with a pyre built on its deck. Brackenwood oak. A cask of lamp-oil beside it.

And on the pyre, wrapped in a grey cloak, Cassian.

---

Callis held the boat.

He couldn't hold a rope. His arms wouldn't. So he stood on the shingle with the mooring-rope passed round his body, under his arms, across his back, and leaned against it, and held the boat in the eddy with his weight.

Corvin poled it out. Into the slow turn of the water. The rope paying out round Callis's body.

Idony was on the boat.

Corvin had wanted him there. He didn't say why. He sat on the boat's stern-thwart with the spar-stick across his knees and the pyre in front of him, and Corvin stood at the pyre with a torch.

---

Corvin lit it.

At the foot. Where the oil was. It caught slowly, and then all at once, and went up into the night with a sound like a sail filling, gold, and the light of it went out over the eddy and lit the drowned stones under the water.

Corvin stood back from it.

He put his good hand on the boat's rail.

And talked.

---

Not to Idony. To the fire.

Quietly. He never raised his voice.

"Our father stood at the back of the line," he said. "At Greyhold. Thirty years. Garran Vale. A knight of the gate. Hearthmere-born. Nothing ever came down out of the sky to his fist." He watched the fire. "He said the noon words every day with his face to the wall. *Keep the watch below. And do not ask.* Every day. Thirty years."

The fire went up into the dark.

"They set him to dig a drain," said Corvin. "Under the grooms' yard. Outside the west wall. It was the work they gave the ones who'd never been chosen and hadn't had the sense to leave." He was quiet. "He was forty. He dug it himself. With a spade. In the rain."

---

"He broke into a stair," said Corvin.

"Under the grooms' yard. A spade's depth down. A slab, and under it, steps. Going down. Old. Older than the Watch's stair, older than the school. Cut in the old stone, the grey stone, the inner wall's stone. With writing on the walls. That hooked hand. All the way down."

He looked at Idony.

"You'd have given your eyes for it."

Idony didn't say anything.

"He went down it," said Corvin. "Alone. With a lamp. For a night. He never told me how far. Far. At the foot of it there was a ledge, and on the ledge, a key." He looked back at the fire. "Black. Iron. Long. With hooked marks on its bow. Older than any key the Watch had."

"The key to the Ruin's lock," said Idony.

"The key to the Ruin's lock," said Corvin.

---

"He came back up," said Corvin. "He put the slab back. He put the drain over it. He told nobody in Greyhold." He watched the fire. "Not the captain. Not the Watch. Not the knights he stood behind for thirty years. Nobody."

"Why?"

Corvin was quiet for a long time.

"Because nobody had ever asked him anything," he said. "In thirty years. Not one thing." He looked at his hand on the rail. "So he didn't tell them."

---

"He told us," said Corvin.

"Everything. At the table, in the grooms' cottage, every night. What he'd seen. What was cut on the walls. He'd copied it, what he could, on the backs of the drain-plans." Something nearly a smile. "What the noon words were for. Not what they said. What they were for. He made us say it back. Every night. Both of us. Two boys with one face."

He took his hand off the rail.

"He died when we were fifteen," he said. "They buried him outside the wall. With the grooms." He looked at the fire. "On top of his stair. He'd asked for the place. Nobody asked him why."

---

The pyre fell in.

All at once. The oak giving way under its own weight, and the cloak and what was in it going down into the fire, and sparks going up in a column into the night over the eddy. Gold. Turning with the water.

Corvin didn't move.

Idony watched his face. In the firelight. It didn't change. He'd thought it would. Long and pale and very still, with the grey eyes on the fire, and it didn't change at all.

His hand on the rail was white.

"He was the warm one," said Corvin.

Quietly.

"I was the one who listened. He was the one people listened to." He watched the sparks go up. "He'd have talked to her. Ashdown. He'd have had her laughing in an hour." He was quiet. "I never could."

On the shingle, at the end of the rope, Callis looked away.

---

She came at moonrise.

Along the shingle bar. From downriver. On foot. Alone.

No knights. No lanterns. He saw her small and dark against the pale stones, walking, with the river on her right, and the moon coming up over the Saltreach bank beyond it.

And behind her, the lion.

He'd grown again. Idony saw it even in the moonlight. Bigger than at the Sun Stair. Bigger than anything on four legs he'd ever seen that wasn't a Titan. He filled the shingle bar from the water to the bank, and his mane caught the moonlight, and his eyes caught the fire.

She stopped at the water's edge.

---

Corvin poled the boat in.

Into the shallows. The pyre still burning on its deck, lower now, red. He jumped down into the water to his knees and walked up onto the shingle. Ten paces from her. Then eight.

"Reader," he said. Over his shoulder.

Idony got down. Into the water. It was cold enough to stop his heart. He walked up the shingle on the spar-stick and stood behind Corvin's shoulder.

Callis behind them, with the rope.

Maren looked at Corvin. Then at Idony.

Then back at Corvin.

"An hour," she said.

"An hour," said Corvin.

---

He told her.

Plainly. As he'd told Idony on the deck in the dark. Quietly.

"It has five," he said. "In its back. You've seen them go in. It's coming home. Here. Tomorrow, or the next day. The reader's glasses say so."

"I know."

"The crown's cage won't hold it."

"I know."

"Your Kessmoor man's written to Ironhythe twice to say so," said Corvin. "Callis heard it in the Brackenwood from a carter who heard it in your yard." He looked at her. "Nothing will hold it. Not the cage. Not the knights. Not your lion."

Maren didn't say anything.

"When it comes," said Corvin, "it'll break the old magic under the hill. The shut place. It's what it's coming for. It'll break it open." He looked at the river. "And I mean to be in the vault before it gets there. With the reader. And the roll."

"For the ring."

"For the ring," said Corvin. "Then it'll answer to someone."

"To you."

"To someone who's thought about it," said Corvin.

---

He didn't offer her anything.

Idony had thought he would. He'd offered Idony a college. He offered her nothing. He stood on the shingle with his bound arm and his long pale face, and the pyre burning down behind him on the water, and told her what he meant to do.

"You can't stop it either," he said. "The big one. You know you can't." He looked at the lion. "I wanted you to know. So you'd stand aside."

"Why?"

"Because he'd have wanted me to ask," said Corvin.

He looked at the fire.

---

She looked at Idony.

For a long time. He looked back. He knew what she was seeing. He'd seen it himself in the brass of the stern-cabin lamp. Thinner. Grey at the temples, as he'd been at the Sun Stair. No stick. Held up by a piece of spar. The brown robe of a hand, still, under his winter coat, filthy from the wyvern.

She looked at his hands. On the spar. Blue with cold.

Then at Corvin. Three paces from her. With one good arm.

Then she put her hand on the lion's mane.

---

He felt it come off the lion.

Like heat. Like the heat off a forge. He'd stood near Orrun at the Scald, at the Sun Stair, at Glasswater. He'd never felt this. The lion wanted it. Wanted to. Three paces. A man with one arm. One stroke.

And Callis behind with a rope, and forty men in the trees with crossbows, and two wyverns.

And Idony in the middle of it.

The lion's head went down. Low. His lip came up off his teeth. The great shoulders bunched.

Maren didn't look at him.

She looked at Corvin. And held the mane.

Idony saw her hand. Her knuckles white in the grey mane, as white as the moon on the shingle. Holding. And the lion trembling under it, all along his length, like a dog on a short rope who can see the hare.

He didn't move.

She didn't let him.

---

The hour went.

Nobody said much after that. Corvin stood on the shingle and watched the pyre burn down. Maren stood with her hand in the mane. The lion stood. The moon went up over the Saltreach bank and the eddy turned and turned over the drowned stones.

At the end of it she looked at Idony.

"He said keep the door," she said. "The farmer. He says it every night. Before he sleeps."

Idony didn't understand.

He looked at Corvin. Corvin did. Idony saw it. Something went across the long pale face, quick, and was gone. Corvin looked at her.

"I will," said Maren.

She took her hand off the mane.

She turned round and walked back down the shingle bar the way she'd come. Small and dark against the pale stones. The lion turned after her, slowly, and went, and looked back once, at Corvin, over his shoulder.

---

Corvin watched her go.

Until she was gone. Round the bend of the bank, into the dark, downriver. The lion last.

"She'll come down after us," he said.

"Yes," said Idony.

"Good," said Corvin.

He turned round and looked at the boat. At what was left of the pyre on its deck, red, going out, turning slowly in the eddy at the end of Callis's rope.

"I'd rather she was there too," he said.
