---

They came up at dawn.

Orrun found the way. Not by the pull: the pull went down. By the stair. By the old ones' tread, worn into the middle of every step by something very heavy a long time ago. He put his feet in it and went up, and she followed the lamp, and the flights went by. Forty steps. A landing. Forty steps.

She'd relit Liss's lamp twice more in the night. With Ada's word, and her hand flat on his side. Her hands were numb to the wrists now. She carried the lamp in both of them, pressed together, like a cup.

At the top there was grey light.

---

Wynn was there.

At the stair's head, on the grass of the grooms' burial ground, with his hounds lying either side of him and his sword across his knees. Three knights behind him. Joby Marsh asleep sitting up against Garran Vale's stone with his lantern out in his lap.

Wynn stood up when he saw the lion's head come out of the ground.

"Nobody came up," he said.

"No."

"Did you find them?"

"No," said Maren.

---

The cage was strung.

She saw it as she came round the west wall into the light. They'd done it in the night. While she was under the hill.

Six chains. Out of the rock below the inner wall, out of the great iron eyes the Kessmoor men had drilled, across the river in long black curves to the far bank. To six iron posts on the Saltreach side, each as big as a house, set in stone, a few hundred paces upriver of the Staithe. Every link as tall as a man. Hanging over the water. The river going under them brown and fast.

On the inside of every link, a line of white.

---

The yard was engines.

Two of them. Kessmoor's. Black iron the size of a barn, brought up the Wend on barges in the autumn and set in the yard on beds of timber. Two more on the Saltreach bank, by the anchors. Idling. Steaming. A sound out of them like a great beast breathing slowly in its sleep. Pipes and rods going from them to the chains' ends, where the chains came out of the rock.

The heartstones were in them. She knew what that meant. Pike had shown her, at the Scald. Hearts. Beasts' hearts, burning.

On the inner wall, the binders. Dane's. Twelve of them, in the Blue College's blue, standing along the wall's top above the eyes, with their hands folded. Waiting.

And on the north tower, in her white surcoat, Rowan.

---

The envoys came during the day.

She saw them across the river. On the slope above the Staithe, on the Saltreach bank, behind the anchors. Tents going up. Flags. Horses. The iron wheel of Kessmoor. The Blue College's blue. A gold sun on white, which had to be Sarovel, though she didn't know who. Others.

They'd come to watch. As they'd come to watch the cage at the Scald.

She didn't go over.

---

The landing was full.

The last of them. Millbrook's lower lane, which had held out longest. Ferriby's stragglers. The crown company's camp-women with their bundles and their children. Greyhold's grooms, all of them, who'd been told by Rowan in the morning to go and had gone to the jetty in a body, with Joby at the front.

The ferry going back and forth on the new rope. Liss in the bow. The crown's barge beside it.

And at the bottom of the jetty road, by the river wall, a knot of people who weren't moving.

---

Seventy, perhaps.

Old, most of them. Thin. The poorest of the lower lane and the river-flats below Millbrook, the people who lived in the reed huts where the bank flooded every spring. Fishers. Eel-trappers. Women who'd taken in washing. They stood together with their backs to the river wall and their bundles at their feet and looked at the ferry going across, and didn't get in the queue.

"They won't cross," said Wynn. "I've asked them three times."

Maren went to them.

---

"The river took my man," said an old woman.

At the front. Small, bent, with a face as brown and creased as a walnut shell and eyes like a heron's. Eighty, perhaps. She looked at Maren and the lion behind her without blinking.

"Mother Ketch," said Wynn quietly. "Millbrook. The lower lane."

"Your mother's river," said Mother Ketch. "It took my man. And my two boys. Off the eel-weir, in the flood, thirty years back." She looked at the ferry. "I'll not go on it. None of us will. We've never been over. We'll not go now." She looked up at the hill. At Greyhold. "We'll go in the stone."

---

Rowan let them.

Into the cellars. Maren went up to the north tower and found her and told her, and Rowan came down and looked at them, seventy people at the foot of the river wall who wouldn't cross, and thought about it for a long time.

"The cellars," she said. "Under the inner wall. They're the deepest stone in the Marches."

"The anchors are in that wall."

"I know where the anchors are."

Thurl was behind her. With his rod. He'd come down from the yard. He looked at the people. Then at Maren.

"She's right and so are you," he said. "The cellars are the strongest roof on the hill. Old vaults. Thick. It's the wall above them that's empty." He looked up at the wall. At the eyes in the rock, with the chains coming out of them. "If it goes, it goes outward. Toward the river. With the chains. Not down."

"You're sure."

"No," said Thurl.

---

She couldn't make them.

She tried. She stood in front of Mother Ketch and told her what was coming. The big one. Out of the sky. With five in its back. She told her about Gullhaven, as much as Lisska had given her, and the reef, and the wave. She told her about the Scald, and the Sun Stair, and Vael. She told her the river was the one thing that stopped a binding.

Mother Ketch listened to all of it.

"You're Liss Ashdown's girl," she said.

"Yes."

"You talk like her." Mother Ketch picked up her bundle. "I'll go in the stone."

---

They went in at noon.

Down the cellar steps under the inner wall. Seventy of them. With their bundles and their blankets and a few hens in a basket. Into the long vaults the Kessmoor men had knocked through, where the drills had stood, lit now by a few lanterns on the scaffolds.

Maren found Joby Marsh at the jetty.

"Stay with them," she said. "With a lantern. Please."

Joby looked at the ferry. At the queue. His grandson was in it, a boy of twelve, with a bundle.

He looked at the cellar door.

"I'll stay," he said.

---

Tam's chain was on the anvil.

She went up to the forge in the afternoon to find him. The north tower's forge, with the fire burning low. And on the horn of the anvil, a link. The last one. Half-struck. The orange gone out of it. Grey.

Tam was standing beside it with his hammer in his hand.

He looked at the link. He looked at the chains going out over the river from the rock below the inner wall, six of them, finished, with every link in them that he'd struck.

He put his hammer down. On the link. On the anvil.

"It's not going to be wanted," he said.

"It was never going to be enough," said Odo. From his chair by the forge door. He'd been sitting there all day. He looked at the link on the anvil. "None of it. I told you."

"You told everyone," said Tam.

"I did," said Odo.

---

Tam carried him down.

The chair and Odo in it. Himself. On his back, the chair's legs over his shoulders and Odo sitting in it facing backward up the road, with his blanket over his crooked legs and his two sticks across his knees, talking the whole way. Down the jetty road. Past the dead lamps. To the jetty.

Liss's boat was there.

The last crossing. She'd said so. When it was dark she'd go over and stay over. The barge would do the rest, for the knights.

---

They loaded it at dusk.

Odo's chair first. Tam set it down in the boat's middle and lashed its legs to the thwarts with rope. Then the last cart, with Odo's tools in it, the good anvil, the tongs and the swages. Then Joby's grandson, with his bundle. Then a woman from Ferriby with a goat.

Then Tam.

He stood in the bow with the lantern. He looked at Maren on the jetty.

"Come over," he said.

"No."

He looked at her for a moment longer. Then he lit the lantern.

---

Liss was the last in.

She stood on the jetty beside Maren. The river going past. The rope humming. The chains overhead in the dusk, black, huge, with their white lines.

"Come over," said Liss.

"No."

Liss looked at the river.

"Then come home after," she said.

Maren took the lamp out of her coat.

The brass one. With its glass chimney. Out, now, cold. She held it out in both her numb hands.

"You said bring it back," she said.

"I said it twice," said Liss.

She took it.

---

The boat went out.

On the new rope. Hand over hand. Liss at the rope and Tam in the bow with the lantern and Odo in his chair in the middle with his back to the river and his face to the jetty. Out into the current. Into the dusk.

Maren stood at the jetty's end and watched it go.

Halfway. Further. The lantern small. The far bank. The Staithe. The ferry post with the rope tied off. A long low shape lying on the shingle above the Staithe, red, that she knew. And beside it, a bigger one, old, lying flat.

And a man. Standing by the post. Waiting.

---

The boat came in to him.

She saw it. The lantern coming up to the post. The man taking the rope. Helping. Somebody getting out. A chair being lifted out of the boat by two men, one of them Tam, and set down on the shingle.

Then a smaller figure, getting out. Liss. Standing on the shingle by the post.

The man standing in front of her.

They were too far to see faces. Two figures by a lantern on the far bank. Then Liss put her hand out, she thought, and the man took it. Or she imagined it.

She didn't cross.

---

Liss didn't come back.

The barge did. Once. The last trip. The knights at its sweeps brought it back across in the dark to the Greyhold side and tied it up under the river wall, for the cage.

After that nothing went over.

The rope hummed in the dark across the river. Black. Empty.

---

The fire came down off the Scarfell at night.

She was sitting on the jetty with her back against the post. Orrun lying across the road behind her with his chin on the boards. The river going by under the chains. The fires of Arn's flights on the hill above Harrow's End, small, a league off on the far bank.

Then, beyond them, to the north-east, high on the dark rim of the moor, fire.

A line of it. Gold. Short. Then another. Then more. Breath. Dragon-breath, in the dark, high up over the Scarfell, coming down. Coming down off the moor toward the river in long lines, one after another. Gold in the night.

Not thirty.

More than she could count.

---

Orrun lifted his head.

He watched the fire come down off the moor. All the way down. Line after line of it, going out over the hills above Harrow's End and coming down there among the Clan's fires, until the hillside on the far bank was all fires, and the sky above it full of the sound of wings.

*They are all coming,* he said.

"All of them?"

*All of them,* said Orrun. *Every one on the high ledges. The old ones. The young ones. The ones who never fly.* He was quiet. *What was on his page.*

She sat on the jetty with the rope humming beside her in the dark and watched the far bank fill with dragons, and didn't sleep.

Above her, on the north tower, Rowan's lantern burned all night.
