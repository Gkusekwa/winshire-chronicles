---

He tried to write on the first morning.

At Liss's table. With Liss's pen and a sheet of Liss's paper and Liss's ink in its pot. He dipped the pen. He got it to the paper.

And the pen went across the page in a long shaking line, like the line a glass draws on silk when the floor's moving under it. And off the edge. And out of his fingers onto the floor.

He looked at the line.

Then at his hand. Shaking. On the table.

He didn't pick the pen up.

---

Maren went that morning.

Down the River Road with Liss. To bring the Marches home. Millbrook first, then the river-flats, then the villages up and down the bank. The ferry would run the other way now. The barge with it.

She came to the settle before she went.

"The farmer's coming back across," she said. "To write for you."

He looked at her.

"Why?"

"Because you can't," said Maren.

She looked at his hands. Then she put her own on top of them, briefly, on the blanket, and held them still for a moment. Then she went.

---

Liss left bread on the table.

And the brass lamp. Filled. With a flint beside it. And three sheets of her ferry-book paper, ruled faint in blue.

"For the farmer," she said. "Tell him not to waste them."

---

He had his glasses.

He hadn't been sure. He'd checked in the night, in the dark, with his shaking hands, going through his coat. Four. Wrapped in the brown robe's torn sleeves, from the terrace at Solenne. Through the ship. Through the wyverns and the stair and the cavern and the rock. One cracked, a long hair-line down its throat. Three whole.

He set them on the windowsill.

Reading them wasn't a working. He'd known that all his life, and the College had never let him forget it: *tide-reading is measure, Reader. Not craft.* It was looking. At quicksilver. At which way it leaned and how far up its throat it stood.

He could still look.

---

The farmer came at noon.

On the red dragon. Across the river. He got down on the jetty and came up to the cracked door and stood in it, looking at Idony on the settle.

"She says you need a hand," he said.

"Two," said Idony.

The farmer looked at Idony's hands. He didn't say anything about them. He came in and sat down at Liss's table and pulled the paper toward him and picked up the pen off the floor and looked at it.

"I write slow," he said.

"So do I now," said Idony.

---

He read. The farmer wrote.

Every two hours by day. Through the night too. Idony got up off the settle on Joby Marsh's stick, which was too short, and went to the window, and leaned on the sill with the four glasses in front of him, and read them aloud.

Lean. Angle from the sill's edge, by the scratches he'd made on it with Liss's knife. Height up the throat.

Numbers.

The farmer wrote them down. In columns. Idony told him how to rule them, and he ruled them, with the edge of Liss's bread-board, slowly, his tongue between his teeth. Big square careful figures. The same hand Idony had seen once on the back of a page Maren carried in her coat. Getting smaller to fit.

---

The first day it was simple.

The Titan was on the Scarfell.

North-east. On the Clan's moor. It had come up out of the far end of the cavern, under the Ruin, the night the hill fell, and gone up the valley and onto the high ground and lain down. The glasses leaned toward it, all four, steady.

And from five quarters, something else.

Dips. Small. In the glasses' lean, every reading, shifting. As if the floor of the country were being pressed down in five places a long way off, and the places were moving.

East. North-west. West. South. And the sea.

Toward the Scarfell.

He read them aloud. The farmer wrote them down.

---

"What are they?" said the farmer.

On the first night. By the brass lamp. With the columns going down the page.

"The five," said Idony. "The ones that woke. The stag from the Ring. The Coil from Vael. The Furnace from the Scald. The Horned Sun. The one with the bell on its back." He looked at the glasses. "Coming to it. It called them. Your old one heard it, you said, on the hill." He looked at the farmer. "You felt it."

"I felt it," said the farmer.

"These are the same thing," said Idony. "In numbers."

---

"Why does it matter?" said the farmer.

The second day. In the afternoon. With the second sheet half full.

Not unkindly. He put the pen down and looked at the columns.

"Nobody can do anything with it," he said. "Can they. Nobody can stop them coming. The cage didn't. Nothing will. So why does it matter, writing it down?"

Idony thought about it.

"Somebody has to have measured it," he said. "So that afterwards, when they ask what happened, there's a page with numbers on it. Not a story. Numbers." He looked at the farmer's page. "Something they can't pretend not to understand."

The farmer looked at the page.

"Vey liked a page with numbers on it," he said.

"Who's Vey?"

"A man who was paid for a reef," said the farmer. He picked the pen up again. "Read me the next one."

---

They talked a little, between readings.

The farmer wasn't a talker. Neither was Idony, now. But there were two hours between every reading, and the river going by outside, and the lamp.

The farmer told him about his daughter. Ten. Who made everyone say things twice. Who'd sat by a warm spring in the caves with a yearling with nothing in its eyes and talked to it about barley for nine days until it turned its head.

"About barley," said Idony.

"She thought it was the right thing to talk about," said the farmer. "Because I'd talked to a little red one about barley on a roof once."

Idony told him about Nessa's ferry. The *Heron*. On the lake at Vael. And the bird in the Long Library, old, with one milky eye, that had called him ferryman when it was fond of him and Reader Vael when it wasn't.

"Ferryman," said the farmer.

"Because I came across the lake every day," said Idony. "On the *Heron*. To read."

The farmer thought about it.

"My mother would say that's a good name," he said.

---

He made the farmer say the numbers back.

At the end of the second day. All of them. Down both sheets. The farmer read them out slowly, with his finger, one column after another, the lean and the angle and the height, every two hours, two days.

Then Idony made him say them again.

"Twice," said the farmer.

"Twice," said Idony.

The farmer said them twice.

---

He tested himself in the night.

On the settle. With the lamp out. With the farmer asleep on Liss's kitchen floor by the fire wrapped in a Clan cloak, and the river going past.

The grammar. All of it. From the first page. The disciplines in their order. The runes. The shapes. Every working in the College's book and the unmaking of every one. The roll's appendix, every column, the chain-words, the lock-words. He went through them all in the dark.

All there.

Every stroke. He could see them. As clearly as he'd ever seen them. More clearly. He could see where every working began and where it turned and where the weight went on the closing hooks.

He said *hold*.

To the cup on the floor beside the settle. Quietly. To see.

It was a word.

He lay there in the dark with everything he'd ever known in his head, whole, and his hands shaking on the blanket, and couldn't do one thing with any of it.

---

The farmer went at dawn on the third day.

To the Scarfell. The Clan had gone up there the day after the hill fell, all of them, to their own moor, because that was where the thing had lain down. His daughter was there. His mother.

He left the pages on the table.

Two sheets, full, and the third half full, ruled in blue and columned in his big square hand. Weighted down with Liss's brass lamp.

"I'll come back for them," he said. In the door. "Or Lisska will."

"Yes."

The farmer looked at him.

"Ferryman," he said. And went down the jetty to his dragon.

---

He read them alone after that.

Every two hours. At the window. Aloud. To the empty kitchen, with nobody at the table to write them down.

He remembered them instead.

He'd never needed to. He'd always had silk, and graphite, and ink. He remembered them now. The lean and the angle and the height, every reading, one after another, going down a column in his head that nobody could see.

The five dips came closer.

---

On the fourth day the first of them reached the Scarfell.

He saw it in the glasses. The dip from the east, the stag's, sliding into the Titan's lean and staying there. Two shapes in the quicksilver becoming one place.

Then the north-west.

Then the west, the Furnace, out of its broken cage.

On the fifth day, the south. The Horned Sun. And last, slowest, out of the sea, the one with the bell on its back.

Five. And the one.

---

Then the readings changed.

He was at the window at midnight on the fifth day. He looked at the glasses. And they were moving.

Together.

All four. The quicksilver in each going up its throat and down. Slowly. Up. Held. Down. All four in time, as if one hand were lifting them.

He knew what he was looking at. He'd seen it once before, on a reef, through the farmer's words: a breath. In, held, out.

Not six breaths.

One.

He stood at the window with Joby's stick under his arm and his shaking hands on the sill and watched the four glasses breathe together in the lamplight, and said it aloud. To the kitchen. To nobody.

"All six," he said. "As one."

---

On the sixth day he went to the door at dusk.

Joby Marsh had come up from Millbrook in the afternoon with a better stick. Hazel. Cut and peeled and the right length. He'd stood it in the door and gone again without saying much.

Idony stood in the ferry-house door with it.

And looked north-east.

Up the valley. Past the long grey heap that had been Greyhold. Past the river wall in the river. Over the far bank, the Saltreach bank, the Drover's Way empty now, the fires gone. Up the hills above Harrow's End. To the Scarfell's rim, dark against the evening sky.

---

There were shapes on the horizon.

Six.

Very far off. Small. Rising off the moor's rim into the air, against the last of the light. Together. Slowly. As a flock rises off a field when something has startled it, all at once, every one.

He couldn't see what they were.

He didn't need to. He'd measured them. He had every number in his head.

They went up off the Scarfell together, six, against the evening, and turned in the air.

East.

Toward the Worldsend.

He stood in the door and watched them go until there was nothing on the horizon but the dark.

Down at the jetty's end a small figure stood with her back to him and her hand in a lion's mane, looking the same way. He didn't call to her.

Then he went in and sat down at Liss's table with the farmer's pages under the lamp, and put his shaking hand flat on them, and said the last reading aloud, through the open door, so somebody would have heard it.
