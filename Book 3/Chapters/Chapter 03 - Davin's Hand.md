---

The boy came down the King's Road on a mule.

Idony saw him from the window. He'd been at the window since dawn with his four glasses on the sill, reading them every two hours and saying the numbers aloud to the empty kitchen, and remembering them, because there was nobody to write them. A mule. Grey, with a crown-blue saddle-cloth gone brown. And a boy on it with his knees up round its neck and a satchel on his back, coming down the last of the road from the downs to where the jetty road turned off for the river.

He stopped the mule in the road and looked at the fallen hill for a long time.

Then at the ferry house. At the charter stone leaning against its wall. At the lion asleep across the jetty road.

He got down and led the mule round the lion, very carefully, and knocked on the cracked door.

---

"Davin," said Liss. Opening it. "Millbrook. The Kettle's boy."

"Not any more," said the boy. "I'm the tribunes' clerk. In Hallowmere." He held up the satchel. "I've a letter for your girl. From the tribunes. They want her to come."

"Come where?"

"To Hallowmere," said Davin. "To tell the city what's coming. The council won't." He looked past Liss into the kitchen. At the table. At the envoys round it, four of them, with their cups. At Idony at the window. "Is this where everyone is?"

"Everyone who's left," said Liss.

---

He couldn't hold a pen.

That was what the boy saw first. Idony watched him see it. He'd come in and been given tea and sat down on the bench, and Idony had got up off the stool by the window and come to the table and picked up Liss's pen to write his own name for him, as a courtesy, and the pen had gone across the paper in a long shaking line and dropped.

The boy looked at the line.

Then at Idony's hands. Grey. Shaking on the table as if they were very cold.

"I write," said Davin. "For the tribunes. Every day. I write a round hand." He picked up the pen. "What do you want written?"

---

The envoys were still arguing.

They'd been arguing for a fortnight. Since the table, the day after the hill fell. Nobody had left. Nobody had agreed to anything. They sat at Liss's table every morning with their cups, and argued, and in the afternoon they walked down to the jetty and looked at the river, and in the evening they came back and argued again.

Liss made tea.

She'd stopped asking them if they wanted it. She just put it down.

---

"The First Guildmaster calls every kingdom to Ironhythe," said Tolley.

He'd said it every morning for six days. The square red-bearded Kessmoor man had had it by semaphore down the Iron Road, tower to tower, and had brought the paper across on Liss's boat, and he took it out every morning and read it again, as if it might have changed in the night.

"To the Hall of Measures," he said. "When the six come down."

"When," said Corrow.

"When," said Tolley. "They're not staying up there. Your own reader says so." He looked at Idony at the window. "Don't you?"

"I say they're drinking," said Idony. "When they've drunk enough they'll come down. I can't tell you when."

"Then when you can," said Tolley, "the First Guildmaster would like to know. Before anyone else."

---

Corrow wouldn't go without the College's leave.

The thin anxious Blue magister had written to Vael four times. By lantern owl. The owls had gone up the valley north and not come back.

"Nine days," he said. "No owl. Not one." He looked at his cup. "Owls don't get lost."

"Then something's happened at Vael," said Rowan.

"Something's always happening at Vael," said Corrow. "That's what Vael is for." He didn't look up. "The owls come back. They always come back."

---

Perrin Sade said Solenne's low city would send whoever could walk.

To Ironhythe or wherever. He said it once, on the first morning, and didn't say it again. He sat on the step in the cracked doorway with his back to the frame and listened to the others, and in the afternoons he went down to the jetty with Liss and helped her with the rope.

Rowan said the crown's council hadn't answered her in a month.

"I write," she said. "Every week. To Hallowmere. The council thanks me." She turned her cup round on the table. "Then it sends more paper."

Davin looked up from his writing.

"The council's got no paper left," he said. "The tribunes have it. It's in the corn exchange."

---

He read the glasses at noon.

At the window. The boy beside him with Liss's ferry-book open on the sill and the pen. Four glasses, standing in their wooden feet in a row. One cracked, a hair-line down its throat. Three whole. He'd carried them in the torn sleeves of a hand's brown robe from the palace terrace at Solenne through a ship and a wyvern's harness and a stair and a cavern and a hill.

He bent and looked at them.

"East," he said. "All four. Lean, the first. Two and a quarter marks past the sill's line. The second, two and a half. The third..."

He said them slowly. The boy wrote them down. In a round clear clerk's hand, quickly, without being told twice.

---

They leaned east.

All four. Hard. Steadily. He'd read them every two hours for six days and they'd leaned east every time, harder every time, toward the Worldsend, toward the lights on the rim of the sky that came up every night at midnight like a second dawn.

And they breathed.

He watched them. All four at once. The quicksilver in each going a little up its throat, and holding, and a little down. Together. In time. As one breath. He'd seen it at the end of the year from the ferry-house door, the night before the six went up. Six breathing as one.

It was slower now.

In. Held. Out. And the in-breath longer every day. He'd timed it on day one at the length of a slow count to forty. It was sixty now.

"In," he said. To the boy. "Sixty. Held, twenty. Out, thirty."

The boy wrote it.

"What's breathing?" said Davin.

---

He tried to tell him.

He'd never been good at telling anyone anything. He'd told it to the bird, in the Long Library, and the bird had known it already. He'd told it to Maren on a dozen shores and she'd listened with her hand on the lion and asked one question, always the right one.

The boy listened too. With the pen in his hand.

"Six of them," said Idony. "Lying together in the mountains. Drinking. Not water. The Deep." He looked at the glasses. "It's in the ground. Under everything. It comes up like water comes up in a spring. They're drawing it. Out of the east. Out of the mountains and the moor and the Wyldmarch. All of it toward them." He thought. "Like the tide going out."

"Before a wave," said Davin.

Idony looked at him.

"In Millbrook," said the boy. "The old men say. Before the big flood, the river went down. All in a morning. Right down. You could walk across to the Staithe on the stones." He looked at the glasses. "Then it came back."

"Yes," said Idony. "Write that down."

---

The west was flat.

He read that too. His western readings. Hearthmere. The Marches. Under the ferry house, under the fallen hill, under the river where the held breath had been. Nothing. The glasses leaned east and didn't know there was a west at all. There was nothing in the west to lean toward.

"Flat," he said. "Write flat."

"How flat?"

"Flat," said Idony. "As a table. As that table. Write it like that."

The boy wrote it like that.

---

He found the margin on the seventh day.

He hadn't been looking for it. He'd been looking for something to compare the breath with. Something old. Anything that had been measured before. He had Davin cut the stitches of his winter coat, where the silk was sewn into the lining, and take it out, and spread it on Liss's table in the morning when the envoys had gone down to the jetty, and weight the corners with cups.

Eleven years of readings. His own. In graphite on oiled silk.

And at the beginning, before any of his own, a copy.

---

He'd made it at twenty.

His first year in the Gallery of Glasses. Every new reader made one. The Gallery's oldest chart, the founding chart of the College of Tides, two hundred and some years old: the six kingdoms drawn small in the middle of a great empty sheet, and the first readers' marks across them, the ones that had begun the measuring. Every reader copied it. It was how you learned the hand.

He'd copied it carefully. He'd been very proud of it.

"There," he said. To the boy. "The edge. South-west. Out past the coast."

Davin bent over the silk.

---

A mark.

At the very edge of the copy. Out past the Saltreach shore, where the first readers had drawn the sea as wavy lines and stopped. Over the Shattered Sea. A small steady rise. No breath in it. No rhythm. The first readers had measured it, faintly, from as far as their glasses could reach, and written it down.

And beside it, in the margin, a word.

*Error.*

In five hands. One under the other. Five readers, across two hundred years, who'd copied the chart and come to the mark and written the same word beside it in their own hand, because the readers before them had. And under the fifth, a sixth hand. Smaller. Neat. Twenty years old and very proud of itself.

His.

*Error.*

---

"What does it mean?" said Davin.

Idony looked at it for a long time.

"It means nobody understood it," he said. "So they said it was wrong."

"Is it?"

"I don't know." He put his shaking finger down on the silk. Beside the mark. "Every sleeper breathes. I've measured five of them. Every one breathes. In, held, out. Like a sleeper. That's how I knew they were sleeping." He looked at the mark. "This doesn't breathe. It rises, and it stays. Steady. For two hundred years."

"Like what?"

He thought about it.

"Like a lamp," he said. "Left burning. In an empty house."

---

He had Davin write down the rumours beside it.

Two of them.

The first he'd written himself, months ago, in graphite, at a fire on the moor outside the Scald, from an old Gearmouth engine-wright called Abner Quayle who was deaf in one ear and turned his head to listen like a bird. A ship called the *Margery*, out of the Western Deep, back with half its crew, and the rest saying they'd seen a mountain walk on a far shore.

*Sailors' talk,* Quayle had said. *They'd been at sea too long.*

He'd written it down anyway.

The second came on the eighth day.

---

A salt-boatman.

Up from Wendmouth on the tide with a flat barge of salt. He'd tied up at the Staithe and come across on Liss's rope for the Hearthmere market, and found the market gone and the hill fallen in and a lion asleep across the road, and he'd stood on the jetty and talked to anyone who'd listen, as boatmen do.

Idony listened.

"Far-stone," the boatman was saying. To Liss. Laughing. "That's what they call it on Gallows Quay. Grey stuff. Old. Flakes off a block, so big." He showed a thumbnail. "A Blackwater salvage house brings it in. Off some wreck out on the Shatters, they say. Sells it to the charm-sellers, and the charm-sellers sell it on, warranted, as old charms." He grinned. "Two silver a flake."

"And?" said Liss.

"And the joke is," said the boatman, "one in ten of them works."

---

Idony had him say it again.

The boatman did. Pleased to be asked. Grey stone. Old. Off a wreck on the Shatters. One in ten works. He didn't know which house. A small one. In debt to everybody.

"Write it down," said Idony. To Davin. "Beside the margin. *Blackwater. Far-stone. Off the Shatters. One in ten.*"

Davin wrote it down.

The boatman looked at the grey man at the window with his shaking hands, and the boy writing for him, and the lion in the road. He stopped grinning.

"Is it important?" he said.

"I don't know," said Idony. "Probably not." He looked at the silk. "Everything is, eventually."

---

Maren was at the table that night.

She'd been out all day. On the River Road with the knights, Liss said, carrying the last of Penfold home. She came in after dark with mud to her knees and sat at the end of the table by the fire, and Liss put a cup of tea in front of her, and she picked it up with her left hand.

He noticed because of his own.

His hands were on the table, shaking. They always shook. He'd stopped seeing it. He saw hers.

Her left hand round the cup. The fingertips grey. Not dirty. Grey, as the old stone of the hill was grey, from the nails to the first knuckle, the colour going out of them toward the palm. And her fingers round the cup so hard the knuckles stood up white under the grey.

It didn't shake.

He watched it for a long time. It didn't shake at all.

Because she was holding it so hard, he thought. So hard nothing could.

---

She saw him looking.

She didn't put the cup down. She didn't hide the hand. She looked at him across the table, at his hands on the wood, and then back at her own.

"The lintel," she said. "At the archive."

"Does it hurt?"

"No," said Maren. "Nothing. That's the trouble." She drank. "It comes back by morning. Less, every time."

"Every time?"

She didn't answer that.

---

He dictated the chart's first line on the eighth night.

Not the readings. The line at the top. The one every chart had: what it was a chart of. He'd made hundreds. In Vael they'd had a form for it, a set phrase, a date and a reader's ring.

He didn't use the form.

"Write this," he said.

The boy dipped the pen.

"*The east is being drunk,*" said Idony. "*The west has been. Something in the south-west has never been drunk at all.*"

Davin wrote it.

He read it back. Slowly. Loud. In the voice he used for the tribunes on the corn exchange steps. Then he read it again, without being asked.

Idony looked at him.

"Twice," he said.

"The farmer's girl does that," said Davin. "Liss told me. I thought it was a good idea."

"It is," said Idony.

He sat at the window with the four glasses on the sill breathing east, all together, slowly, and his grey hands in his lap, and listened to the boy read it a third time, to himself, under his breath, to get it right.
