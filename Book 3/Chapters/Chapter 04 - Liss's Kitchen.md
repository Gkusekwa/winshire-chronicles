---

The reader had the silk out on Liss's table.

Spread flat, with cups on the corners and the boy from Millbrook holding the edges down. The six kingdoms small in the middle, and at the south-west edge, past the Saltreach coast, a mark, with *error* beside it in six hands. The last of them his.

"I said to Davin it was like a lamp," said Idony. He looked at the mark. "It isn't."

He was quiet. Then, slowly, as if he were reading it off something: "Light. Something that only gives. That doesn't drink." He looked at Maren. "Everything we've measured for a year drinks. The Titan drank. The six are drinking now. This doesn't. It's the only thing on the chart that doesn't."

---

Orrun was in the doorway.

Lying across it. Too big for the kitchen. His head through the cracked door and on the floor by the settle, his body out in the road in the dark. He'd been asleep, she thought. His eyes were half shut.

He opened them.

*Light,* he said.

---

She felt it before he said anything else.

Through the bond. In the place behind her eyes. A memory. Coming up in him from a long way down, slowly, as a stone comes up out of the river-bed in a flood, rolled over and over.

A river, wider than this one. Brown. With no rope across it, and no ferry, and no hill on the far side.

A door. In the rock. Plain. Grey.

And beside it, people. Tall. Taller than people. Not people. Standing at the door in a ring. Looking at him. A young lion, very young, with his mane not grown. Lying in front of the door.

Then going away.

---

*They didn't die,* said Orrun.

Maren didn't move.

*The ones who set me at the door. When I was young. When the river was a different river.* His eyes were on the silk, on the table, on the mark at its edge, but she didn't think he saw it. *They put me there and told me what to keep. And then they went.*

"Where?"

*Into the light,* said Orrun.

---

Idony had gone very still.

He sat on the bench with his shaking hands on the table and didn't move at all. The boy looked from him to the lion and back.

*West,* said Orrun. *I watched them go. From the door. I could see a long way then. They went west along the river and over the hills, toward where the sun goes down. Over the sea.* He was quiet. *Into the light. That is what they said. We are going into the light. Keep the door.*

Maren looked at the mark on the silk.

South-west. Over the sea.

"That's a song," she said.

She heard her own voice say it. Small, in Liss's kitchen. Every child in the Marches knew it. Ada sang it. Liss had sung it to her over this table when she was small enough to sit under it. *The old ones went up into the light, and the one fell into six.*

*Yes,* said Orrun. *Somebody made a song of it.*

---

The kitchen was quiet.

Liss was at the fire. She'd stopped stirring the pot. She stood with the spoon in her hand and looked at the lion's head on her kitchen floor.

"Into the light," she said. "My mother sang that."

"So did mine," said Davin.

Idony was looking at the mark.

"Write it down," he said. To the boy. His voice was very quiet. "Beside the margin. Under the far-stone. *The lion says the ones who set him at the door went into the light. West. Over the sea.*" He shut his eyes. "Write *the lion says*. Exactly that."

Davin wrote it.

---

The lights didn't come that night.

She was on the jetty at midnight. She'd been on the jetty at midnight every night since the six went up, watching the east. The glow on the rim of the world. Breathing. She waited.

It didn't come.

The east stayed dark. Stars, down to the line of the Scarfell. Nothing past it.

She stood there until her feet were cold and went in.

---

Idony was at the window at dawn.

On his stool. With the four glasses on the sill. She came into the kitchen and saw his face and stopped.

"They've come down," he said.

He didn't look round. He was looking at the glasses.

"The lean's moved. East-south-east. All four." He bent closer. "And it's broken. The breath. It's not one any more." His shaking finger hovered over the glasses without touching them. "Six. Six breaths. Out of time with each other. Like six people sleeping in one room."

"Where?"

"Lower than the mountains," said Idony. "Closer. Out of the high ground, onto the flat." He sat back. "The Plains. The Hallow Plains. They've come down onto the Plains."

---

The rider came at noon.

To the Staithe. On the far bank. A grey mare, lathered to the hocks, with a rider on her who was nearly as grey: dust from the boots to the hair. A Wyldmarch horse-clansman. She knew the saddle. She'd seen saddles like it at the Moot, a year ago, on the Hallow Plains, when the plains had still been green.

Liss went across for him.

He came up the jetty on foot with the mare left at the Staithe, and he looked at the lion in the road, and went down on one knee in front of him, the clans' way. Then he got up and looked at Maren.

"You're the lion-woman," he said. "The Keepers said. From the Moot." He was breathing hard. "Elder Tova sent me. Of the horse clans. To find you."

---

"She sent me when the lights came," he said.

At Liss's table. With tea he didn't drink. His hands round the cup.

"Over the mountains. At the Worldsend's foot, where the Plains start. Every night. We watched from the high pastures." He looked at the cup. "The grass on the high pastures goes black under it. Not fire. Black. And the stones." He looked up. "The standing stones on the Plains. The old ones. They crack. You can hear them from a league off. Like ice."

"The clans?"

"Getting ready to run," said the rider. "When they come down. West. Every clan. The horse clans and the stonehorn clans and the Keepers out of what's left of the Thornwood. With the herds. Ten thousand on foot. More. Children. The old. Every stonehorn on the Plains, every mare."

"They've come down," said Maren. "At dawn."

The rider looked at her. Then at the grey man at the window with his glasses. He put the cup down very carefully.

"Then they're running," he said.

"West where?"

"Here," said the rider. "Hearthmere. Over the Wend." He looked at Maren. "There's nowhere else. Everything east of the Wyldmarch is mountains. Everything north is the High Crown. Everything south is dead from the drain. A hundred leagues of it. West there's the scrub and the moor, and nothing on them. The only land past it that'll feed a herd is yours."

He looked at the fallen hill through the window.

"Or was," he said.

---

"And the border's shut," he said.

"Shut?"

"Knights on the road," said the rider. "Crown men. At the ford on the Greenwold road, up the river. We sent riders ahead to ask. I came with them. They turned us back." He looked at Rowan. Across the table, in her white surcoat. "By knights. With hounds. They said the council's order. No clans. No herds."

Rowan didn't say anything.

She put her hand inside her surcoat. And took out a paper. Folded. Sealed. A rider had brought it from Hallowmere that morning, the first answer the council had sent her in a month, and she'd read it on the jetty and folded it very small.

She unfolded it on the table.

---

Maren read it.

Rowan pushed it across to her, and she read it herself. In the council's hand. With the crown's seal at the foot, in blue wax.

*The border of the kingdom is closed to the clans of the Wyldmarch and their beasts by order of the crown's council. No herds, no beast-folk, no Keepers shall be admitted. The knights of the Greenwold road shall turn them back.*

She read it to the end, and then the seal.

"*The clans and their beasts,*" she said.

"Yes," said Rowan.

"Running from six Titans."

"Yes."

"And the council's turning them back."

"The council doesn't believe in Titans," said Rowan. "The council believes in grain. There isn't any." She folded the paper again. "Ten thousand clansfolk and their stonehorns would eat the Candle Downs to the root in a month. That's what the council believes."

---

She wrote to the tribunes that night.

At Liss's table. Davin's letter was there. From the street tribunes of Hallowmere: *Come and tell the city what's coming. The council won't.* It had been on the table three days. She hadn't answered it.

She answered it now. In her own square hand. Davin watched.

*I can't come. I'm going east.*

*Tell the city the truth. Something is coming, and the council has shut the door on the people running from it.*

*Maren Ashdown.*

She blotted it and gave it to him.

"Will they read it out?" she said.

"I'll read it out," said Davin. "On the corn exchange steps. Twice."

---

She told Rowan in the morning.

On the jetty road. In front of the knights, because the knights were there, forty of them, and she didn't see why she should do it behind their backs. Orrun behind her, awake, with his head up.

"I'm going east," she said. "To bring them out."

Rowan looked at her.

"Through the council's border," said Rowan.

"Through it."

"No."

---

She said it in front of the knights.

"I forbid it," said Rowan. "As the senior knight at the river. Under the council's order. No knight of this school goes to the border."

"I'm not a knight."

"You were a student of this school."

"There's no school," said Maren. She looked at the hill. "There's no hill."

Rowan didn't look at the hill.

"If you go through the council's border," she said, "with ten thousand clansfolk and every stonehorn on the Plains behind you, you'll be outlawed. By name. With a price on you. You and the lion."

"Then I'll be outlawed."

"I know you will," said Rowan. "I'll be the one who has to sign it."

---

Then Rowan turned her back.

On the jetty road. In front of the knights. As she'd turned her back on the River Road a year ago, the morning the farmer walked down it with his three dragons. Maren saw the knights see it. Wynn, with his hounds. The woman with the falcon. Forty faces.

Maren stood and looked at her back.

She didn't say anything.

She'd turned to go, Orrun turning with her, when Rowan said it. Without turning round.

"The Greenwold road."

Maren stopped.

"That's where the knights are," said Rowan. To the river. "The council's. With hounds. The Greenwold road's the only road from the Plains that'll take a herd." She was quiet. "Don't go by the Greenwold."

Then she turned round again, and looked at Maren, and walked past her up the jetty road toward the fallen hill, and didn't look back.

---

Wynn caught her at the ferry-house door.

With his two grey hounds. Ash and Sorrow. Standing either side of him with their heads level with his belt.

"I'll come," he said.

"No."

"The hounds are good with herds. You saw. At the landing."

"I saw," said Maren. "That's why. Stay here. There's people here. They'll need herding too." She looked at the hounds. "Rowan will need you."

Wynn looked at her for a while.

"She will," he said.

---

Liss put bread in her pack.

And cheese. And a twist of salt. And the brass lamp.

Maren took the lamp out again and put it on the table.

"I'll not need it," she said. "There's no dark on the Plains. They're burning."

Liss looked at the lamp. She didn't put it back in.

"Come home after," she said.

"I always do."

"You don't," said Liss. "You always come home before. Before the next thing." She did the pack's buckles up herself, with her big rough hands, too tight. "After's different. Come home after."

---

She went east at noon.

Down the jetty to the rope. Liss took them over herself, the lion lying flat in the middle of the boat with the water an inch from the gunwale, and kept her eyes on the rope the whole way. At the Staithe Maren got off, and Orrun after her, and the boat went back.

Then up through the huts and the drying racks, onto the old drove track that ran east over the Marches hills, away from the river, toward the Plains.

Orrun walked beside her.

She didn't look back until dusk.

---

At dusk, on the top of the hills, she stopped.

She didn't know why. Orrun stopped with her. She turned round.

Below her, the valley of the Wend. Grey in the last light. The river a darker line in it, going south. The fallen hill across it, small, a lump. The ferry house, too small to see. The Hearthmere bank beyond, and the downs going away west, and the Scarfell's long dark rim away to the north.

And over the river, fire.

---

Dragon-fire. Gold. Short. A line of it, low over the Wend, far up the valley in the north. Then another below it. Then another. One after another, coming down the river, low over the water, going south.

Breath. Dragons breathing as they flew, in the dusk, to light the way.

She counted them.

Twenty. Thirty. More. Coming down out of the north past the fallen hill and the ferry house and on south down the river toward Millbrook, toward Cotter's Cross, toward the coast.

She knew whose.

---

*The farmer,* said Orrun. Beside her. Watching it. *And the Clan.*

"Going south."

*To his sea,* he said.

She watched the fire go down the river until it was past Millbrook and too small to see.

Then she turned round, east, toward the Plains, where the sky on the far rim was a dull red that wasn't the sunset, and walked on into the dark.
