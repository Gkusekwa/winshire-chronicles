---

The chair was under its cloth.

It had been under its cloth since the night they burned Teo. A grey cloth, plain wool, the kind the women of the hold wove for horse-blankets, thrown over it on Arn's word by somebody whose name nobody remembered, and nobody had taken it off.

Black oak. Arn knew what was under it without looking. He'd sat on it as a boy, when his father wasn't in the hall, and been beaten for it. Dragons carved up the legs. Dragons on the arms, with their heads at the ends where a chief's hands went. A dragon across the back with its wings out, worn smooth in the middle where three hundred years of chiefs had leaned.

It stood at the top of the hall, on the dais, in the gap between the two high tables.

He sat on the bench beside it. Where he always sat. Where he'd sat for a year.

Eight days since the six went up off the Scarfell. Below him Varrhold's riders sat at the long tables and waited to hear what the farmer had said about the Clan's dragons.

---

The two high tables stood side by side on the dais, and the benches of the hold ran down the hall below them to the door.

The red thread's on the left. The grey's on the right. They'd always run that way, since before his grandfather, since before anyone kept count. In his father's time the two tables had eaten together and drunk together and married each other's daughters and fought each other at the autumn games, and sat under one chair.

Then his father died.

Then the tables had stopped eating together. Then Teo.

Then the war, and the farmer, and the smoke on the Roosts, and two pours at the black water, one hand each, him and Ulric. And the tables ate together again. And still nobody sat in the chair.

A chief was named when both halves poured at Coldtarn. Everyone knew that. They'd poured. Twice.

Nobody had said a name.

---

"The farmer says they're not his to give," said Arn.

He said it to the hall. On the eighth day, at the evening meal, with both tables full. The riders of the ledges, red and grey, fifty of them on the benches, and the old ones at the ends, and the young ones on the steps at the back because there wasn't room.

"The three," he said. "He says they're not his. He says ask them."

"And?" said a red-thread rider. Bjarke, from the high ledges. Young. Loud.

"And the old one says she's tired," said Arn. "She says she was the Clan's for three hundred years and three partners, and she'll fly where the farmer goes, and we can put our thread on the red one if we can catch him."

---

Nobody on the red side laughed.

He'd known they wouldn't. He watched them not laugh. Bjarke looking at his cup. The others looking at Bjarke.

At the end of the grey table, old Hulda Sarr laughed. A short bark. Like a fox. She put her hand over her mouth and laughed into it until her eyes ran.

"If we can catch him," she said. "Oh, that's the old one. That's her to the bone."

Ulric was grinning. On the grey bench. Across from Arn.

Arn didn't grin.

---

Gorm Askell brought the ledgers in the morning.

Of course he did. Arn's steward. Long and sour and ink to the elbow, limping since the ford. He came into the hall with three books under his arm and put them on the table in front of Arn and opened the first one to a page he'd marked with a strip of leather.

"Aurveth," he said. "Bred on the high ledges. In Rurik's grandfather's time. Fed by the Clan, harnessed by the Clan, bonded three times to riders of the Clan." He turned to another strip. "Kharrak. Bred in the spring pens. Your father's time. Mutton from the Clan's flocks, eleven years." He turned again. "Lisska. The pens. Imra Sallow's." He looked at Arn. "On the books. All three."

"I know what's on the books."

"I'm saying they're on them," said Gorm Askell. "The riders know it. The riders want it said."

---

Arn looked at the page.

At the name in Gorm's cramped hand. *Imra Sallow. The green, from the spring pens. Bonded in her fifteenth year.* The Clan's best scout. Dead at the mill, in the autumn his father died, beside Sigrun and Dag, the three of them breaking the old law to fight kin.

He shut the book.

"Then say it," he said. "Say it to the farmer. Say it to the green one. See what she says."

Gorm Askell looked at him for a while.

"She'd bite me," he said.

"She would," said Arn.

---

He walked the pens in the morning.

He always did. His father always had. Every morning, down the stair from the hall to the lower ledges, along the rail, past every pen. The spring pens, with their oak bars. The keepers with their barrows of mutton. A hundred dragons and more. The Clan's.

Bred here. Fed here. Kept.

Not bonded. Most of them never would be. A dragon chose, or it didn't, and most didn't. The Clan kept them anyway. For the flights. For the harness. For the eggs. For the count in Gorm's books that said how strong the Clan was.

He walked the rail.

---

They were silent.

All of them. Every dragon in every pen. He stopped at the first pen and listened and there was nothing. No grumbling. No hiss of a young one at the keeper's barrow. No sound at all.

They were standing. Every one. Facing east.

Toward the Plains. Their heads up and still.

He went along the whole rail and they were all the same. A hundred dragons in their pens, standing, facing east, silent, and the keepers with their barrows standing in the passage between, not knowing whether to put the mutton down.

"Since dawn," said the old keeper at the end. Leir. "They stopped humming at dawn. All at once. Like a door shut."

---

The rider came from the edge at noon.

From the eastern watch. Where the Scarfell broke off into the scrub above the Plains. A young grey-thread rider, out of breath, with her dragon blowing on the hall steps behind her.

"They've come down," she said. "Off the mountains. Onto the Plains. All six. At the foot of the Worldsend." She was breathing hard. "There's smoke. All along the eastern rim. Black. You can see it from the edge."

Arn looked east. From the hall steps. He couldn't see anything from here. The moor. The sky.

"The Plains are burning," said the rider.

---

Wren was in the yard.

With the farmer's mother. They'd come down from the caves that morning on the old grey beast, to see the farmer off, the farmer's mother on its back and the girl walking beside it. And with them, behind them, the young dragon. Ildra. The one from Vael. The one the girl had talked back into her own head.

He'd seen her come into the yard.

He'd seen her stop. And look round. And see him on the hall steps. And leave the girl and the grey beast and walk across the yard toward him.

---

She stood a length off. Behind him.

He went down the steps. Into the yard. She came after him. A length behind. He went along the yard to the stair to the pens. She came after him. He went down the stair. She came down the stair, carefully, her claws on the old stone, a length behind.

He stopped at the first pen.

He turned round.

"Go back to the girl," he said.

---

She didn't go.

She looked at him. Pale-bellied. Taller than a grey beast. With somebody in her eyes. He'd seen her eyes in the caves in the autumn with nothing in them, standing by the spring with the black and the red, a binder's emptiness. There was somebody in them now.

Looking at him.

"Go on," he said. "Back. Go."

He pointed up the stair.

She went up the stair. Slowly. He watched her go up, and turn at the top, and stand there looking down at him.

---

He sent her back four times.

Along the rail, past the pens. To the end and back. Every time he turned round she was there. A length behind. Every time he told her to go back to the girl, and every time she went up the stair, and every time she came down it again when he wasn't looking.

The fifth time the girl came down after her.

Wren. Ten. Thin, with her hair in a plait and her father's chin. She came down the stair to the pens on her own, and walked along the rail between the silent dragons as if she did it every day, and took hold of Ildra by the horn, by the little stub of horn over her eye, as you'd take a calf by the ear.

"She likes you," said Wren.

---

"She's not mine," said Arn.

Wren looked at him. Then at the dragon. Then back at him.

"That's what I say," she said.

She led Ildra back up the stair by the horn. Ildra went. At the top, she turned her head and looked back down at him over the girl's shoulder.

He turned round and looked at the pens. At a hundred dragons standing silent, facing east.

His hands were shaking. He put them on the rail.

---

Gunna Tarne was on the rail at the far end.

Sitting on it. With her clay pipe. She'd come down from the ledges on Tamsin and sat there all morning, watching him walk the pens with a young dragon a length behind him, and said nothing.

Half her face was scar. From the Roosts. From the burning resin, the night of the smoke, when she'd gone down onto the Choosing Ledge on Tamsin for the young ones. Her arm was stiff from the shoulder. Tamsin lay on the ledge above her with her wing folded, the trailing edge black and thin where the fire had taken it.

She took the pipe out of her mouth.

"Five times," she said.

"I know."

"That's not a dragon that's lost," said Gunna. "That's a dragon that's found something."

"She's the girl's."

"She's nobody's," said Gunna. "Yet." She put the pipe back in her mouth. "You've walked these pens every morning for a year, Arn Varr, and not one of these has ever followed you a length." She looked at the pens. "Not one."

---

The farmer was in the yard in the afternoon.

Below the hall. With his three. The red one lying in the middle of the yard where the carts turned, taking all of it. The green one on the wall. The old one by the gate, too big for the yard, her head on the cobbles and her tail out past the gatehouse into the lane.

And the farmer standing among them. Talking.

Out loud.

---

Arn heard him from the hall steps.

"I know you don't want to," said the farmer. To the red one. "I'm not asking you to want to. I'm asking you to go."

The red one said something. Arn couldn't hear it. Nobody could. The farmer could.

"Because they're on the road," said the farmer. "With nothing. Because I flew them off their roofs with nothing, and now the farmers have put them off the land, and somebody's nailed my name on every toll gate on the coast." He listened. "No. No, you can't burn the toll gates. Because that's exactly what they want."

The young riders were on the steps.

Bjarke and three others. Sitting on the hall steps in the sun with their cups. Watching a man stand in a yard and argue with a dragon as if it were his brother.

Bjarke laughed.

---

"Eat," said the farmer. To the green one. On the wall. "You're thin. You've been thin since the reef. I don't care if you're not hungry. Eat the sheep."

The others laughed. All four of them on the steps. A barley farmer telling a dragon to eat its sheep.

"How far?" said the farmer. To the old one. By the gate. He waited. "That's not far enough. To the coast is further than that." He waited. "I know you did it before. You were younger before." He waited again, and then he laughed himself, suddenly, and put his hand on the old one's head. "All right. All right. I'll tell her."

The riders on the steps laughed harder.

---

Then the red one lifted its head.

In the middle of the yard. Slowly. And turned it, and looked at the steps. At the four young riders with their cups.

The green one on the wall lifted hers. And turned it. And looked.

The old one by the gate opened one gold eye the size of a cartwheel's hub. And looked.

All three. At once. At the steps.

Nobody on the steps laughed after that.

Bjarke put his cup down. Very carefully. On the step beside him. As if it might break.

The farmer looked round at the steps, puzzled. He hadn't seen. He'd been talking to the old one.

"What?" he said.

Nobody said anything.

---

He asked for the flights that night.

At the chair. At the high tables, after the meal, with both tables there and the young riders back on the steps very quiet. The farmer stood at the foot of the hall, between the two tables, where a petitioner stood. He'd never sat at either table. He'd never asked to.

He asked plainly.

Gullhaven's people on the Coast Road with nothing. A thousand. More. Walking west to Wendmouth. The Council's contract on his three, nailed on every gate. And the one with the bell on its back, lying in the light in the mountains, turned with its head toward its sea.

"When they come down off the Plains," said the farmer, "it'll go home. To the coast. To my coast." He looked up the hall at the chair. "I need the flights. Half. I'll not ask for all."

---

"The Clan's place is the Scarfell," said Arn.

He said it from the bench beside the chair. Quietly. He'd known what he'd say since the rider came in from the edge at noon with the smoke.

"There are six of them on the Plains. Not a hundred leagues off. The Clan's families are on this moor. The caves are full of children. Your own." He looked down the hall at the farmer. "The Clan holds the Scarfell. The coast's the Council's. Let the Council keep its own coast."

"The Council won't," said the farmer. "You were there. You saw."

"I saw," said Arn.

---

"Teo would have gone," said Ulric.

From the grey table. Not loudly. He didn't look at Arn when he said it. He looked at his cup.

"Teo would have been off the ledge before the farmer finished asking," said Ulric. "And you know it."

---

He did know it.

That was the trouble. He knew it as surely as he knew what was under the cloth without lifting it. Teo would have gone. Teo would have laughed at the riders on the steps and gone down into the yard and put his arm round the farmer's neck and said *Well, what are we waiting for,* and gone. Teo had been like that at sixteen, throwing apples at a green dragon nobody else dared go near. Teo had been like that at the ford.

Teo was ash in the black water.

And Arn was sitting on a bench beside an empty chair, saying the Clan's place was the Scarfell.

It galled him. It sat in his throat like a bone.

---

"Half," he said.

He heard his own voice say it.

"Half the flights. Red and grey. To the coast with the farmer." He looked at Ulric. "You take them. You're the one who wants to go."

Ulric looked at him across the hall.

"I'll take them," he said.

"Forty pairs," said Arn. "Not one more. The rest stay. The ledges stay, and the pens, and the caves." He looked at the farmer. "I hold the Scarfell."

The farmer looked up the hall at him. At the bench. At the chair beside it under its cloth.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't," said Arn.

---

They went at dusk on the tenth day.

Off the Roosts. Forty pairs, red thread and grey, mixed, as they'd flown at Gullhaven. The farmer's three among them, the old one slow at the back with the red one off her crooked side. Ulric at the front behind Sigge Brack on Sigge's grey, with the white scar through his eyebrow, not looking back.

Arn stood on the high ledge and watched them go.

Down off the Scarfell into the valley of the Wend. South. Low over the river. Breathing as they went, fire going out ahead of them in the dusk to light the way, gold, short, one after another, down the river toward Millbrook and the coast.

He watched until the last of the fire was too small to see.

---

He went back into the hall.

It was empty. The tables cleared. The fire low. The chair under its cloth at the top of the hall between the two tables.

He sat down on the bench beside it.

He didn't know how long he sat there. Long enough for the fire to go to embers.

When he looked up, Ildra was in the doorway.

---

At the far end of the hall. In the porch. Not coming in. Standing with her head through the door and the rest of her out in the dark, looking down the length of the empty hall at him.

He didn't send her away.

He didn't call her either.

He sat on the bench beside his father's chair and looked at her down the hall, and she looked back, and neither of them moved, until the fire was out.

---

On the eleventh day the riders on the edge sent word that the smoke in the east was thicker.

On the twelfth, that it was moving. West. Slowly. Toward the border.

Arn doubled the watch on the edge. He sent riders to the caves to tell Bel Fenn to be ready to move the children, and Bel Fenn sent back to say she'd been ready since the autumn, thank you, and where would he like them moved to.

He didn't have an answer.

Ildra slept in the hall's porch that night. And the next. He stepped over her tail going in and out, and didn't say anything about it, and nobody else did either.
