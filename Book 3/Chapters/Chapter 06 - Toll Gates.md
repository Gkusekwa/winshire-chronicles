---

The Coast Road ran along the edge of everything.

Along the top of the shingle, mostly, where the sea had piled it. Over the creeks on bridges or on fords at low water. Through the fishing towns, which were the Coast Road's reason for being, each of them a few dozen tarred houses on a bar or a cape, with a smoke-house and a chapel and a quay and a gate. Every town had a gate. Calder had known that all his life, as you know things about a country you've never been to. His father had driven barley down the Way to Wendmouth every autumn and come back with a list of what the gates had cost him, and said a word over it Ada didn't let him say in the house.

He knew it differently from the air.

From the air you could see the gates. Every one. A bar across the road at each town's edge. And on the road, between the gates, people. Gullhaven's people. He'd come down the coast with Ulric's riders to find them somewhere that wasn't a road.

---

Gullhaven's hill was empty.

They came down on it on the twelfth day. The salt-store, its fortress roof where Aurveth had lain with sixty people on her back. The farms behind the pans, their doors shut. The smoke-houses standing in the sea where the spit's end had been, with their white crosses, empty.

Nobody.

"Two weeks since," said Ulric. He'd talked to a farmer's wife at a gate. "They put them off before the year was out. Said they'd fed them the winter and there was nothing for the spring." He looked at the shut doors. "They're on the road west."

---

They found them on the thirteenth day.

From the air, first. A long thin line on the Coast Road going west, between the shingle and the scrub. Two leagues of it. Moving as people move who've been walking for two weeks and have nowhere to get to by any particular day.

Calder brought Kharrak down at the line's tail.

They knew him.

That was the first thing. They looked up at the red dragon coming down onto the road and didn't scatter. An old man with a stick stopped and looked at Calder on Kharrak's neck, and took his hat off, and put it back on. A salt-boy, eleven or so, ran along the line shouting. Then they were all looking.

Then a woman came back along the road toward him with her white arms bare.

---

"Master Fenn," said Hessa Pane.

She stood in the road and looked up at him. Thinner. Her arms not white any more, brown and scabbed with the cold. A shawl over her head.

"You came," she said.

"I came."

She looked at Kharrak. At Ulric's grey coming down behind. At the flights, wheeling over the road, forty pairs, red and grey.

"Well," she said. "You're late."

---

There were about twelve hundred of them.

Ulric's riders counted. Old women. Salt-boys. Net-menders. Families with carts nobody owned, pulling them themselves because there was nothing to pull them with. A few donkeys. A goat on a string. Hessa's mother, the eldest salt-wife, bent double, in a hand-cart pushed by two salt-girls, with her eyes shut and her tin cup in both hands in her lap.

"Where's Nan Gorrie?" said Calder.

"Stayed," said Hessa. "There was a farmer's widow at the salt-store farm who'd known her when they were girls. She took her in." She looked at the road west. "One. Out of four thousand. One farmer's widow."

---

The first gate was at Sallow Bar.

A fishing town on a gravel bar, where the Coast Road crossed a tidal creek on a timber bridge. Forty houses. A smoke-house. A chapel to the Drowned Lady with a blue-painted door. And across the bridge's far end, a bar of timber on a post, and men with crossbows.

He saw it from the air on the fourteenth morning. The column bunched up at the bridge's near end, stopped, three hundred people standing in the road, and the gate down.

He saw people at the gate handing things over.

---

He went low.

Over the creek. Low enough to see. A woman at the gate holding out a blanket, and a man with a crossbow taking it and throwing it on a pile. A net. A kettle. A pair of boots. A man with a fiddle under his arm, standing, and then holding it out.

A copper a head. That was what the board on the gate post said. He could read it from the air. A silver a cart.

They didn't have coppers. They hadn't had anything for two weeks.

So they were paying in what they'd carried.

---

He saw Hessa Pane at the gate.

Pushing the hand-cart. Her mother in it, with her eyes shut. Hessa stopped at the bar and said something to the crossbowman, and he shook his head and pointed at the board.

Hessa stood there.

Then she leaned down into the cart and took the tin cup out of her mother's hands.

---

He knew the cup.

He'd seen it at dawn on the spit every morning for a month. The eldest salt-wife holding it out over the water at the spit's end with both hands, bent double, the brine from the pans in it. *Lady, we give you back your salt. Keep the stones.* He'd seen it on the jetty at the pour. Hessa's hand round it. Shaking the last drop out.

Hessa held it out to the crossbowman.

The crossbowman took it, and turned it over, and shrugged, and threw it on the pile with the kettles and the boots.

He lifted the bar.

---

He brought Kharrak down on the bridge.

On the far side. Past the gate. On the timbers, which groaned, with Kharrak's tail in the creek. The crossbowmen went back off the bridge all together into the town's one street with their crossbows up.

Ulric came down beside him on the gravel bar.

Forty pairs came down behind them. On the bar. On the shingle. In the creek, at low water, standing in the mud to their bellies. On the chapel roof, which was a mistake, because it fell in.

The town came out of its doors to look.

---

The gate-holder was a one-eyed man in a leather coat.

He came out of the gatehouse with a crossbow on his shoulder and stood on the road and looked at forty dragons. He had a face like a boot left out in the rain. His men called him the Gaffer.

"You'll be the farmer," he said.

"I'll be the farmer."

"Thought so," said the Gaffer. He looked at the gate post. At the board, a copper a head. And above the board, nailed to the post, a paper with a blue wave at its head. Calder could read it from where he stood. He didn't need to. *For the taking and holding of the three dragons bonded to the outlaw Calder Fenn. Alive. Ten thousand gold.*

The Gaffer looked at the paper.

Then at the dragons.

---

"A copper a head," said Ulric. Before Calder could open his mouth. He'd got down off his grey and come over the gravel with a purse. The Clan's purse. Leather. Heavy. "Twelve hundred heads. Call it a thousand. They're thin." He held it out. "Silver for the carts."

"No," said Calder.

Ulric looked at him.

"You're not paying him," said Calder. "Not for this. He's taking their blankets."

"He's holding the gate," said Ulric. "That's what he's for. That's what the gate's for. That's how the coast is." He held out the purse to the Gaffer. "A thousand coppers. And the carts."

The Gaffer took the purse.

---

"And the cup," said Calder.

The Gaffer looked at him.

"The tin cup," said Calder. "On the pile. The old woman's. I want it back."

The Gaffer looked at the pile. At the cup on top of it, dented, with a crust of salt still round the inside.

"Bought and paid," he said.

"Then I'll buy it."

"With what?" said the Gaffer. He looked at Calder's coat. At his hands, one of them still wrapped in a cloth. "You've got a price on you, farmer, but you've no money. Everyone on the coast knows that. The Clan pays for you."

---

He looked at the paper on the post again.

Calder watched him look at it. At the ten thousand. And then at the dragons on his gravel bar, forty of them, and the one on his bridge with its tail in his creek, watching him with one gold eye.

The Gaffer was quiet for a long time.

Then he laughed. A short one. Like a gate-hinge.

"Ten thousand's a lot of money," he said. "Forty dragons is a lot of dragons." He went to the pile and picked the tin cup off the top of it and turned it over in his hand. "I'll take the Clan's coppers," he said. "And stay alive. That's my price."

He tossed the cup to Calder.

Calder caught it with his good hand.

---

Then the Gaffer went to the gate post.

He reached up and took hold of the paper with the blue wave on it and tore it off the nail. He folded it once and held it out.

"For a keepsake," he said.

Calder took it.

"You'll find one on every post from here to Blackwater," said the Gaffer. He put his crossbow back on his shoulder. "I'd not stand under any of them, if I were you. Not every gate's got a man on it who can count."

---

He gave Hessa the cup on the far side of the bridge.

She looked at it in his hand. Then at him. She didn't take it.

"I sold it," she said.

"He gave it back."

"For nothing?"

"For forty dragons," said Calder.

Hessa looked at the dragons on the gravel bar.

Then she took the cup and leaned into the hand-cart and put it back in her mother's hands, and her mother's fingers closed on it without her eyes opening.

---

There was a ship's clerk on the quay at Sallow Bar.

A thin man with a ledger, at a table, by a coaster loading salt fish. He'd been there through all of it. He hadn't looked up. When Calder came along the quay he looked up.

"Berth?" he said.

"Where to?"

"Anywhere you like. From Wendmouth. This boat goes to Wendmouth." He licked his pencil. "Wendmouth to Corsa, the next one. If you've got the fare."

"What's the fare?"

The clerk told him.

---

Calder knew exactly what it was.

He'd grown barley all his life. He knew what a bushel fetched at Wendmouth in a good year and a bad year, and how many bushels a man needed to see his family through a winter, and what was left over if he was lucky. He did the sum in his head, standing on the quay. He didn't need his finger.

A berth to Corsa was a year's barley.

For one person.

"Who pays that?" he said.

"Anyone who wants to get to Corsa," said the clerk. "And there's plenty that do." He looked along the quay at the column, crossing the bridge. "They say the Council's seeing to people in Corsa."

"Is it?"

The clerk looked at him.

"For a year's barley," he said, "the Council will see to anyone."

---

"They're not going to Corsa," said Calder.

That night. At a fire on the gravel bar. With Ulric and Hessa and the old man with the stick, whose name was Abel Tarrow and who'd been the harbour-master's clerk before the harbour-master drowned.

"Nor Wendmouth," he said. "Not for work at the floating market. There's no work for twelve hundred at the floating market. There's a gate."

"Then where?" said Hessa.

He'd been thinking about it since the quay.

"Inland," he said. "Up the Wend. Up the Way. To the Marches." He looked at the fire. "Harrow's End. The farms round it. The Saltreach bank went home in the spring, but half the farms on the Way are standing empty since the war. There's land lying fallow." He looked at Hessa. "There's mine."

---

Hessa looked at him across the fire.

"Barley," she said.

"Barley."

"We're salt-folk. We don't know barley."

"I'll show you," said Calder. "It's not hard. You put it in the ground and swear at the weather."

The old man with the stick, Abel Tarrow, laughed. Then he stopped, and looked at the fire, and didn't say anything for a while.

"How far?" he said.

"A hundred leagues," said Calder. "More. By the road." He looked up. At the dragons on the bar, asleep in the dark, forty of them, like a herd in a yard. "Not by the road."

---

Wendmouth's gate was on the sixteenth day.

The big one. On the Coast Road bridge into the city. Stone, with a gatehouse, and a bar of iron, and the Saffery house's colours painted on the gatehouse shutters, red and black, the beast-traders. The column bunched up at the bridge for half a league.

They walked through. The Clan's purse paid. The Saffery gate-captain counted the coppers himself, very politely, and wrote it in a ledger.

Calder walked through with them. On foot. With Kharrak overhead.

He shouldn't have.

---

He saw the net first.

In the crowd at the gate. A man in a sailor's coat with something long under it, and the something came out, and it was a tube of brass on a stock, like a crossbow without the bow, pointed at him. A net-gun. He'd seen one at Gallows Quay a year ago, on a rack, with a price on it.

And a hound beside the man. Hooded. On a short chain.

And another man, in the crowd behind, with something smoking in his hand.

---

Lisska came off the gatehouse wall.

He hadn't known she was there. She came straight down off the wall onto the man with the net-gun, wings half open, and the net-gun went off into the air and the net went up over the gate in a great slow unfolding and came down on the Saffery gate-captain and his ledger.

The man went down under Lisska.

The hound came off its chain. Hooded. Bound. It went for Calder with nothing in it but what the man with the smoke had put there.

It got as far as the bridge.

---

He didn't know why he did it.

He stepped back. Off the road. Onto the bridge's edge, over the creek where it ran under into the harbour. The hound came after him. And he went off the edge, into the creek, to his waist, cold, and the hound came off the bridge after him into the running water.

And stopped.

In the creek. With the water going round its legs. He saw it stop.

It stood there. Then it shook its head, hard, and the hood came off, and its eyes were full. Somebody in them. A hound. Brindled. Frightened. Looking at him and the bridge and the crowd as if it had never seen any of them.

It climbed out of the creek on the far side and ran.

---

Ulric's riders had the two men by then.

On the road. Face down. With Clan boots on their backs. The man with the smoke had thrown his pot into the harbour.

The Saffery gate-captain came out from under the net.

He was very polite about it. He apologised for the inconvenience. He said such things happened at a busy gate and the house of Saffery regretted them. He said the men would be held for the Council.

Then he asked for the toll for the dragons on the wall.

---

"It's true, then," said a man in the queue.

Behind Calder. At the gate. When it was over, and Calder was standing in the road with the creek running out of his coat. A sailor. Weathered. With a bag on his shoulder.

"What is?"

"That's the farmer," said the sailor. To the man beside him. "With the dragons. I said." He looked at Calder. "I'm up from the Marches. On a salt-barge. Four days ago from the Staithe." He shifted the bag. "They're saying there the lion-woman's gone east."

Calder looked at him.

---

"East where?"

"Into the Wyldmarch," said the sailor. "The clans are running out of it. Something on the Plains. And the crown's shut the border on them." He grinned. "And she's gone. Against the crown's order. The knights' captain stood on the jetty and forbade her, in front of everybody, they say. Turned her back on her." He shrugged. "And she went anyway. With the lion."

"When?"

"Six days back," said the sailor. "They say the council'll outlaw her for it."

Calder stood in the road with the water running out of his coat.

He thought of the picture Lisska had given him. A woman under a cracked stone with one hand flat on it, holding it up.

Of course she had.

---

The lift began on the seventeenth day.

From the gravel bar outside Wendmouth's gate, where the column had camped. The baskets. The war-harness from Gullhaven, oil-black leather, the straps and rings. The Clan had brought every basket it had. Forty pairs, two baskets each when a dragon could take two.

The old first. Then the children. Then the sick.

Up the Wend. Over the Reedmarsh. Over Cotter's Cross. Up the Drover's Way, over the farms he'd known all his life, to Harrow's End.

Two hundred a day.

---

He came down on his own top field on the twentieth day.

Above the village. Where the barley had stood, the last summer before everything. Where his father had stood. Stubble now, a winter old, grey and flattened by the snow.

The first families from Gullhaven were sitting in it.

On the stubble. With their bundles. Looking at the valley below them, the river and the village and the empty farms on the Way, the hills going up behind to the Scarfell, as people look at a field they've been given.

Hessa Pane's mother was on a blanket at the field's edge. With her eyes shut. With the tin cup in her lap.

---

He stood at the gate of the top field and looked at them.

He'd been to Corsa and stood in the Tidehall and read numbers to the Council. He'd been thrown a tin cup by a one-eyed man for the price of forty dragons. He'd stood in a creek and watched a hound come back into its own eyes. He'd been told a berth to Corsa was a year's barley, and he'd done the sum without his finger.

Everything on this coast cost something.

He thought about it, standing at the gate, with the people from the spit on his stubble.

The only thing on the whole coast that hadn't cost anything was a dragon standing on a roof with a white cross on it.

He went down into the field to show them where the plough was.
