---

She crossed the Ysse at a drove ford.

On the sixteenth day. Alone, with the lion. Rowan had said don't go by the Greenwold, so she hadn't. She'd gone east from the Staithe by the old drove tracks, the ones the cattle-drovers used before the Way was rutted, over the Marches hills, past the last farms where people came out of their doors to look at the lion and went in again, and on over the scrub and the moor where there were no doors at all, and on the sixteenth day she came down to the river.

The Ysse.

It came down out of the Worldsend, out of the lake, cold and green and fast. The Plains' own river. Here, at the eastern edge of everything, it ran between the empty country on this side and the Plains on the other.

There was no one at the drove ford. There were stones in the water with the drovers' marks on them, and a cairn on the far bank.

Orrun went in first.

---

The Plains were dead.

She'd seen them green, a year ago, at the Moot. Grass to the edge of the sky. The Worldsend white at the back of it all day and never nearer. Standing stones alone or in rows, humming.

They didn't hum now.

The grass was grey, as if all the colour had run out of it into the ground and the ground had swallowed it. The standing stones were grey. The sky was grey. Nothing moved on the Plains for as far as she could see but the wind, and the wind didn't move the grass much, because the grass had stopped caring.

The drain, the reader had called it. When the stag went. A hundred leagues dead.

She walked east across it with Orrun for two days and saw nothing alive.

---

On the eighteenth day she saw dust.

Ahead. East. A low brown line on the edge of the grey, the whole width of the horizon. Moving.

Then she heard them.

Hooves. Thousands. And under the hooves, something heavier. A slow grinding thunder, like the river in a spring flood rolling stones along its bed.

Orrun stopped.

*Stonehorns,* he said.

---

They came out of the dust in a line.

The herds first. Grey beasts, rock-plated on the shoulders and the back, the size of a cart, with horns that curved forward and down like the shares of a plough. Hundreds. The Wyldmarch's draught-beasts, its war-beasts, the stonehorn clans' wealth and their living. They came on at a walk, heads low, with men walking between them with long poles.

Then the people.

On foot. Carts. Horse-clansfolk on their mares, and on the mares' backs, in front of the riders and behind them, children. Two to a saddle. Three, some of them. Old people in carts. Old people walking. Keepers in their green, with wolves.

Ten thousand. More. She couldn't count them. She tried.

---

Elder Tova rode out to meet her.

On a bay mare with a white face. An old woman. Older than Ada. With her grey hair cropped as short as a man's and a face like a field after ploughing, all furrows. Her hands on the reins were brown and knotted like the roots of a tree.

She stopped her mare a length from the lion. She looked at Orrun for a long time.

Then at Maren.

"The lion-woman," she said. "From the Moot."

"Yes."

"You came," said Tova. Slowly. As if every word had to be weighed first. "I didn't think you would. I sent the boy anyway." She looked west, past Maren, toward the Ysse. "The border's shut."

"I know."

"Then how will we cross?" said Tova.

"Not by the Greenwold," said Maren.

---

Keeper Rhun came that night.

To Tova's fire. A broad grey woman in the Keepers' green, with a wolf at each knee, grey as she was. She sat down by the fire without being asked and looked at Maren across it.

"I knew Hesper," she said.

Maren waited.

"She died at the Moot," said Rhun. "With her cat. Holding the last lane. We heard. You were there."

"I was there."

Rhun looked at the fire.

"She said there'd be a lion," she said. "Before. In the Thornwood, before any of it. She said a lion would come out of the west with a woman on it, and we should listen." She put her hand on the nearer wolf's head. "Nobody listened. The Moot was too busy being a Moot." She looked up. "We're listening now."

---

She saw the six at dusk.

From a rise of standing stones. Grey, nine of them in a ring, on a low swell of the Plains a day west of where the clans had camped. Tova took her up there on the mare's crupper, and they stood among the dead stones and looked east.

She'd seen the dust. She'd heard the stones cracking, at night, far off. Like ice.

She hadn't seen them.

---

The Plains were burning.

Not with fire. A line of black. Far off, across the whole of the east, at the Worldsend's foot, where the grey grass met the grey sky. The grey going black in a long band, as if someone had drawn a line across the world with charcoal and the line was spreading.

And in the black, six.

---

She knew the first one.

Grey. A hill. A long rough ridge of plates down its back like a broken mountain range. She'd stood at the foot of it in a cavern under the river with a dark sword in her hand while it put its brow to the stone. Six places in its back where the stone was newer than the rest.

Beside it, a stag.

Antlered. The antlers going up like the branches of a winter wood, bare, black against the burning. And round it, moving, trees. A forest. Oaks and thorns and ash, walking. Pulling their roots up out of the dead ground and swinging them forward and putting them down again, a whole wood moving across the Plains beside the stag like a herd round its leader.

The stag from the Ring. She'd seen it stand up out of the Thornwood a year ago, from the Moot's shingle, with the trees falling away from it like water.

---

Then something long and pale.

Coiled. White as frost. Lying in the black with the ground round it gone white, rime, a ring of white on the burned grass, spreading and fading as the coils moved. She knew it from Glasswater. From the shore, with the ice coming back thicker every dawn and the water coming over Dye Street.

The Coil.

Then something low, and wide, and plated. Like the ironbacks in the Fells, but the size of a field. Lying flat. The ground round it shining. Glass. And on it, things hanging off its sides, black, broken. Chain. Kessmoor's chain. Still on it.

The Furnace. She'd watched it go into its cage.

---

Then the light.

She had to look away. White and gold. Coming off something lean and tall and deer-shaped, the size of a hill, with one long horn going up from its brow, spiralled, shining. The light came off the horn in rays, as it came off the sun at noon, so the dusk round it was day for half a league.

The Horned Sun. She'd seen it stand up in a bay with the sea round its knees.

And the last one.

Dragging itself.

Over the dry plain. Slowly. A back like a reef, barnacled, weed hanging off it dead and brown. The size of an island. And on its back, grey columns, broken. A dome, fallen in. And hanging in the dome, a bell.

She heard it. Faint. A league and more off. Ringing as the thing on whose back it hung dragged itself over the burned grass toward the west.

---

Six.

All together. Moving west. Slowly. The burning going ahead of them and spreading behind.

She stood among the dead stones and counted them again. She'd known there were six. She'd stood in the vault. She'd watched the five come home through the farmer's letters. She'd seen them go up off the Scarfell from the ferry-house jetty, six shapes on the rim of the sky.

She hadn't seen them close. All of them. Together.

Tova sat on her mare beside the stones, silent.

Orrun was lying at the foot of the rise. With his chin on the grey grass. He was looking at the first one. The grey one. His kin.

He didn't say anything either.

---

The Ysse had four fords.

Four places where the drove roads came down off the Plains to the river and crossed it, where the bed was gravel and the banks were low.

She looked at them from the bank on the twentieth day. The river high. Green. Fast. Snowmelt out of the Worldsend, out of the lake, coming down past them with a sound like wind in a wood. The fords were under it. You could see where they were by the way the water humped over the gravel.

Too deep for a child. Too fast for an old man. The stonehorns could cross. The people couldn't.

---

"Four," she said.

Orrun looked at the river.

*Two,* he said. *Me, and one more. One at a time. I can't hold four.*

"Then two. And move it down."

He was quiet.

*It will cost you,* he said.

"I know."

*Your feet,* said Orrun. *Or your hands. Or something else. I don't know which. It never tells me which.*

"I know," said Maren. "Two."

---

He went into the river at the northern ford.

Upstream of it. Into the green water to his shoulders, and lay down in it, across the current. The water went over his back white.

And at the next ford, a league south, a lion stood up out of the river-bed and lay down in the water.

She felt it go out of her. Like a cut opened. Like blood. She stood on the bank and felt the cold come up her legs from the ground, and didn't sit down.

---

They lifted the bed.

Both of them. She felt it through Orrun. The stone of the river-bed under them, the gravel and the rock under the gravel, coming up. Under the water. Under their bellies. A sill of stone across the river upstream of each ford, rising until the current broke over it white and went over in a smooth hump, and below it, where the ford was, the water slowed.

And shallowed.

From a man's chest to a man's waist. From a child's head to a child's chest.

There was no word in it. She couldn't have said one if she'd wanted to. The Plains were dead and the river was running water, and nothing she could say would take on either. It was him. His stone. Through her. Through the bond.

It was all she had out here.

---

The stonehorns went in below the sills.

Tova's idea, or the stonehorn clans'. They'd done it for three hundred years at every ford on the Plains. The herd-men drove them into the river in a line, below the sill, side by side, shoulder to shoulder, facing upstream. Grey plated beasts the size of carts, standing in the river with the water piling white against their chests.

A wall. A wall of stonehorns.

And in the still water behind them, in their lee, the people crossed.

---

Four days.

Night and day. She didn't sleep. She walked the bank from ford to ford with the cold in her legs and counted.

When the second ford was over, the shadow got up out of the river with the water pouring off it and went down the bank to the third, and lay down again, and the sill behind it sank. Then the fourth. Orrun didn't move from the northern ford at all.

The mares went over with the children. Two to a saddle. Three. The horse-clan riders walking at their heads in the water to their waists, leading them, with the children holding on to the manes and each other and not crying, mostly. The carts. Old people carried. A woman with a baby held over her head in both hands, walking through the water behind the stonehorns with her eyes shut.

The Keepers' wolves ran the herds' flanks.

On both banks. Grey, low, quick, along the edge of the crossing, turning back the stonehorns that broke the line, turning the sheep, turning a mare that had panicked in the current, nipping her heels until she went on. Keeper Rhun on the far bank with a wolf at each knee and the rest of them running, calling them with a sound in her throat like a wind over stones.

---

The beast-flood came on the twenty-second day.

She heard it first. A sound out of the east like the stonehorns' sound but faster. Higher. Wilder. A drumming that came up through her feet.

Then she saw it.

Everything. Everything on the Plains that could still run. Coming west ahead of the six, ahead of the burning. Wild horses. Plains elk with their heads up and their eyes white. A herd of feral stonehorns, a hundred of them, running mad. Wolves. Deer. Hares by the thousand. A bear. All of it together, all of it running, a brown flood coming over the grey grass toward the river.

Toward the southern ford.

Where the last of the stonehorn clans were still in the water.

---

She was a league off.

At the third ford, empty now. She saw it coming and there was nothing she could do from a league off. Orrun was in the river at the northern ford. The shadow was at the southern one, lying across the current upstream, with three hundred people in the water below it, and a herd of feral stonehorns coming at it out of the east at a run.

She knelt on the grey grass.

She put both hands flat on it.

---

No word.

No count, even. She didn't have time to count. She put her hands on the dead ground of the Plains and reached through the bond for him, three leagues off, in the river, his stone, the weight of the earth in his feet, and pulled it.

Through her. Through her hands. Into the plain.

And out.

---

The ground came up.

She saw it. Between the beast-flood and the southern ford. The grey turf tearing. Lifting. A ridge of grey stone coming up out of the Plains like the back of something surfacing, a quarter of a league long, slanting across the flood's path. Waist-high. Then man-high. Grey rock with turf on its top and roots hanging off it, still rising.

The beast-flood hit it.

The feral stonehorns first. They hit the ridge at a run, and broke on it, and turned. North. Along it. All of them turning together, as water turns along a wall, away from the ford, along the ridge, up the bank, and away north across the Plains in a long brown wave.

The ford was clear.

---

She couldn't get up.

She tried. She put her hands on the ground and pushed and her legs didn't come.

She looked at them.

Her feet. Her boots. Her legs, kneeling on the grey grass. She could see them. They were there. She couldn't feel them.

From the soles to the knees.

Nothing.

She hit her knee with her fist. She watched her fist hit it. Nothing.

---

*Maren.*

He was coming. Up out of the river at the northern ford, three leagues off, the water pouring off him. She felt him come. Fast. Faster than she'd ever felt him move.

"I'm here," she said.

*Your legs.*

"I know."

*I told you,* said Orrun. *It never tells me which.*

"I know you told me."

---

He carried her across the last ford.

On the twenty-fourth day. When the last of the stonehorn clans were over and the last of the herds, and the shadow went back down into the river-bed and the sills sank and the Ysse came down over the fords high and green and fast again as if nothing had happened.

On his back. Behind his mane. She held on with her hands. Her legs hung down his sides and she couldn't feel his back under them, or his warmth, or the water when it came up round her boots.

Tova rode beside them on her mare.

At the top of the west bank she turned in the saddle and looked back across the river. At the ridge, grey on the grey plain, a quarter of a league long, with the turf still on its top.

"Grey Mother," she said. Under her breath. Not to Maren.

Then she looked at Maren's legs, hanging.

"The stone came up," she said.

"Yes."

"The stonehorns turned," said Tova. "I've walked these Plains seventy years. I've never seen a stonehorn turn for anything." She looked at the lion. "What did it cost?"

"My legs," said Maren. "For a few days. It's coming back."

Tova was quiet.

"Only that?" she said.

---

The crown's riders came at dusk.

Three of them. Out of the west, out of the scrub, on horses, a long way from their own bank. Outriders in the crown's blue, and the one at the front with a falcon on his fist. She'd never seen him. He rode up onto the rise above the northern ford and stopped.

And looked.

At ten thousand people on the west bank, with their carts and their fires and their mares. At the stonehorn herds, cropping what they could find in the scrub. At the Keepers' wolves. At the river behind them, high, fast, uncrossable, that they'd crossed.

At the lion.

He looked at the lion for a long time. Then at the woman on its back with her legs hanging down.

He put the falcon up. It went west, low, and was gone. Then he turned his horse round and the three of them went back the way they'd come.

"Somebody will tell the council," said Tova.

"Let them," said Maren.

---

The scout came on the twenty-fifth day.

A horse-clan boy. Fifteen. Tova had sent him west after the crown's riders to see where they went. He came back at a gallop and slid off his mare by the fire and said they'd gone back west over the scrub toward the Wend, toward the Greenwold road, and hadn't stopped.

And something else.

"The drovers at the wells say there were dragons," he said. "On the Wend. Down in the west. Ten days back, more." He was breathing hard. "Many. Going south, down the river, toward the sea. The drovers talk about nothing else."

Tova looked at Maren.

"Yours?" she said.

"No," said Maren. "The farmer's."

"What farmer?"

Maren looked at the fire.

"He's going to the sea," she said. "There's a coast there that needs him."

---

On the twenty-sixth day she could feel her feet.

Pins first. Then cold. Then the ground, a little, through her boots. She stood up in the morning by Tova's fire and her legs held, mostly, and she walked to the river and back.

Then she walked to the head of the column.

Ten thousand people. Behind her. Their carts and their mares and their stonehorns, the herds grazing on the west bank, the Keepers' wolves lying in the grass, the children running between the carts. The Plains behind them, across the river, grey and dead and burning far off in the east.

West. Over the scrub and the moor, toward the Wend. At a herd's pace. Four leagues a day.

Orrun walked beside her. She put her hand in his mane. She could feel it. She left it there.
