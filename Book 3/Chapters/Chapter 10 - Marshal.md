---

The council's rider came on the twenty-ninth day.

Down the King's Road to the river, on a crown horse that had been ridden too far. Rowan was on the jetty with Liss, watching the rope. She saw the blue wax on the paper before the man was out of the saddle, and knew what it was.

*Captain Ilse Rowan, of the school of Greyhold, to attend the council of the crown at Hallowmere. To answer for a knight of her school.*

She read it to the end, and then the seal. Then she folded it very small, as she'd folded the last one.

"Well," said Liss.

"Well," said Rowan.

---

She rode on the thirtieth with twelve.

Twelve knights on greyharts. Wynn with Ash and Sorrow at her heels. The rest of the forty she left at the river under Aldous Pryne, who'd been a knight longer than she had and wouldn't thank her for saying so, to keep the road down to the ferry and help Liss with whoever came over.

Three days by the King's Road. West, over the downs. The greyharts went at the long easy walk they could keep all day, their heads high, their ears turning.

The country was dead the whole way.

---

She'd stopped noticing, mostly.

The grass grey at the roots. No hum in it. The farm wells sour. Larks gone. She'd ridden on dead ground for more than a year now, since the night the Titan went out of its chains at the Ruin, and most of the time she didn't notice it any more than she noticed her own feet.

What she noticed was the hounds.

Ash and Sorrow ranging ahead of the column through the grey grass, noses down, tails up, quartering. Turning together without a word between them. Coming back to Wynn's knee and going out again. Ash put up a hare at the edge of a barley stubble and Sorrow was across its line before it had gone ten yards, and Wynn whistled once and they both left it and came back, grinning.

Everything else had stopped. The hounds hadn't.

---

Hallowmere on the thirty-second day.

Grey stone and slate, in terraces down to the Mere. She came in by the east gate at noon as the bell was ringing. Nobody in the street stopped. Nobody faced anything. They used to face the Mere at noon, when the lamps were lit and the wells were sweet. Now the bell rang and people went on queuing for bread.

The queues were long. They went round corners.

The lamps on their posts in the better streets were glass. Just glass, with dust on the inside. The sweet wells by the corn exchange had gone brackish; a woman at the pump spat out what she'd drawn and drew again. The Mere, at the bottom of the town, was grey and low and smelt of mud.

---

A woman ran at them in the tanners' lanes.

Down by the Mere, where the lower town was. Rowan had brought the column round that way because the upper streets were full of bread queues. The woman came out of an alley with her apron in her fists and caught at Wynn's stirrup.

Her girl. Five years old. Gone since the morning. Somewhere in the lanes.

Wynn looked at Rowan.

"Go on," said Rowan.

---

Wynn got down. He asked for a shoe. The woman ran and came back with one, a child's clog, worn through at the toe.

Wynn held it under Ash's nose. Said a word to him, quietly. Not a working word. Just a word. Ash's name, and the name of the thing he wanted.

Ash put his nose down and went.

---

It took a quarter of an hour.

Through the tanners' lanes, and the dyers', and across a yard full of hides stretched on frames, and down a stair, with Wynn running behind and Sorrow running behind Wynn, and the woman behind them both. And Rowan on her horse, walking, at the back. And behind Rowan, by the end, half the lower town.

They came out of their doors to watch. They followed. Men off the tanning-pits with their arms stained to the elbows. Old women. Children. A crowd. Following a hound through the streets as if it were a lamp being carried.

Rowan watched their faces and understood after a while.

Nobody in Hallowmere had seen anything work in more than a year.

---

The girl was in a dry cistern under a broken grate in a court behind the dyers'.

Frightened, and unhurt. She'd climbed in after a cat and couldn't climb out. Ash stood over the grate and barked once, and Wynn got down on his belly and reached in and lifted her out by the wrists.

The court was full. They didn't cheer. They stood and looked at the hound.

An old man near Rowan's stirrup said it. To nobody. "How does it still do that?"

---

"It's not the Deep," said Rowan.

He looked up at her.

"The lamps were the Deep. The wells. The words." She looked at Ash, sitting now in the court with his tongue out, being stroked by twenty hands. "That's a hound and a man who've agreed about something. You can't drain that out of the ground. It isn't in the ground."

The old man thought about it.

"Can I have one?" he said.

---

The council sat on the thirty-third.

In the crown's chapter-house at the head of the Crown's Square. A long room with a high roof and the orders' banners hung down the walls, faded. A dead lamp on a post at each corner of the square outside.

She stood at the foot of the table in her mended surcoat. She'd mended it again on the road. White, with Greyhold's grey tower on the breast, and a long darn down one side where it had torn on the jetty road the day the hill came down.

Lord Ambrose Thale at the head of the table. Old. Thin. Careful. The chain of office lying on his chest like something too heavy for it.

---

The knight-captain told it first.

Sir Hamon Crale. From the crown's post on the Greenwold road. A heavy man, red in the face, honest, she thought, and frightened, and ashamed of being frightened, which was to his credit. He stood at the side of the table with his gloves in his hands and told the council what his outriders had seen at dusk on the twenty-fourth day. His falcon had brought it to the post. He'd come after it.

Ten thousand people. On the west bank of the Ysse. Across already, at the drove fords, where there'd been no fords that would take a herd. Their herds with them. Stonehorns. Horses. Wolves.

"And the knight," said Crale. "Ashdown. On her lion." He turned his gloves over. "It's the size of a barn now, my lords. Bigger. It looked at my falconer across the river." He stopped. "He turned his horse round. I'd have done the same."

Nobody at the table blamed him for it. She could see them not blaming him.

---

The council decided quickly.

It had decided before she came, she thought. It said so in its own way.

The line at the Wend would hold. The clans would stay on the far side of it, on the Saltreach moor, between the Ysse and the river. There was no grain. The kingdom could not feed ten thousand clansfolk and their beasts. The Candle Downs would be eaten to the root in a month.

All true. She'd said it herself, at Liss's table.

And the knight.

---

The clerk had the paper ready.

He laid it in front of her at the foot of the table. Fair-copied. With a space at the bottom.

*Maren Ashdown, a knight of the school of Greyhold, having gone east against the order of the crown's council to lead the clans of the Wyldmarch and their beasts to the crown's border, is outlawed by name. Five hundred crowns to any who bring her to the crown's justice.*

And the space. For her school's captain.

"The school must sign," said Thale. Gently. "She's yours, Captain."

---

She'd said she would.

On the jetty road. *I'll be the one who has to sign it.* In front of the knights, with her back turned.

She took the pen from the clerk.

She'd never in her life said she'd do a thing and not done it. Not since she was nine years old and swore to her father she'd ride the bay colt that had thrown him, and rode it, and broke her collarbone, and rode it again with her arm strapped. She wasn't going to begin at a table in Hallowmere.

She signed it. *Ilse Rowan, Captain, Greyhold.* Square. Plain.

She put the pen down.

---

"And when they come," she said.

The table looked at her.

"When ten thousand people come to Sir Hamon's barricade," said Rowan. "With their children on the saddles and their herds behind them. And something behind the herds." She looked at Thale. "What are his knights to do?"

"Turn them back," said Thale.

"With what?"

---

Nobody answered.

She hadn't thought they would. She let them not answer for a while.

Crale looked at his gloves.

"Thank you, Captain," said Thale.

---

Davin was on the chapter-house steps.

Sitting on the top one in the sun, with a writing-case on his knees. Seventeen, eighteen now. Thin. Ink on his fingers. The tribunes' clerk. He got up when she came out.

"Captain."

"Master Davin."

"I read her letter," said Davin. "To the square. On the twentieth. *Something is coming, and the council has shut the door on the people running from it.*" He looked at the doors behind her. "The tribunes have asked the council four times since to let the clans in. The council's stopped answering the tribunes."

"I know."

Davin looked at her.

"Did you sign it?" he said.

"Yes."

He nodded slowly. As if he'd been told the price of something and it was what he'd expected.

"She said you would," he said.

---

The crown's chapel was off the square.

A small high room of grey stone behind the chapter-house. She went in at dusk because the door was open and the square was full of bread queues and she didn't want to go back to the knights' hall yet and be looked at.

Benches. A stone floor worn into hollows. And on the altar step, a row of lamps.

Glass lamps. Twelve of them, in a line. The ones they used to say were the gods' gift to the crown. They'd burned without oil here for three hundred years. Now they were glass, with dust in them, and someone had put a tallow candle in a dish at the end of the row, for light to see the glass by.

---

She'd never prayed.

Knights of Greyhold didn't, much. The school had a chapel on the hill once, and old knights who went to it. She'd gone at her vows, because you did, and at funerals. She'd stood. She'd never knelt and asked anything of anyone she couldn't see.

She sat on the back bench.

She lit a candle from the one on the altar step, out of habit, because that was what you did in a chapel, and put it in the dish beside the other, and sat down again.

---

She thought about the chain.

She hadn't let herself. Not properly. Not in forty days.

The north tower. The noon of the three hundred and fifty-fifth day. The eyes tearing out of the rock one after another and the yard engine going over the wall with Dane on it. And her horn in her hand.

Three short notes.

She'd blown them. The crown's men had knocked the pins out of the drums and the chain had run out into the river, link after link, each as long as a man. The crown's chain. The second she'd held for the crown, and the second that had gone. And she'd taken her knights and the camp down the jetty road and across, and been the last one off the hill, on foot.

---

She'd counted them.

On the jetty road. Going down. She'd counted the seventy in the cellars dead. The cellar door was under the fallen slab. She knew it. She'd let go of the chain, and the hill would come down on the seventy, and she'd counted them, all seventy, while she was running.

They hadn't died. The lion had torn the hill open from above, and Joby Marsh had brought them up out of it with his lantern, all seventy, into the dusk. She'd heard it across the river that night, from a groom who'd swum the Wend to tell her.

That didn't change the counting.

She'd counted them dead and let go anyway.

---

She'd do it again.

She sat on the back bench and knew it. If it were noon on the three hundred and fifty-fifth day again, and the horn in her hand. She'd blow the three notes. She'd count the seventy and blow them anyway, to get the knights and the camp down the jetty road.

That was the trouble.

A knight who'd let go of the crown's chain to save the people on the jetty road would let go of the crown's order to save ten thousand. She'd known that about herself since the jetty road. She'd turned her back on Maren so as not to have to know it in front of the knights.

---

The candle she'd lit burned down.

She watched it go. The flame getting small in its dish, beside the glass lamps that had been the gods' gift, the dust in them going gold for a moment and then grey.

She didn't pray.

She sat there until it went out. Then she got up and went out into the dark of the square and back to the knights' hall, and told Wynn they'd be riding in the morning.

"Back to the river?" said Wynn.

"No," said Rowan.

---

East. By the Greenwold road.

Four days, with twelve. Out of Hallowmere by the north-east gate and up through the farm country, the fields grey, and into the oaks.

The Greenwold. Old oak woods, miles of them, dark under the leaves even at noon, the road a long green tunnel through them. The aurochs were in the oaks. Black, wild, bigger than any ox. They stood among the trunks and watched the column go by with their heads low, and the hounds kept close to Wynn and didn't range.

Nobody said much.

---

The crown's post was at the ford.

At the eastern edge of the Greenwold, on the thirty-eighth day. Where the road came out of the last of the oaks and went down over grey open ground to the Wend, small up here and fast over its gravel, and crossed it. And on the far bank the moor, grey, open, going away east out of sight.

A barricade across the road. Oak trunks. Felled from the wood's edge and dragged across, three deep, with a ditch dug in front and the spoil thrown up behind. A fire. Tents. Sixty crown men with pikes and crossbows. Crale's knights, a dozen, with their hounds tied to a picket line.

And Crale himself. Back from Hallowmere two days ahead of her, on a faster horse.

He came out to meet her, red in the face.

"Captain."

"Sir Hamon."

He looked at her twelve. He didn't ask why she'd come. He knew.

---

She got down off her horse and walked up to the barricade and looked over it.

East. Over the ford. The road going on up the far bank onto the moor, empty. Somewhere out there, eight days off, ten, ten thousand people walking west at a herd's pace.

She put her hand on the nearest oak trunk.

"Take it down," she said.

---

"That's against the council's order," said Crale.

"Yes."

"Captain. I can't..." He stopped. He looked at his own men by the fire. At the barricade. At her. "I'll have to report it."

"Yes," said Rowan. "Write it down."

---

They used the greyharts.

Her knights put ropes on the first trunk and their greyharts leaned into the traces. Greyharts weren't draught beasts. They'd never been asked. They did it, because their riders asked them. The trunk came off the road a yard at a time, grinding, and rolled into the ditch.

Then the second.

The post's own ox-team was tethered behind the tents. After the second trunk, a crown man Rowan didn't know went and untied them and brought them up without anyone telling him to. Then another man. Then the pikes went down against the tents and the crown men were on the ropes too.

Crale stood by the fire and watched.

By dusk the road was open. The ditch was still there, with the trunks in it. They threw planks across it, wide enough for a cart.

---

Crale brought her his report that night.

By the fire. Written out on a board across his knees, in a slow heavy hand. He held it out to her without a word.

She read it. It was honest. *On the thirty-eighth day the barricade on the Greenwold road was taken down on the order of Captain Ilse Rowan of Greyhold, against the order of the crown's council.*

She took his pen and wrote her name under the line. *Ilse Rowan.* Square. Plain.

"There," she said. "Now it's mine."

---

The council's rider came on the forty-second day.

The same man. The same horse, ridden as hard. She was over the ford on the moor, a mile east of the post, with Wynn and the hounds, looking at nothing, when she saw him come down the road from the woods, and she rode back to meet him.

Blue wax. Thale's own hand, inside. Careful. Small. She read it standing by the fire with the crown men watching her read it.

---

*Captain Rowan.*

*The council has received Sir Hamon Crale's report.*

*Since you have opened the road, you will keep it.*

*The council names Ilse Rowan, Captain of Greyhold, Marshal of the Border, with every man of the crown east of the Greenwold under her command, and the road in her keeping.*

*Sir Hamon has asked to be relieved. The council has relieved him. No lord of the council would go in his place.*

*There is no grain.*

*The matter of the knight Ashdown is pending.*

*Ambrose Thale.*

And under it, the crown's seal. Blue wax. The crown and the twelve lamps.

---

She read it again.

*Pending.* Thale's word. He'd used it once before, on the steps of the chapter-house, for a farmer with three dragons. She'd been there.

She folded the letter once this time, along its length, and put it inside her surcoat against her chest.

The seal was the crown's. A seal was a seal. The council had put its seal on the road in her keeping. Whatever it thought of her, it had bound itself to that, under the crown, and a seal didn't come off because the man who'd pressed it was afraid.

She'd always believed that. She believed it now.

---

"Marshal," said Wynn.

He said it to see how it sounded.

"Captain'll do," said Rowan.

---

The falcon came in on the forty-fifth day.

At dawn. Out of the east, low over the moor, to the fist of the outrider she'd sent out east three days before. And the outrider himself an hour after it, on a lathered greyhart, up the road through the gap where the barricade had been.

She was at the fire.

He didn't get down.

"Two days off, Marshal," he said. "On the moor. Eight leagues out. Coming west on the road. Slow. Herd's pace." He wiped his face. "I counted them from a hill for an hour and gave up. Ten thousand. More. And the herds. Stonehorns as far as I could see. Horses. Wolves running the flanks."

"And at the head?"

"A lion," said the outrider. "The size of a barn. With her on it."

---

Rowan stood up.

East. Over the ford, the moor going away grey toward the Plains. The road on it, open, empty, and behind her the ditch planked across and the oak trunks lying in it.

Two days.

She went to see to the greyharts.
