---

The grass went on being grey.

All the way west. Across the empty country between the Ysse and the Wend, eighty leagues of scrub and moor. Grey to the roots. Nothing in it. The stonehorns cropped it to the dirt every evening and stood in the morning with their ribs showing, and walked on.

Four leagues a day.

Maren had stopped counting the leagues somewhere after the thirtieth day. She counted other things.

---

Tova counted the dead.

Every dusk, at her fire, on her fingers. The column's runners came to her from the tail of it as the carts drew in, and told her, and she folded a finger down for each and said the name.

Eleven since the Ysse.

Old people, mostly. A herd-man gored by a stonehorn bull that was hungry and frightened and didn't know him in the dark. Two infants, in one night, in one cart, from the cold. Their mother walked the next day at the cart's tail with her hands empty and her face shut, and nobody spoke to her, because there wasn't anything to say.

"Eleven," said Tova, on the forty-fourth day. She looked at her hand. "On the Plains in a hard winter we'd lose eleven."

"It's not winter."

"No," said Tova. "That's what I mean."

---

The column was a league and a half long now.

It had got longer as it got slower. The front of it would be at the night's camp while the tail was still coming over the last ridge, carts and mares and stonehorns and people on foot, in a long grey line over grey ground. The Keepers' wolves ran the flanks, a long way out. The children walked. The mares had carried two and three to a saddle at the Ysse. They couldn't now.

She walked at the head. Orrun beside her.

---

Her feet were cold in the mornings.

Since the ridge. They'd come back, the feeling, all the way to the knees. But in the mornings before the sun was on them they were cold to the ankle, and it took an hour's walking before she could feel the ground properly through her boots.

She didn't tell anyone.

*You're walking strangely,* said Orrun.

"I'm walking."

*Strangely,* said Orrun.

---

A falcon came over on the forty-fifth day.

High. Out of the west, against the sun. It turned once over the head of the column and went back the way it had come.

She knew the bells on its jesses before she could see them. She'd heard them all her life, from the mews on the hill.

"Greyhold," she said.

Tova shaded her eyes.

"Yours?"

"Not any more," said Maren.

---

She saw the oaks on the forty-seventh.

Dark along the whole western sky at noon. A wall of them, low, going north and south out of sight. The Greenwold. The road ran down the last of the moor toward them, straight, to the Wend, small and fast up here over its gravel, and crossed it, and ran on up the far slope and in under the trees.

And where it went in, there were people on it.

---

She'd thought about this.

For eighty leagues. Walking. What she'd do at the barricade. Whether Orrun would have to go through it, and what that would cost, and whose men would be under it. She'd thought it through every way and come out at the same place every time, which was that she'd do what she had to and pay for it after.

There wasn't a barricade.

---

There had been. On the far bank, past the ford. The oak trunks were in the ditch at the side of the road, three deep, where something had dragged them. The ditch across the road had planks over it. Wide enough for a cart.

And beyond the planks, on the road, where it went in under the first of the oaks, a woman on a horse.

Small. Neat. Iron-grey hair cut short. A white surcoat with a long darn down one side. A big grey horse with a white blaze, standing very still.

Behind her, on both sides of the road, men. Knights on greyharts and crown men on foot. In two lines. Facing each other across the road. As if they were waiting for a wedding to come down it.

---

Maren took the lion over the ford and up to the planks, and stopped.

Orrun stopped beside her.

Behind them, the column came up to the river and stopped too, slowly, a league and a half of it, as a long train of carts stops, the front first and then the rest bunching up. She could hear it behind her. Ten thousand people going quiet.

Rowan looked at her.

---

"You're outlawed," said Rowan.

Plain. Not loud. It carried.

"By name. Five hundred crowns. The council made it, and I signed it." She didn't look away. "In Hallowmere. On the thirty-third day."

"I know you did," said Maren.

Rowan's face didn't change.

"And I'm Marshal of the Border," said Rowan. "Since the forty-second. The road's in my keeping."

She turned her horse side-on. Across the road's edge, on the far side, at the end of the right-hand line of men. So that the road was open in front of her, down the lane between the lines, and into the oaks.

---

Maren looked at the road.

Then she put her hand on Orrun's neck, and he lay down.

Across the near side. At the end of the left-hand line, on the planks' edge. On the road. His great head toward the oaks and his tail back toward the river. A wall on one side of the road, and a woman on a horse on the other.

Between them, the way in.

---

They came all afternoon.

She stood by Orrun's shoulder and watched them come. She watched faces. A boy asleep on a load of tent-poles with his mouth open. An old man with a hen under his arm. Tova's carts first, up out of the ford, with the old people. Then the mares, with the children. Then the herd-men on foot with their poles, and the stonehorns, huge, grey, slow, with their plated shoulders brushing the planks' edges and their horns lowered, going past the lion's head one by one and turning their eyes to him and going on. Then the Keepers in their green. Then the wolves, coming in off the flanks at a throat-sound from Rhun and slipping down the lane between the crown men's boots like water. Then more carts. Then more people.

The crown men stood in their lines and watched them go by. A woman with a baby on her hip held it up to show a pikeman, for no reason, and the pikeman looked at the baby, and nodded.

---

Rowan didn't move.

All afternoon. On her horse at the other side of the road. Maren could see her across the column, between the carts, and then not, and then again. Sitting very straight. Watching the clans come in.

Neither of them said anything.

Not across the road. Not when the light went. Not when the crown men lit torches along the lines so the tail of the column could see the planks, and the tail came in, the last carts and the last herd-men and the mother with the empty hands, walking at the very end, in the dark.

Then the road was empty.

---

Tova had waited.

At the oaks' edge, on her bay mare. She'd been first in and she'd sat there the whole afternoon and watched every one of them come past, and when the last was in she rode back a little way, toward the planks, and looked across the road at the knight on the grey horse.

"Is that your captain?" she said.

"She's the crown's marshal," said Maren.

Tova looked a while longer.

"She looks like a captain," she said.

---

Rowan came to the fire later.

Tova's fire, in a glade a quarter-league into the oaks, with the clans' fires going away from it in every direction through the trunks, hundreds of them, under the leaves. She came on foot, alone, and stood at the edge of the light until Tova looked up and moved over on the log, and then she sat.

She gave them her terms. To Tova. Not to Maren.

---

The Greenwold was the crown's hunting forest.

Nobody had hunted it in a year. There was grass in the glades under the oaks: grey, like everywhere, but grass. The clans were to keep inside the wood and off the farmland to the west of it. That was the crown's line now, the western edge of the Greenwold. The farmers beyond it were the crown's and hungry already.

"There's no grain," said Rowan. "I'll not lie to you. The council has none to send and wouldn't send it."

Tova waited.

"But the aurochs are the crown's," said Rowan. "And the crown isn't using them."

---

Tova looked at her.

"We'll take the aurochs," she said.

"Not all of them," said Rowan.

Something went across Tova's furrowed old face that might have been the beginning of a smile. It didn't get far.

"Not all of them," she agreed.

---

Maren sat across the fire from Rowan and didn't speak.

Rowan didn't look at her. She talked to Tova about the glades and the water and where the Keepers' wolves should run so as not to frighten the farmers, and about the dead, and where the clans could bury them, which was anywhere they liked, she said, it was a forest.

The jetty road was there. At the fire. Maren could feel it there. The morning on the jetty road with the knights watching, and Rowan's back.

Neither of them said it.

---

Rowan got up to go.

She stood at the edge of the light, as she'd come, and looked at Maren then. Once.

"Your feet," she said.

Maren looked at her.

"You're walking on them as if they were someone else's," said Rowan. "Since the planks. I watched you." She turned to go. "See to them."

She went off through the oaks toward the road.

Maren looked at the fire.

*She watched you,* said Orrun, from the dark behind her. *All afternoon.*

"I know."

*You watched her.*

"I know," said Maren.

---

Dickon came on the forty-ninth.

A boy from the Staithe. Twelve. On one of the ferry ponies, a fat brown thing with a white nose that he'd ridden two days up the River Road alone, asking the way, with a satchel on his back. He found the clans' fires by riding into the oaks till a wolf stood up in front of him, and then he sat on the pony and didn't move until a Keeper came.

They brought him to Maren with the satchel still on his back.

"From the old man," he said. "Liss wrote it. He said it had to be you."

---

Liss's hand.

Big. Round. Careful. A ferrywoman's letters, every one made separately as if each was a rope being tied. Idony couldn't hold a pen. Davin had gone back up to Hallowmere to his tribunes. So Liss wrote for him now at her kitchen table, as she'd written at the end of the last winter, slowly, while he said it.

Maren read it by the fire with Tova and Rhun and Dickon watching her.

---

*Maren.*

*He says to tell you they've divided.*

*The bell one first. It turned off on its own on the 25th day. He saw one of the leans swing off south on its own and didn't know what it was for two days.*

*Then the other five. All at once. On the 41st day at noon. He says the six leans went still for a long breath, and then each of them leaned a different way.*

*He worked out which was which from the charts and how they lean. He sat up two nights. I made him sleep the third.*

---

*The cold one, the coil, is going north. Toward Isvaren.*

*The hot one, the one with the chain on it, is going west and north. Toward Kessmoor.*

*The bright one is going west and south. Toward Sarovel.*

*The one with the forest is going back east. Toward the Wyldmarch.*

*The bell one's going to the sea. He says the farmer will know about that.*

---

*He says to tell you this plain.*

*The big one, the last one, is coming west. Toward here. Toward Hearthmere.*

*He says they're each going home. Each to the ground it slept under. And its home is under you.*

*He says he's sorry it's slow.*

*Liss.*

*P.S. The lights are gone off the mountains. He says that's how you know.*

---

She read it to the end.

Then she gave it to Tova, who couldn't read, and read it again aloud, for Tova and Rhun. The fires went on round them through the oaks. Hundreds of them. Ten thousand people who'd walked a hundred leagues away from the thing in the east.

And the thing in the east had turned round, and was coming after them.

Not after them. She knew that. It didn't know they were there. It was going home.

Home was here.

---

Tova was quiet a long time.

"Then we've walked the wrong way," she said at last.

"No," said Maren. "You've walked the only way there was. It was coming here anyway. It always was." She folded Liss's letter. "Now there's a road out the other side of it. West. And a marshal on the road."

Tova looked at her.

"And you?"

"I don't know," said Maren.

---

The Kessmoor rider came on the fiftieth day.

Down the Greenwold road from the west, through the oaks, on a big black horse. Not bonded. She could tell by how it went, steady and stupid and paying the wolves no attention at all. A man in black and grey with a brass clasp at his collar. He rode to the crown men's fire at the planks, and then Rowan sent a knight to fetch Maren, and she went.

He had sheets. Printed. Kessmoor printed things.

He handed one to Rowan and one to Tova and one to Maren, and bowed, the same to each, and got back on his horse.

---

*To every kingdom of Winshire. To the crown's council of Hearthmere, the Tide Council of Saltreach, the Clan of the Scarfell, the clans of the Wyldmarch, the College of Isvaren, the regency of Sarovel. To the lion's knight, and to the dragons' farmer.*

*The Hall of Measures at Ironhythe. The sixtieth day.*

*Kessmoor will host.*

*Agathe Pike, Master Engineer.*

---

Maren looked at hers.

*To the lion's knight.* Not her name. Kessmoor had printed round it. A kingdom that didn't want to be told it was inviting an outlaw, inviting her anyway. She almost laughed.

Rowan's said *the Marshal of the Border, for the crown.*

Rowan read it and folded it once along its length and put it inside her surcoat.

"Go," she said.

---

"You'll go?" said Maren.

"I'm the crown's marshal," said Rowan. "The crown will send me, because nobody else will go." She looked down the road west, through the oaks. "And the crown will want its marshal where the kingdoms are, and its outlaw where it can't see her." She looked at Maren. "Go to Ironhythe. Be the lion's knight. Kessmoor's found you a name."

Tova was reading her sheet upside down, gravely.

"I'll go," said Tova. "For the Wyldmarch. Somebody should."

---

Dickon had a tale.

He told it at the fire that night, with his mouth full of aurochs, because a boy who'd ridden two days alone had a tale whether he had one or not. He'd had it from a courier at the Wend crossing, he said, who'd had it from a bargeman, who'd come up from the coast.

A gale off Corsa. Ten days back. The herring fleet out, and the gale coming, and the fleet running for the harbour. And the harbour chain up across the mouth between the towers, because the winch had broken in the wind and nobody could lower it.

"And the green dragon came," said Dickon. "The farmer's. And she took the chain in her claws, out of the sea, and held it up. Up over the water. For an hour. In the gale. And the boats all ran in under it."

He looked round at them all.

"That's what the bargeman said."

---

Maren looked at the fire.

She could see it. She didn't know if it was true. Lisska, in a gale, with a chain in her claws, that went down in the sea on both sides of her and must have weighed more than a hill. Holding it up. Out of temper, probably. Saying so.

The farmer would be on the tower. Shouting at her to let go.

She didn't say anything.

*She's too far,* said Orrun. *I can't hear her. I've tried.*

"I know," said Maren. "She's on the sea."

---

She went down to the planks after, in the dark.

Rowan was there. At the crown men's fire, alone, with the printed sheet on her knee, reading it again by the light. She looked up when Maren came, and didn't get up.

Maren stood at the fire's edge.

"Thank you," she said. "For the road."

"It isn't yours," said Rowan. "It's theirs." She looked back at the sheet. "And I'll be the one who has to answer for it."

"You always are."

"Yes," said Rowan.

---

Maren went back up the road into the oaks.

Behind her, the ford, and the moor beyond it. Grey, open, empty, going away east under the stars. Somewhere at the far end of them, beyond the Ysse, beyond the Plains, something as big as a hill had turned round, and was walking home.

She walked into the trees. Orrun beside her.

Her feet were cold. She could feel them.
