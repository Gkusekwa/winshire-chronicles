---

He was late.

He knew it from the air. The clock told him. He came in over the chimneys of Ironhythe on Kharrak an hour after noon on the sixtieth day, with Lisska off his left wing, and the clock was there in front of him on its rise above the smoke, white-faced, black-handed, telling the whole valley it was an hour past.

*It's looking at us,* said Lisska.

"It's a clock."

*It's looking,* said Lisska.

---

Two days from Corsa.

West along the coast, and then up the Coldrun where it came down out of the Fells, over the canals, over the locks and the lift-bridges and the barges on their tow-ropes. He'd left Ulric at Corsa with the forty pairs, and Aurveth at the farm in the top field with the Gullhaven families, and come with the two of them because the paper had said *the dragons' farmer* and that seemed to mean dragons.

He'd stared at the paper a long time at Corsa. Printed. Every letter the same as every other. Nobody at Corsa had ever seen print except on the Kessmoor bills of lading. Marrow had turned hers over and looked at the back.

---

Ironhythe was smoke.

He'd been told. He hadn't believed it. A hundred chimneys and more, going up out of a valley at the Fells' foot like a wood of dead trees, and the smoke off them going up in columns into the cold and spreading out under the sky in a brown roof. Under it, the sheds. Long and iron-roofed. Row after row. The canals between them, black. The streets.

And lamps. On every corner. Green. Lit, at noon. Glass globes on iron posts with green fire in them, steady, not flickering.

*Not the Deep,* said Lisska. *Something else. Something that smells.*

---

They came down in the Hall's yard.

A great square of cobbles behind the Hall of Measures, full of carriages and horses and grooms. The horses screamed. The grooms didn't run. They caught the horses' heads and held on and looked at the dragons with the faces of men working out how much a dragon weighed.

A clerk came out of a side door with a brass rule.

He walked up to Kharrak. Calder watched him. He walked down Kharrak's length with the rule, laying it end to end on the cobbles beside him, and wrote in a book. Then he looked up at the wing.

"Would he open it?" said the clerk. "For the record."

Kharrak opened it.

The clerk measured it and wrote it down.

---

The doorkeeper wrote him down too.

At the great door. In a ledger, with a pen. *The dragons' farmer. One hour and four minutes after noon.* He looked up at the clock tower to get the minutes right, and wrote them, and blotted them.

"You're expected, sir," he said.

---

The Hall of Measures was long and grey and cold.

Grey stone walls. Iron trusses in the roof, painted black, holding it up in long arches like ribs. And at the far end, behind a wall of glass, the clock's works.

He stopped in the doorway and looked at them.

Wheels. Brass. Hundreds of them, all sizes, turning. Some fast, some too slow to see. Teeth into teeth. And hanging from the middle of it all, down through a slot in the floor of the works and on down the whole height of the glass wall almost to the hall's floor, a pendulum. As long as a ship's mast. With a brass weight on its end the size of a cart-wheel. Going back. Going forward. Slow. So slow you could have walked beside it.

He could hear it. Every swing. *Tock.*

---

And round the walls, in glass cases, brass.

Rules. Weights. A brass yard on velvet, and a brass foot, and a brass inch, very small. Weights in a row from the size of a barrel to the size of a seed. A brass jar marked *GALLON*. An hourglass as tall as a child.

Kessmoor's measures. The ones everything else in Kessmoor was measured against.

He didn't know why it made him want to laugh. He didn't.

---

The table was round.

He'd never seen one. A round table. A great ring of dark wood in the middle of the hall, with chairs all round it, and nobody at its head because it hadn't got one. The gallery ran round three sides of the hall above, crowded. Guildsmen. Clerks. People.

At the table, the kingdoms.

And two seats with printed cards in front of them. *The lion's knight.* *The dragons' farmer.*

The lion's knight's chair had someone in it.

---

She didn't look round.

She'd heard him. The whole hall had heard him; you didn't walk into a quiet hall with two dragons outside it and nobody hear. She sat in her chair in a plain grey coat that wasn't a knight's, with her hands on the table, and looked at the clock.

He walked round the table to the other card. It was next to hers. Kessmoor had put the two outsiders together.

He sat down.

"You're late," said Maren. Without looking at him.

"The clock told me," said Calder.

---

He knew most of them.

Rowan, for the crown, small and neat in her mended surcoat, sitting very straight. Thale hadn't come. Marrow in her blue silk, for the Tide Council, and beside her Hal Rennick for the Bench, with his gold ring in his ear and his boots, for once, on the floor. Arn, for the Clan. Wren behind his chair with her hands folded.

Tova for the Wyldmarch. He knew her from the scouts' talk. A furrowed old woman with her grey hair cropped like a man's and root-knotted hands, sitting as if the chair were a horse she didn't trust.

A thin cold woman in Isvaren's blue for the College. Master Beatrix Quell. Calder didn't know her. Arn told him later that Vael's owls had gone thin since the spring and nobody at the College would say why.

A tall fair woman in Sarovel's white, very upright. Lady Ismay Aurel, for the regency. And beside her, in a grey smock with ink on his fingers, a young man who didn't look as if he'd ever sat in a chair like that in his life. Tribune Mattias, for the unchosen.

---

And Kessmoor.

A small bald man with a ledger under his hand. First Guildmaster Edmund Kaye. He didn't speak much. When he did, the hall went quiet to hear him, because he spoke quietly.

And beside him, tall and heavy-shouldered, with grey hair cropped short and smoked spectacles pushed up on her forehead and soot in the creases of her knuckles, Master Engineer Agathe Pike.

She looked at Calder across the round table as if she were measuring him for something.

---

It took four days.

It would have taken longer anywhere else, Arn said. In Kessmoor they did it to the clock. Each sitting began on the hour, and ended on the hour, and in between Kaye's clerk read out what each kingdom was to give, in a high clear voice, and they argued, and then they signed.

He'd not known you could argue to a clock. You could.

---

Sarovel gave its knights.

Lady Ismay Aurel stood and said it. Sarovel had ranked its people by their bonds for three hundred years. The chosen above the unchosen. A knight was a knight because a beast had chosen him. Now there were not enough of the chosen left, and the beasts that had chosen them were dead or broken or gone wild.

"Sarovel will take unbonded knights," she said. "The unchosen will carry lances."

Tribune Mattias didn't stand.

"And be paid as knights," he said.

Lady Ismay looked down at him. He looked up at her. Nobody in the hall moved.

"And be paid as knights," said Lady Ismay.

She signed it.

---

Kessmoor gave its ban.

Kaye said it himself. Forty years since the Scald. Since Orrin burned. Forty years Kessmoor had forbidden the Bleed: the thing everyone else called the Deep. No words. No workers. No charms. A kingdom of engines that had lived on what it could measure.

"For the duration of the war," said Kaye, "the Bleed may be used in Kessmoor. Under licence."

"And measured," said Pike.

Kaye looked at her.

"And measured," he said.

---

Saltreach gave its captains.

That was the strangest. Rennick sat at a table with a crown's marshal and a regent of Sarovel and a master of Isvaren's College, and laughed. Not loudly. To himself. At the joke of it. A pirate who held the Saltings' channels, at the same table as the kingdoms whose ships he took.

"The Bench will stand with crowns," said Marrow. "For the war."

"For the war," said Rennick. "And they'll pay us."

"We'll pay you," said Kaye.

Marrow signed for the houses. Rennick signed for the Bench: a mark, and his thumb pressed in ink beside it, with the print of his gold ring on the thumb's side, which he did on purpose.

---

And the Wyldmarch.

Tova didn't stand either. She sat with her root-knotted hands flat on the table and looked round it. At Kessmoor. At Isvaren. At Sarovel. At the crown. Slowly. At each of the kingdoms that had come into the Wyldmarch for beasts, for three hundred years, with nets and chains and binding-lamps.

"The Wyldmarch will trust you," she said. Slowly. Weighing each word. "We have nobody else to trust."

She put one finger on the table.

"One thing," she said. "No more heartstones. From living beasts. Not one. Not a bird."

She looked at Pike.

---

Pike looked back at her a long time.

Calder had heard about the cage at the Scald. Everybody had. Three canals round the Furnace, chilled, and the engines that chilled them, and what the engines burned. Hearts. Beasts' hearts, cut out living and set in iron so the Deep in them could be drawn off like water out of a cask.

Pike pushed her spectacles further up on her forehead.

"Accepted," she said. "We'll find other fuel."

"You said that very fast," said Tova.

"I measure fast," said Pike.

Tova looked at her a moment longer. Then she signed. A mark: a little horse.

---

The clause came on the sixty-fourth.

Last. After the costs. Kaye's clerk read it in the same high clear voice he'd read everything in, so that it sounded like everything else.

*All Titan-matter and relics recovered in the course of the war shall pass into the keeping of the Assembly of Kessmoor, for study and safe custody, at the war's end.*

He looked up from the paper.

Nobody said anything.

---

Calder thought about it.

Titan-matter. Plates. Bones. Hearts. Whatever was left of a thing as big as a hill when it was dead, if anything ever killed one. And the relics. The six in the Last Titan's back, grown into it, that Maren had seen.

If there was an end to the war, and anything left to keep, Kessmoor could keep it.

There wouldn't be.

---

"If there's an end," said Rennick, "they can have the lot."

Somebody in the gallery laughed.

Marrow signed. Rowan signed. Arn signed without reading it, because he couldn't, and didn't ask anyone to read it again. Quell signed. Lady Ismay signed and Mattias signed under her.

The clerk brought it round to the two printed cards.

---

Calder signed his name.

Under *the dragons' farmer*. Slowly. With the whole table waiting and the pendulum going *tock* behind the glass. CALDER FENN. Every letter on its own. The D the right way round.

He pushed it along to Maren.

She signed it. *Maren Ashdown.* In her square hand, under *the lion's knight*. Her own name, that the crown had put a price on.

He saw Rowan see it. He saw Rowan look at the clock.

---

Then Maren said it.

That afternoon. When the clause was signed and the costs were signed and Kaye had asked, quietly, whether any member had other business before the sitting closed on the hour.

She stood up.

---

She told them about the West.

Plainly. As she'd have reported to a captain. The lion's memory: that the ones who'd set him at his door, long ago, before the chaining, had gone *into the light*. West. Over the sea. Where the sun went down. The tide-reader's chart: the old realm's chart, with a mark in its south-western margin, and *error* written beside it in six different hands, because six different scholars had thought it couldn't be right. The breath of the world, drunk in the east by the six as they woke. Drunk in the west long ago. And something in the south-west, past the edge of every chart, that had never been drunk at all.

"Whatever put them to sleep the first time," she said, "went there. Over the sea." She looked round the table. "We can't win this with what we've got. We can hold. For a while. Then we lose. I want to go and find what put them to sleep."

---

The hall was quiet.

The pendulum went *tock*.

Then Rennick laughed. Not unkindly. As a man laughs at a child who's said she'll catch the moon.

"Over the sea," he said. "Past the Shatters. Where nobody's ever come back from."

"Somebody has," said Maren. "Or there'd be nothing in the margin."

---

And Calder said it.

He hadn't meant to. He'd meant to keep his mouth shut and fly home. But he sat there in the cold hall with the clock going and thought of the low harbour at Corsa, the hulks on the mud, the fires on their decks, two thousand people inside the chain, and the thing with the bell on its back dragging itself to the sea.

"It's a song," he said.

She turned her head and looked at him.

"It's a song," said Calder. "While people drown." He didn't look away. "There's a harbour at Corsa with two thousand people living on the mud, and the bell one's coming home to the sea, and nobody'll move them. And you want to go over the edge of the world after a mark in a margin."

He meant it. All of it.

She didn't flinch.

"Yes," said Maren.

---

Kaye answered for the table.

Quietly. As he did everything.

"Kessmoor's Western Deep ships are needed for the war," he said. "On the coast. At the canals' mouths. Every hull we have." He looked at her. "The Assembly can't spare a ship to sail past the Shatters on a mark in a margin. Nor can any kingdom at this table, I think." He looked round it. Nobody disagreed. "I'm sorry, knight."

"So am I," said Maren.

She sat down.

---

He saw the young man in the gallery then.

Above Kessmoor's seats. Among a crowd of men and women in grey smocks, labourers, with the look of people who'd walked a long way: Sarovel's hands, someone said, three thousand of them come up the road to work the foundries for bread. And in the middle of them a young man with his elbows on the rail, not looking at the table at all.

Looking up. At the clock.

At the white face and the black hands and the pendulum going back and forth over the floor, as if he were trying to work out how it knew.

Maren looked up at him. Once. Then down again.

Calder didn't know who he was.

---

The dragons were in the yard at dawn on the sixty-fifth.

Where they'd been for five days, among the carriages, with the grooms walking wide round them and the clerk with the brass rule coming out every morning to measure something new. Kharrak asleep on the cobbles. Lisska awake on a carriage-house roof.

And the lion in the yard with them. Lying along the wall. As big as Kharrak now. Bigger.

Lisska and Orrun had been talking all night. He could tell. Lisska had that look.

*I told him about the chain,* she said. *In the gale. He didn't believe me.*

*I believed her,* said Orrun.

*He told me about the stonehorns,* said Lisska. *At the river. I believed him.*

---

Maren came across the yard.

Alone. In her grey coat. She came to look at Lisska. She stood by the carriage-house wall and looked up at the green dragon on the roof, and Lisska looked down at her, and something went between them that Calder didn't hear.

Then she turned to go.

---

"Blackwater," said Calder.

She stopped.

He hadn't been asked. He said it anyway, to her back, standing by Kharrak's shoulder with his hand on the plates.

"The smugglers' inlet. Under the mountains' southern end. I was there before the winter. There's no law. There's reef-pilots: families that know the channels nobody else does. Some of them go further than they say." He looked at the cobbles. "Ask for a reef-pilot. And don't pay what they ask first."

---

She turned round.

"You said it was a song," said Maren.

"It is."

He looked up at the clock. White. Black hands. Telling the whole of Ironhythe it was a quarter past five in the morning, which nobody had asked it.

"Some songs are true," said Calder. "Doesn't stop people drowning while you sing them."

She looked at him a long moment.

"No," she said. "It doesn't."

---

They named Rowan at noon.

The last business. Kaye's clerk read it out: the Accord would need one marshal, over all its kingdoms' forces, for the war. Each kingdom was asked to name one. Each kingdom named Rowan. Quell first. Then Arn. Then Tova, who'd watched her sit a greyhart at the side of a road all afternoon. Then the rest.

Calder understood it. Hearthmere was too broken to frighten anybody. Its marshal couldn't make it a kingdom again by winning.

Rowan stood up.

"I'll do it," she said.

That was all.

---

They left by different doors.

He saw her go. Out of the Hall's west door, onto the canal road, with the lion getting up off the yard wall and going round the end of the building to meet her. West. Toward the coast. Toward the Worldsend's southern end and the inlet under it.

He went out the yard door, to the dragons.

Behind him, in the hall, the pendulum went *tock*, and the clock that had never been wrong told the kingdoms of Winshire that it was noon.
