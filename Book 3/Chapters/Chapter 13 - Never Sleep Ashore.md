---

The stone was still there.

Kit had known it would be. She went out to look at it anyway, on the sixtieth day, at the bottom of the spring tide, because that was what you did with a thing that was going to ruin you: you went and looked at it, so it knew you weren't afraid.

She was afraid. She looked at it anyway.

It lay across the throat of the Merrins' channel out of Blackwater, the only one deep enough for the *Kittiwake* to get out over the Shatters. While it lay there the Merrins had no salvage, and the money Ostrey had lent against the ketch would fall due all the same.

---

Jory rowed.

He was better at it than she was now. Nineteen, thin, brown as a nut, with their father's long hands. He took the boat out of the inlet's mouth and round under the black cliffs, through the Merrins' channel where it wound between the reefs, without once looking at the water. He didn't need to. He knew where the rocks were as he knew where his own feet were.

At the channel's throat he shipped the oars and let the boat drift.

They looked down.

---

There it was.

Under them. A yard under, at this tide. Grey. Square. The size of a chapel, lying on its side across the deep water at the channel's narrowest place, from one reef to the other, like a door shut across a passage.

Cut stone. Somebody had cut it, long before anybody living. Its top face was flat, and on the flat, under the clear water, she could see the hooked writing. The old script. Lines of it, worn. The same as on the milestones.

It had stood on the end of the drowned road's last pier for longer than Saltreach had been a word. Then at the end of the winter the sea had gone out of the bay at Gullhaven at noon and come back with the Bell in it, and every pier on the Shatters had shaken, and the last one had dropped its top stone into the Merrins' channel.

---

"It's not moved," said Jory.

"No."

"It's not going to."

"No," said Kit.

She leaned over the side and put her hand in the water. It was cold. She couldn't reach the stone. She held her hand there anyway, flat, as if she could.

Through the water, very faint, so faint she might have dreamed it, a hum.

---

They rowed back on the flood.

Into the inlet. Blackwater. She looked at it as they came round the point as she'd look at a ship she was thinking of buying, because she was thinking of losing it, and that was the same thing backwards.

Black cliffs, both sides, going straight up into cloud. Black water between, brown-black, stained with the peat that came down off the mountains in every stream. And at the inlet's head, the town. Tarred houses on stilts. Hundreds. Leaning on each other over the water like drunks, with ladders down to boats and walkways between them, and smoke out of every chimney.

Smugglers' ships at anchor in the deep water. A dozen. Sellswords' fires on the shingle beach below the cliffs. Nobody on the headland. No light. No harbour-master. No toll.

No chain.

---

Blackwater had no chain.

Every harbour on the coast had a chain across its mouth. Corsa. Wendmouth. Gullhaven, when there was a Gullhaven. Every ship paid chain money to have it lowered, going in and going out. That was Saltreach.

Blackwater didn't.

The Council had tried. Three times in her father's life the Council had sent a harbour-master and a chain to Blackwater. The first chain had gone missing in a week. The second had been sold back to the Council at Corsa by the men who'd taken it. The third harbour-master had been found in the inlet in the spring, floating, very peaceful.

The Council had stopped sending them.

---

On the north shore, the boathouse.

A great tarred shed over the water, with its sea-door hanging off and its roof gone. Torn off. Last winter. By dragons. The farmer's: the outlaw from the Marches with three dragons of his own and six of the Clan's behind him, who'd come down out of the dark one dawn and ripped the roof off the shed where some gentleman's binders were keeping three young dragons on chains.

The whole of Blackwater had watched from its stilt-houses.

Nobody had put the roof back. Nobody would rent a shed a dragon had opened. Kit thought it was the most useful thing that had ever happened in Blackwater, and the shed was the most honest building in the inlet.

---

The Merrin house was at the eastern end.

Tall. Stilts as thick as masts. Ochre shutters, the house colour, peeling. A walkway to the next house and a ladder down to the water, and under the house, between the stilts, roofed over, the hold.

The *Kittiwake* lay off it on her mooring.

Two-masted. Broad in the beam. Shallow. Built by her grandmother's money to sit down on a reef at low water and float off again on the flood, and carry stone. Not pretty. Kit had never seen a prettier thing in her life.

She went aboard. She slept aboard. She always had.

---

The inlet thought it was funny.

The Merrin woman, who had a house, a good house, and slept on her ship. In Blackwater. Where the worst thing that could happen to you ashore was a knife, and the worst thing that could happen afloat was a squall.

She let them think it was funny.

It was a rule. Not even the rule for here: the rule for over there, and she'd kept it so long she couldn't stop keeping it.

*Never sleep ashore over there.*

---

Her great-grandfather had made it.

Abram Merrin. A reef-pilot, like every Merrin since. Who'd gone out over the Shatters one summer eighty years ago, further than anyone went, chasing a wreck that wasn't where it should have been. And found the road.

The drowned road. Piers of old stone, broken off, sticking up out of the sea every few leagues, going away south-west. Each one an island now, with birds on it. Each one with a stone on top like a milestone, with the hooked writing on it and a number.

He'd followed them. Island to island. Milestone to milestone.

And at the end of them, a shore.

---

She'd been there.

Six times. With her father. Twice since, alone, with Jory.

She didn't think about it in words. Merrins didn't. They didn't talk about it at the table, or at the fire, or at all. You learned it in the boat. Where the road went. How to read the stones. What to bring back: the grey stone, the humming kind, in blocks off the old buildings, that the Merrins chipped into flakes and sold through a Saffery man on Gallows Quay at Wendmouth as warranted old charms.

One in ten did something. Nobody knew why. Nobody knew which. People bought them anyway, at two silver, because one in ten was better than any charm a contract-worker would sell you.

And the rule. Abram's rule. That every Merrin kept and none of them talked about.

---

Her father had kept it thirty years.

Hal Merrin. Who'd taught her the road from her tenth summer. Who'd never slept a night ashore over there. Who'd drowned on the road in a squall eleven years ago, at the ninth milestone, when the ship's boat went over on the reef with him in it, and the *Kittiwake* was a cable off, and there was nothing she could do except watch.

She'd brought the ketch home alone. With Jory. Who was eight, and sat on the stone ballast in the hold the whole way and didn't cry, and read her the milestones going back, one by one, by eye.

He'd never needed the book. She still did.

---

Ostrey came on the sixty-second.

Master Orme Fosse. Ostrey's factor at Blackwater: the only house in the League that kept a factor here, because Ostrey insured half the cargoes that went out of this inlet and didn't ask what they were.

He came along the walkway to the Merrin house in the morning in his grey coat, with Ostrey's grey cuff, and his round spectacles, and a paper. A soft, pleasant, round man. Who'd never in his life forgiven anyone anything, and was pleasant about it.

She met him on the walkway. She didn't ask him in.

---

"Mistress Merrin."

"Master Fosse."

He held out the paper. She knew what it was. She took it anyway.

*The House of Ostrey to the House of Merrin. The sum lent against the ketch* Kittiwake *falls due on the hundredth day. In default, the ketch and her gear pass to the House of Ostrey.*

Her name at the bottom, in her own hand, from the end of the winter. When the Bell had shut half the Shattered Sea and dropped a chapel into her channel and there'd been no salvage, and no money, and six crew to feed.

---

"Thirty-eight days," said Fosse. Pleasantly.

"I can count."

"I know you can, Mistress Merrin. The Merrins have always counted well." He looked past her, down through the walkway's slats at the water, and the hold under the house. "The house of Ostrey would like to say it's sorry. It isn't. But it would like to say it."

"Thank it for me."

Fosse smiled.

"And the house would still like to know," he said, as every Ostrey man had asked every Merrin for eighty years, "what it is your family goes so far to fetch."

"Rocks," said Kit.

"Rocks," said Fosse. "Of course."

He went back along the walkway.

---

Gannet watched him go.

From the *Kittiwake*'s rail, where he'd been splicing a line, and hadn't been. Her bosun. Fifty, perhaps. Thick. Wendmouth born, with a Wendmouth face, the kind that came to meet you before the rest of him did. He'd come east in the autumn, after the Hallorans fell and the Saffery house cleaned out the old Halloran beast men from Gallows Quay. He'd come to Blackwater because it had no questions in it.

Kit had taken him on because he could handle a ship, and lie to an Ostrey man, and both were worth money.

She trusted him exactly as far as his contract. Not a finger further.

"Thirty-eight days," said Gannet. The smile arriving first.

"You heard."

"I hear everything," said Gannet. "That's what you pay me for."

---

Jory wanted to go.

That night, aboard, at the table in the stern cabin, with the lamp swinging. He said it as he always said things, quietly, to the table, not to her.

"We could go out. By the eastern channels. Round."

"The eastern channels are shut. The Bell shut them."

"Not all. The fourth one's open. I looked."

"The fourth one's two fathoms at the bar," said Kit. "She draws two and a half with ballast. Without ballast she'll go over in the first swell past the Shatters. You know that."

He knew it. He looked at the table.

"Then we lose her," he said.

---

"Then we lose her," said Kit.

She looked round the cabin. Her father's charts in their rack. Her grandmother's sea-chest under the bunk. The lamp. The old tin luck-tag nailed over the door, that some Wendmouth charm-seller had sold her father thirty years ago, that had never worked in its life.

And on the shelf by the lamp, the book.

The Merrin book. Abram's. The milestones, every one, drawn in his hand, and their numbers, and the turns between them. Four generations of corrections in the margins. Her father's hand. Her own.

Not Jory's. He'd never needed to write in it.

---

She thought about the feather that night.

She didn't often. It came when she was afraid of money.

She'd been ten. The *Kittiwake* hadn't had a keel yet; she'd been a hull on the stocks on the beach below the house, with no money to finish her. And one night her grandmother had come down the ladder to the beach with a box.

Wenna Merrin. Small, fierce, black-eyed, with a voice like a gull's. She'd opened the box by the fire on the beach. And in it, on a scrap of old sail-cloth, a feather.

---

Long as a child's arm. Red, going to gold at the tip, going to something brighter than gold that Kit's eyes couldn't hold.

Warm.

Her grandmother had let her hold it. Once. That night. In both hands, on the sail-cloth. It was warm all the way through. Warm as a hearthstone the morning after the fire's gone out. She'd held it and felt it breathe, almost, against her palms, and then her grandmother had taken it back and shut the box.

"Where did it come from?"

Her grandmother had looked at her a long time.

"From where we go," she'd said. And then nothing else, ever.

---

She'd sold it the next week to the Halloran house.

At Wendmouth. For the keel. Kit had seen it once more, two years later, from the quay, on a velvet cushion in a glass case in the window of the Halloran counting-house on Gallows Quay. Rusk's window. Not for sale. Just there. So that everyone who came by could see that the Hallorans owned something nobody else did.

She'd stood on the quay and looked at it through the glass and not told anyone it had been hers.

Her father wouldn't talk about it either. She'd asked him, once, on the road. He'd looked at the sea.

*Never sleep ashore over there,* he'd said. As if that were an answer.

---

The gull-hawk came on the sixty-sixth.

Into Blackwater at noon from the west, low over the water, to the perch on Fosse's roof. Ostrey's bird. From Corsa. Fosse's boy took the tube off its leg and ran it in.

By dark the whole inlet knew, though Fosse hadn't said a word. A Corsa smuggler had come in on the same wind with a hold full of something he wasn't saying, and drunk at the sellswords' fire on the beach, and talked.

---

Kit was at the fire.

With Gannet, and Jory, and two of her crew. She'd come ashore to buy salt pork and stayed to hear, because you stayed when a Corsa man was talking. He had the voice for it. He'd had it from the Ostrey clerks, he said, who'd had it from the Council.

The kingdoms had signed a thing. At Ironhythe. In Kessmoor. An Accord. Every crown, and the Clan, and the clans out of the Wyldmarch, and the Tide Council, and even the Bench: Hal Rennick of the Saltings had put his thumb to it.

"And the lion's knight was there," said the Corsa man. "Her that's outlawed in Hearthmere. And the dragons' farmer. Sat side by side."

Somebody whistled.

---

"And she stood up," said the Corsa man. "The lion's knight. At the end. And asked for a ship."

He looked round the fire, enjoying it.

"To sail west. Past the Shatters. Past the edge. Where nobody comes back from." He drank. "To find what put the beasts to sleep the first time. That's what she said. The Ostrey clerk had it word for word."

"And?"

"And Kessmoor said no," said the Corsa man. "Every ship's wanted for the war. And Rennick laughed in her face." He wiped his mouth. "And the farmer said it was a song."

---

Kit didn't move.

She sat on her driftwood log with the salt pork in her lap and looked at the fire, and didn't look at Jory, and felt him not look at her.

*West. Past the Shatters.*

Beside her, Gannet laughed.

---

Not loudly. The smile arrived. She saw it come up his face in the firelight as it always did, first, before anything else.

"Ashdown," he said.

The Corsa man looked at him.

"The lion's knight. Her name's Ashdown." Gannet poked the fire. "I know her. Or I knew her. Before the lion. When she was a student up at Greyhold in Greyhold blue, with a practice sword." He poked the fire again. "She took a cub off me. On the jetty at Millbrook. Two years back. The day before the harvest fair."

"A cub?"

"A duskcat," said Gannet. "Young. Bill of sale and everything. Worth a gold piece to a gentleman in Wendmouth who kept a menagerie." He smiled at the fire. "She knocked the pin out of the cage with a practice sword and let it go into the reeds. In front of the whole village."

---

He was quiet a moment.

The fire cracked. Out on the black water the smugglers' ships rode at anchor, lanterns at their sterns.

"I haven't forgotten," said Gannet.

He said it pleasantly. Like a man telling you the price of salt.

---

Kit wasn't thinking about the cub.

She was thinking about the lion.

Everybody on the coast had heard of the lion. A lion that had been a mountain lion once, people said, and was the size of a house now, and bigger every time somebody told it. That lay down across roads. That held up hills.

That moved stone.

---

She looked out past the fire. Past the ships. Past the inlet's mouth, where the black cliffs stood out into the dark sea, and the Merrins' channel wound away between the reefs, and at its throat, a yard down at low water, lay a chapel's worth of old grey stone across the only road out.

Thirty-four days.

"Jory," she said.

He looked at her.

"Get the book out," said Kit. "In the morning. I want to look at the turns past the channel. All of them." She stood up with the salt pork. "And find out where Ironhythe is from here. In days. By lion."

Gannet looked up at her from the fire. The smile didn't move at all.
