---

The inlet saw him at dusk.

The whole of it. At once. She felt it see him. They came down the cliff path in the last of the light on the seventy-sixth day, Orrun first, the old man and she on his back, and the path was narrow and steep and cut in black rock, and Orrun was too big for it. He came down it anyway. Stones went off the edge under his paws and fell a long way into black water.

Below, the town.

---

She'd heard about Blackwater from the farmer.

In a handful of words, at Ironhythe, by a dragon. He hadn't said it was like this. Black cliffs going up into cloud. Black water. Houses on legs, tarred, crowded round the inlet's head, leaning on each other over the water. Lanterns coming on in the windows. Ships at anchor.

And on the shingle below the path, fires. Men round them. Sellswords. A lot of them.

They looked up.

---

She heard it go round the inlet.

A shout first. Then another, further off. Then the sound she'd remember: ladders. All round the inlet's head, one after another, people pulling up the ladders of the stilt-houses. A clatter, and then the next house, and the next, going away round the water in the dusk like a stick drawn along a fence.

Out in the deep water the ships slipped their moorings. She saw them do it. Men running on decks. Cables going. Sails half up. Standing off into the middle of the inlet, as far from the shingle as the inlet would let them go.

On the beach the sellswords left their fires.

Walking backwards. Fast.

---

Orrun came down off the last of the path onto the shingle.

He walked to the fires. The sellswords had gone. He looked at the fires a while, as if wondering what they were for. Then he lay down among them, carefully, between them, so as not to put a paw in one.

He put his chin on his front paws.

*It's very black,* he said. *The water. Like the river under the hill.*

"I know."

*They're frightened.*

"Yes."

*Good,* said Orrun, and closed his eyes.

---

Behind her, the old man laughed.

Idony. On Orrun's back still, tied on with the strap Liss had made him, his thin shaking hands round her waist. He'd laughed most of the way from the ferry house. At nothing much. At the country. At being out in it.

"Ladders," he said. "They pulled up the ladders. Against a lion." He laughed again, and coughed, and laughed. "Oh, Maren. That's the best thing I've seen since Vael."

She undid the strap and got him down.

---

Eleven days.

Out of Ironhythe on the sixty-fifth by the canal road south-east, and then off it, across country. Orrun at his long lope, the one he could keep up all day. She'd never asked him how far. Twenty leagues in a day. More. She'd sat on his back with her eyes shut against the wind and let him go.

To the river first. To the ferry house. On the sixty-ninth.

Liss on the jetty. The old man on the bench by her door with everything he owned beside him, packed. The founding chart in its oilcloth. Two of his tide-glasses wrapped in wool. A bundle of Liss's letters to read again.

He'd been sitting there two days, Liss said. Waiting. He'd seen her coming in the glasses, he said. He hadn't. He'd guessed.

---

"Bring him back," Liss had said, on the jetty. Low. While Idony was being got up onto the lion. "He eats nothing. He'll talk you deaf."

"I'll bring him back."

"Bring yourself back while you're at it," said Liss.

She'd turned and gone into her kitchen before Maren could answer.

---

Then south. Down the Wend's west bank. Across at the Staithe. East along the Coast Road. Round Wendmouth by night, because of Saffery's gate and the bounty men. Past the empty hill where Gullhaven had been: the sea grey and quiet below it, the spit gone, the houses gone, nothing on the hill at all but the grey grass and a chapel door painted blue lying on its face.

Round Corsa inland, by the Saltreach Track.

She didn't go into Corsa. She saw it from a hill on the seventy-third. Tall, narrow, painted, round its harbour. Dragons over it. Small at that distance. Forty of them, turning.

She went round.

---

Now Blackwater.

She stood on the shingle among the abandoned fires with the old man leaning on her arm and the lion asleep at her feet, and looked at the dark houses with their ladders up, and the ships standing off in the inlet with their lanterns swinging, and the whole black place holding its breath.

She had three silver and eleven coppers.

And in her coat, in the inside pocket, heavy for its size, a small brass seal with a ring cut in its face, and six lines going into the ring.

---

Nobody came that night.

She made the fires up and got the old man warm and fed him half a loaf, which he complained about and ate. They slept against Orrun's side on the shingle. Out on the water the ships rode at anchor with men awake on their decks all night, watching.

---

Kit Merrin came at dawn.

Maren didn't go to find her. She came.

A boat. Rowing across the inlet from its eastern end, alone at first, a woman at the oars, and behind her a second boat with a man in it rowing slower. The first boat came straight in to the shingle below the lion and grounded, and the woman shipped her oars and got out into the shallows and walked up the beach.

Lean. Weathered. About forty. A nose that had been broken and set crooked. Black hair with grey in it, tied back. A sailor's coat. She walked up the shingle to the lion and stopped ten paces from him and looked at him.

She didn't look at him as the sellswords had. She looked as if he were a cargo. As if she were working out how much he weighed, and where in a hold you'd stow him, and whether the hold would take it.

---

"You're the one who wants to go west," said the woman.

Not a question.

"Yes," said Maren.

"I'm the one who can take you." The woman looked at Orrun a while longer. Then at Maren. Then at the old man sitting up against Orrun's side with a blanket round him. "Kit Merrin. Of the *Kittiwake*." She tipped her head at the inlet's eastern end. "That ketch off the house with the yellow shutters. The ugly one."

"Maren Ashdown."

"I know," said Kit Merrin. "The whole coast knows. They're saying it in Corsa now, that you asked Kessmoor for a ship and Kessmoor laughed." She looked at the lion. "Kessmoor's ships draw four fathoms and couldn't get past the second milestone. I can."

---

The second boat came in.

The man got out of it. Thick. Fifty, perhaps. A Wendmouth face. He came up the shingle behind Kit with his hands in his coat, smiling.

The smile arrived first. She knew it before she knew him.

Then she knew him.

"Mistress student knight," said Gannet.

---

The jetty at Millbrook. The cage. The pin.

A student in Greyhold blue. With a practice sword and her brother beside her with his mouth open, and a young duskcat in a cage with a cheap padlock on the pin, and this man on the jetty with two men behind him and his smile.

He looked older. Heavier. The smile hadn't changed at all.

"Gannet," she said.

"You remember." He was pleased. He looked at Orrun. "And you've grown a cat of your own since. A big one." He shook his head, admiring. "That cub was worth a gold piece to a gentleman in Wendmouth. Bill of sale and everything."

"It was worth more than that to the cub," said Maren.

The smile didn't move.

"My bosun," said Kit Merrin, without turning round. "He's good with a ship. Don't lend him anything."

---

They talked prices on the seventy-eighth.

Aboard. In the stern cabin of the *Kittiwake*, at a table with a lamp swinging over it, and charts in a rack, and an old tin luck-tag nailed over the door. Kit on one side. Maren on the other. Idony beside Maren on the bench, very upright, with his shaking hands flat on the table so they'd shake less. Gannet in the doorway.

Orrun on the deck. The whole deck. The *Kittiwake* sat lower in the water than she had, and Kit had looked at her waterline over the side when he lay down and said nothing at all.

---

Kit had done her sums.

Maren could see it. She'd done them before she rowed across. She had them in her head in a row, as Maren had her drills, and she laid them out on the table one at a time.

---

"First," said Kit. "Money."

"I haven't any."

"I know. Three silver, and coppers." Kit didn't smile. "Blackwater counts other people's money for fun. Everyone on the beach knew by noon." She put her finger on the table. "The *Kittiwake* is in debt to the House of Ostrey. The sum lent against her. It falls due on the hundredth day. Twenty-two days from now. If it isn't paid she's Ostrey's, and Ostrey will sell her to the Council for a coal-barge, and nobody goes west at all."

"I can't pay it."

"No," said Kit. "But the Accord can."

She looked at Maren's coat. At the inside pocket.

---

Maren took the seal out.

She put it on the table. Small. Brass. Heavy. The ring and the six lines.

"The whole coast knows about those too," said Kit. "Every seat at Ironhythe got one. Kessmoor's clerk said so to a Corsa clerk, who said so to a gull-hawk." She didn't touch it. "A warrant under that seal is drawn on the Accord's chest. For the war's necessary costs."

"I know what it's for."

"Then you know what the *Kittiwake* is," said Kit. "She's the only ship on the coast that can take the lion's knight where she says the war's going to be won." She leaned back. "Seal Ostrey's paper with that, and the Accord pays my debt. That's the first thing."

---

Maren looked at the seal.

*For the war's necessary costs.* Kaye's quiet voice. *The Assembly will honour it.*

*And keep a record of it.*

She thought about that. A record in Ironhythe, in a ledger, in a clerk's hand. The lion's knight's warrant, drawn for the debt of a Blackwater salvage ketch. Kessmoor would know. Anyone who could read Kessmoor's ledgers would know. Where she'd gone, and on what.

She thought about the low harbour at Corsa, and the farmer saying *it's a song*.

---

"Second," said Kit. "Labour."

She told her about the stone.

A block of old grey stone the size of a chapel, lying across the throat of the Merrins' channel a yard down at low water. Since the end of the winter, when the Bell came up. The Merrins' channel was the only deep road out of the inlet that went where the Merrins went. Without it the *Kittiwake* couldn't get out over the Shatters with ballast in her. Without ballast she'd go over in the first swell.

"I can't shift it," said Kit. "There's no crane in Blackwater that'll lift it, and no drawer who's any use on water, and no money to pay one if there were." She looked up through the cabin roof, at the deck, where the weight of the lion was. "They say your lion moves stone."

"He does."

"Then he moves that," said Kit. "Second thing."

---

"And third?"

Kit was quiet a moment.

"Passage there and back," she said. "Both ways. My ship, my crew, my road. And everyone aboard keeps the rule. You, the old man, the lion. My crew. Everyone."

"What rule?"

"*Never sleep ashore over there.*"

---

Maren waited.

"Why?" said Idony.

Kit looked at him. The first time she'd looked at him properly. The thin grey old man with his hands flat on her table.

"I don't know," she said. "My great-grandfather made it. Eighty years ago. My grandmother kept it and my father kept it and I keep it." She looked at the lamp. "My father kept it thirty years. He didn't drown of breaking it. He drowned of a squall. But he never told me why, and I never asked twice."

"And if somebody breaks it?" said Maren.

"Then they stay there," said Kit. "I don't take them home." She put her hands flat on the table too. "And the road stays secret. The Merrins' road. Whatever you see. That's the rest of the third thing."

---

Maren sealed it.

Kit had the paper. Ostrey's. With a space at the bottom where a guarantor could stand, because Ostrey's papers always had one. Kit held the lamp. Maren pressed the brass seal into the wax at the foot, hard, and lifted it.

A ring. Six lines going into it.

*The Accord, for the war's necessary costs. The lion's knight.* Kit wrote it under the seal herself, in a quick clerk's hand, and Maren signed below it. Her own name.

---

"Now salt," said Kit.

She went out to the galley and came back with a dish. A plain clay dish, chipped, with grey sea-salt in it, coarse, damp. She set it on the table between them.

"Saltreach doesn't hold with paper," she said. "Paper burns. This doesn't."

She took a pinch from the dish between finger and thumb. She held it.

"From the same dish," she said. "On the tongue. Then say it with me."

---

Maren took a pinch.

It was cold and wet and gritty. She put it on her tongue. It burned, a little. Kit did the same.

"*Salt between us,*" said Kit. "*What's sworn is kept, or neither of us is fit to drink with.*"

"*Salt between us,*" said Maren. "*What's sworn is kept, or neither of us is fit to drink with.*"

The salt went to nothing in her mouth. Kit swallowed. So did she.

"There," said Kit. "Now you're a Saltreach contract. Break it and you're unsalted. Nobody on this coast will sell you water."

---

The old man took a pinch too.

He wasn't party to it. He reached out with his shaking fingers and took a pinch out of the dish anyway, very carefully, and put it on his tongue, and shut his eyes.

Kit watched him. She didn't stop him.

"Third century," said Idony, with his eyes shut. "Before the League. Corsa's founding charter has it: *salt between us.* The same words. I read it at Vael when I was twenty-two. A copy of a copy." He opened his eyes. He was beaming. "I never thought I'd taste one."

Kit looked at him for a long moment.

"You're not party to it," she said.

"No," said Idony happily. "I'm a witness. Witnesses can taste. It's in the charter."

Kit looked at Maren.

"Is he always like this?"

"Yes," said Maren.

---

Gannet witnessed too. From the doorway.

He didn't take salt. Kit didn't ask him to. He stood in the door with his hands in his coat and watched it, smiling, and when it was done he went up on deck past the lion without a word.

---

The old man went down into the hold on the seventy-ninth and didn't come up.

The *Kittiwake*'s hold. Under the deck, aft, among the ballast. Kit had told him the ballast was far-stone: grey old blocks off the far shore's buildings, that the Merrins stowed low to keep the ketch stiff and sold in flakes on Gallows Quay. Idony had gone down the ladder as if it were a stair into a library.

She went down at noon to bring him bread.

He was sitting on a block of grey stone with one of his tide-glasses unwrapped on his knee, staring at it.

"Look," he said. Without looking up. "Look at it, Maren."

She looked. The glass was a glass. She couldn't read it.

"It's climbing," said Idony. "Here. In a hold. On the sea." He laid his shaking hand on the stone beside him. "It's never climbed on water in its life."

---

She spent the days on deck with Kit.

Talking prices. That was what Kit did instead of talking. The price of salt pork in Blackwater and in Corsa. The price of a berth. The price of a reef-pilot's word. The price of a lion's labour, which Kit set very high, so as to feel she'd got a bargain. Maren lost every argument. She didn't mind.

Her left hand stayed in her coat.

The fingertips had been grey since the night at the ford. Since before. She'd stopped counting from when. They were grey and they didn't feel the wood of the rail or the cold of the morning, and she kept them in her pocket on the brass seal where nobody could see, and Kit, who saw everything else, didn't say anything about it.

---

She went up on the cliff at dusk on the eightieth.

Alone. With Orrun. Up the path they'd come down, to the top, where the black rock stopped and the wind came off the sea. To look west along the coast. Toward the Saltings and Corsa and everything the farmer was trying to hold.

The light was going. Red over the sea. The coast going away west, low, grey, folded, to the edge of sight.

And far off along it, over the Saltings, a speck.

Green. Turning. In the last of the light. High, over the salt flats, alone. Turning and turning, as she did when she was looking for something on the ground.

---

Orrun lifted his head.

He looked at it a long time.

*It's her,* he said. *The green one.*

"Can you hear her?"

*No.* He kept looking. *Too far. She can't hear me. I can't hear her.* He watched the speck turn. *She's looking for something.*

"Him, probably."

*No,* said Orrun. *He's with her. She's looking at the sea. Something in it.*

The speck turned once more, and went down behind the coast's edge, into the dark.

---

They went back down the path in the dark.

Below them the inlet. The lanterns. The ladders, she saw, had been let down again, here and there, one at a time. Out on the water the *Kittiwake* rode at her mooring off the yellow-shuttered house, low in the water where the lion had lain, with a light in her stern cabin where an old man sat on a block of stone watching a glass climb.

Twenty days to the hundredth.

The salt was still on her tongue, a little. She could taste it.
