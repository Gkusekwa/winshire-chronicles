---

The chain was a black line on the water.

He saw it every morning from the signal-field. Across the harbour mouth, between the two round towers, a line drawn on the sea. When it was up it stood a foot clear of the water, black with tar, dripping, the links as long as his forearm. When it was down you couldn't see it at all. It lay on the harbour floor, and ships went over it.

They paid to go over it.

---

He went to see how.

On the sixty-seventh, the day he got back from Ironhythe. He went down to the eastern tower on foot and stood outside its window with the ship-masters, in the queue, and watched.

A window in the tower's foot, with an iron grille. Behind it, a clerk with a book. Behind the clerk, through a door, the winch-house: a great wooden capstan with twelve men on its bars, and the chain coming in through a slot in the wall and going round the drum. New iron on the drum. Gearmouth iron, a ship-master told him, after the gale, when the old drum split and the green dragon held the chain up.

"That was yours," the ship-master said. "The green one."

"Yes."

"I was outside," said the ship-master. "In the gale. I came in under her." He looked at Calder. "Thank you."

Calder didn't know what to say. He said nothing.

---

The ship-masters paid at the window.

Each one. Going in or going out. By weight. The clerk looked up the ship's name in his book, and the ship's weight beside it, and said a number, and the ship-master pushed silver under the grille. The clerk wrote it down. Then he rang a bell, and the twelve men on the capstan leaned, and the chain went down into the sea with a long iron roar, and the ship went out over it.

Then up again. Roaring.

Chain money.

---

The chain-master was a man called Cobb.

Old. Deaf. Exact. He sat at the back of the winch-house on a stool by the drum and watched the chain go round and counted the links under his breath as they came in, every time, and if the count was out by one he'd stop the capstan and look for the reason.

Calder asked him what the low harbour paid.

Cobb didn't hear him. Calder asked again, louder.

"Nothing," said Cobb. "Nothing in it goes anywhere."

---

The low harbour.

He'd counted it from the air. Forty-one hulks. On the mud inside the chain, behind the deep-water quays, at the harbour's back where the old ships went to die. On their sides. Leaning on each other. Washing on what was left of their rigging. Smoke from the fires on their decks.

Two thousand people and some.

He'd been to Marrow on the twenty-fourth day and asked her to empty it, and she'd asked the table, and the table had said *herring season* and not moved. He'd been to Ironhythe and said *it's a song while people drown* to a lion's knight in front of every kingdom in Winshire, and meant it.

And the harbour was still full.

---

Dorcas Kell spoke for the hulks.

He found her on the sixty-seventh, on the *Merry Agnes*. A whaler. Dead forty years on the mud, with her ribs showing through her planking at the stern and a whole family living in each of her old oil-holds. Dorcas had the captain's cabin. A gutter. Fifty. Big forearms crossed with white scars from the gutting-knives, old and new, one over another.

She didn't ask him in. She sat on the rail and looked down at him on the mud.

"I know who you are," she said.

"Then you know what I want."

"You want us off the mud." She spat over the rail, not at him. "Before the bell one comes in. The whole coast knows." She looked at the hulks round her. "We'll not go."

---

"It's coming," said Calder.

"I believe you," said Dorcas. "I've seen your green one. I came in under her in the gale with the herring." She leaned on the rail. "We'll not go anyway."

"Why?"

She looked at him as if he were simple.

"Because this is the house," she said. She put her scarred hand flat on the *Merry Agnes*'s rail. "Leave your hulk and walk up the hill, and by night there's someone worse on it. And then where are you? On a hill. With nothing." She shook her head. "Nobody leaves the mud. You don't leave your house because a farmer tells you the sea's coming. You'd not leave your farm."

He thought about that.

He wouldn't have.

---

He sat up most of that night on the Tidehall's roof.

With Lisska. Looking down at the low harbour in the dark. The fires on the hulks going one by one. The mud shining when the moon came out. The black line of the chain across the harbour mouth, up for the night.

*They won't leave the boats,* said Lisska.

"No."

*Then take the boats,* said Lisska. As if it were obvious. *With them in.*

He looked at her.

---

He went to the chain-clerk in the morning.

Not Cobb. The clerk at the window, with the book. He asked to see it. The clerk said it was the Council's book and not for showing. Calder stood there. The clerk looked past him at the dragon on the tower's roof.

He was shown the book.

He couldn't read it fast. He made the clerk read him the front pages. The rules. Every harbour rule since the chain was hung. Who paid what. Who could lower it. Who could sign for what.

And on the fourth page, the one he'd hoped was there.

---

*Any contract of the Council for labour in the harbour under the sum of one silver may be signed by the Speaker alone, without the vote of either table.*

An old rule, the clerk said. For odd jobs. A boy to scrape the weed off the sea-steps. A man to row the harbour-clerk across to the western tower. Nobody had bothered the tables with them in a hundred years.

"Under a silver," said Calder.

"Under a silver," said the clerk. "Anything."

---

He wrote it himself.

At Marrow's table, in the back passage of the Tidehall where she'd stood by the window, that afternoon. On a sheet of the Council's paper. Slowly. It took him an hour. She sat across from him in her blue silk and didn't help and didn't look at what he was writing until he'd finished.

He pushed it across.

*The dragons of Calder Fenn, and the dragons of the Clan that fly with him, hire their labour to the Council of the Tide, for the clearing of the low harbour of Corsa.*

*Not to empty it. To lift the hulks, with the people in them, off the mud and up onto the old ropewalk terrace behind the city, above the wave-mark, and set them down whole.*

*The gutters can walk down to the herring every morning. The houses lose nothing of the season.*

*The price: one copper.*

*Calder Fenn, of Harrow's End.*

---

Marrow read it.

She read it twice. Then she put it down on the table and put her square hand flat on it and looked at him over it.

And laughed.

---

He'd never heard her laugh. He didn't think anyone at the Tidehall had. A clerk going past the end of the passage stopped and looked round, as if something had fallen.

The laugh was short, and real. It came up out of her like a wave on a sea-wall and went over and was gone, and she sat there in her blue silk afterwards with her eyes wet and her calm figurehead's face all undone.

"One copper," she said.

"That's the price."

"Under a silver." She shook her head. "You found the odd-jobs rule. The boy who scrapes the steps." She wiped her eyes with the back of her hand. "Who read it to you?"

"The chain-clerk. He didn't want to."

---

She signed it.

Alone. As the rule said. *Orla Marrow, Speaker.* She didn't ring for a clerk. She didn't call the tables. She signed it, and blotted it, and then she opened the purse at her belt and took out a copper and laid it on the paper.

A Corsa copper. The ship-and-wave worn almost off it.

"From the Council's speaker," she said. "To make it binding. A contract's not paid till the coin's crossed." She pushed it to him. "Don't spend it."

"What will the tables say?"

"Nothing," said Marrow. "They can't. It's under a silver." Something moved at the corner of her mouth. "And it costs them nothing. That's what you found. It costs them nothing at all."

---

He went to Nell Tallis on the sixty-ninth.

The wyvern quay. Straw. Iron rings. Five wyverns hooded on their chains, heads turning. The board on its post. *For hire. Any contract. Ask for Tallis.*

She was on her barrel. Mending something else.

"Master Fenn."

He held out a copper of his own. Marrow's stayed in his coat.

---

"Can you pay?" said Nell Tallis.

She said it before he could. She'd been waiting to.

"One copper," said Calder. "For the clearing of the low harbour. Same terms as mine. Signed by the Speaker under the odd-jobs rule." He kept his hand out. "Your wyverns are small and quick. I need lines carried under the keels. Before the big ones lift."

She looked at the copper in his palm.

Then she looked at the board on the post.

"Any contract," she said.

She took it.

---

They started on the seventieth.

Ulric worked it from the signal-field. He stood on the hill above the Tidehall with his arms folded and shouted. Ulric didn't fly, not by himself. He'd never been chosen. He rode behind Sigge Brack on Sigge's grey when the Clan went anywhere, and the whole coast called it Ulric's grey, and Sigge let them. But for the lift he stood on the hill and shouted, and the forty pairs went where he shouted them, because it was Ulric.

He'd been a war-leader all his life. He'd never led a war like this one.

He laughed most of the first day.

---

The wyverns went first.

Down onto the mud round the first hulk. Small, black-green, quick, with Nell's people riding them. Each with a line in its claws, an iron chain-end on a rope. They went in low over the mud and under the hulk where it lay on its side, through the gap under its bilge, and out the other side, and up. Four lines. Six.

Then the dragons came down.

---

Two pairs for the first. The *Cormorant*, a coaster, small. Four dragons on the four corners. The war-harness chains taken up from the wyverns' lines and hooked to the harness. The people inside the *Cormorant* in her hold, nine of them, holding on to anything, because Dorcas had told them they were going and that was that.

Ulric shouted.

The four dragons went up.

---

The *Cormorant* came off the mud with a sound like a boot coming out of a bog.

Long. Sucking. Then a crack as the mud let go. Then she was in the air. Swinging. Water and mud pouring out of her seams. A child screaming inside her. Four dragons over her, wings going, the chains straight down.

Up over the deep quays. Over the counting-houses. Over the streets. The whole of Corsa came out to look. Up the hill, slow, swinging, to the old ropewalk terrace behind the city where the hulk-folk had spent two days building timber cradles out of the Council's own wood, under a contract for one copper.

They set her down in her cradle.

Upright. For the first time in thirty years.

---

The child stopped screaming.

Nine people came up out of her hold onto her deck, on a hill, with the whole of Corsa below them and the sea beyond, and stood there.

Then a woman began to laugh.

---

Thirty-four hulks in seven days.

The small ones on two pairs. The big ones on four. Kharrak took the biggest alone, the *Ostrey Pride*, a grain-ship dead twenty years with eighty people in her. He took her up on a single great chain slung under her keel, with Lisska over him calling the swing, and set her down on the terrace's top step so gently that nobody in her hold knew they'd landed until Lisska put her head in at the hatch and told them.

*They screamed,* she told Calder, afterwards. *When I put my head in.*

"Of course they did."

*They screamed more at me than at the flying,* said Lisska. She thought about it. *I liked it.*

---

The last seven were too rotten.

Dorcas told him. The *Merry Agnes* among them. They'd come apart in the chains. Their keels were mush. If you lifted them they'd break in the middle and drop their people on the mud from a height.

So the hulk-folk hauled them.

---

Off the mud. On rollers. Up the old slipway at the harbour's back, where Corsa had launched its ships when it built them, a hundred years before.

The hulk-folk on the ropes. Two hundred to a rope. And at the head of the ropes, the beasts.

Dorcas's ox. A big red draught ox called Patience, with one horn broken, that had pulled fish-carts up from the quays for fifteen years. And beside Patience, a stonehorn.

---

He hadn't known it was there.

Nobody had told him. It came down out of the scrub behind the city on the seventy-fifth day when Dorcas went up onto the terrace and banged a pan. Huge. Grey. Old. Its shoulder-plates mossy. Its horns curving forward like ploughshares, one of them chipped. And its eyes white.

Blind.

"It got loose off a drover's herd on the Track," said Dorcas. "Twelve years back. It's lived on the hill ever since. We feed it fish-heads." She banged the pan again. The stonehorn turned its white eyes toward the sound and came to it. "It comes to the pan."

She put the rope over its plates herself.

---

Patience pulled. The stonehorn pulled. Two hundred people on each rope pulled. And the *Merry Agnes*, forty years dead, came up off the mud onto the rollers with her ribs creaking and her oil-holds full of families, and went up the slipway a foot at a time.

Calder stood on the slipway and watched.

Above him the forty pairs wheeled over the city, resting. The wyverns sat on the counting-house roofs with their hoods back. Ulric on the signal-field, not shouting now, watching with his arms folded.

A blind stonehorn and an ox and two hundred gutters, hauling a dead ship up a hill.

---

It took two days for the seven.

On the seventy-sixth day the low harbour was empty.

Mud. Bare. Shining. Gulls walking on it. Holes where the hulks had lain, filling with water. A ring of old rubbish where their sides had been, and a child's shoe, and a broken pot.

Nothing else.

---

The houses' clerks came down to look.

In their good coats with their house colours on their cuffs. They stood on the deep quay and looked at the bare mud where two thousand people had been, and then they looked up at the hill, where forty-one hulks sat on their cradles on the ropewalk terrace with smoke coming out of them, washing on their rigging, children on their decks.

They didn't know what to say.

One of them took out a little book and wrote something in it.

---

The herring came in that afternoon.

The boats to the deep quays, loaded. And down the hill from the terrace, from the hulks, by the old ropewalk steps, the gutters came. Dorcas first, with her knives in a roll under her arm. Two thousand of them. Down to the quays, to work, as they always had.

Nobody in the houses lost a fish.

---

Ulric found him on the slipway at dusk.

Big, grey-bearded, slow. He stood beside Calder and looked at the empty mud.

"Teo would have laughed," he said. "My nephew. Arn's brother. The one they killed." He was quiet a while. "When they were boys, Teo and Arn, they'd make a game of anything. They'd have made a game of this. Who could lift the biggest boat." He laughed, low. "Arn would have lost. Arn always lost to Teo and never minded."

He put his big hand on Calder's shoulder.

"When this is over," he said. "When the bell one's been and gone. I'll take you to Coldtarn. Up the Scarfell. Show you where we pour." He squeezed, once. "You've never seen it."

"No."

"You should," said Ulric. "A man who lifts a harbour should see where we pour."

---

The Blackwater man came on the seventy-eighth.

To the Saltings Arms, on the quay. Two days in on a fast boat with a hold full of something he wasn't naming. He drank, and talked, as they did.

Calder was at the back. With Nell Tallis, who'd bought him a drink with his own copper, she said, to see what it was worth.

---

"A lion," said the Blackwater man. "As big as a barn. Bigger. Came down the cliff path in the dusk. With a woman on its back and an old man behind her, tied on." He drank. "Lay down on the beach among the fires. The sellswords walked backwards into the sea. And every house in the inlet pulled up its ladder." He laughed. "Clack, clack, clack, all round the water. Like a stick on a fence."

The tavern laughed with him.

"And at dawn," said the Blackwater man, "the Merrin woman rowed across to it. Alone. In her boat. And walked up the beach to it and looked it over like it was a cargo she'd come to buy."

---

Nell Tallis looked at Calder.

"Merrin," she said. "The reef-pilots. The ones who go further than they say."

He didn't answer.

*Ask for a reef-pilot,* he'd said. In a yard at Ironhythe at dawn, to her back. *And don't pay what they ask first.*

He looked at his drink. He didn't smile.

Outside, across the dark harbour, the chain went up for the night with its long iron roar, and on the hill behind the city forty-one hulks sat on the ropewalk terrace with their lamps lit, above the wave-mark, where the sea couldn't reach.
