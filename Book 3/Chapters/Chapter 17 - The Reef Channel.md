---

Orrun hated the sea.

He said so on the shingle on the eighty-fourth day at dusk, with the tide going out and the reefs coming up black out of the water beyond the inlet's mouth, one after another, like the backs of something asleep.

*It moves,* he said. *Stone doesn't move. Water moves. This water moves all the time and never goes anywhere.*

"It's tide."

*I know what it is,* said Orrun. *I don't like it.*

He went into it anyway.

---

Lions didn't swim. She'd been told that all her life. Mountain lions didn't, and stone lions didn't, and the thing Orrun was now, that was both and more than both, didn't either. He walked into the black water off the end of the shingle until it was at his chest, and then at his shoulders, and then he was swimming. Badly. Head high. Ears flat. Paws going like a dog's.

Out past the inlet's mouth. Toward the reefs.

Kit, in the boat beside her, watched him go.

"I'd not have believed that," she said, "if I'd been paid."

---

They rowed out after him.

Kit at the tiller. Jory at the oars. Maren in the bow with the lantern. Behind them, the *Kittiwake* rode on her mooring off the yellow-shuttered house with a lantern at her stern and the old man on her deck, wrapped in a blanket, refusing to go below.

The Merrins' channel wound out between the reefs. Jory took them along it without looking.

At its throat he shipped the oars.

---

She could see the stone.

Not well. A paleness under the black water, a yard down. Square. Long. Lying across the channel from reef to reef. The tide still going out. In an hour it would be a foot under. Not less. Kit had said so. Kit had charged for it.

Orrun had got onto the reef.

The near one. He'd hauled himself up its side, streaming, and stood on its lip, the water to his belly, shaking his head like a wet dog and looking extremely put out.

*Now,* he said. *Before it moves again.*

---

She got out of the boat onto the reef.

The rock was wet and black and furred with weed, and cold, and it was the first stone she'd stood on since the shingle that wasn't loose. She went up it on hands and knees to its top, a little above the water, and knelt there.

She put both hands flat on it.

Cold. Wet. And under the cold, the reef. Old stone, hard, going down a long way. Dead, like everything. And under that, under the reef, the sea's floor. And lying in it, across the throat, the block.

She could feel the block.

It hummed.

---

*Two,* said Orrun.

They came up out of the reef.

Out of the rock itself. Grey. Stone. The shape of lions. Two of them, one on either side of the channel, rising out of the reef's black side as if the rock were water and they'd been swimming in it, standing up, shaking themselves. Rock-coloured. Lantern-coloured. Silent.

Jory, in the boat, made a sound.

Kit didn't make any sound at all.

---

The shadows went down into the channel.

One at each end of the block. Into the black water, which didn't splash, as if it didn't notice them. Down. She felt them go down through her hands, through the reef, as she felt her own arms. She felt them reach the bottom. Felt them find the block's ends. Felt them put their shoulders under it.

*Now,* said Orrun.

She pulled.

---

She didn't know how else to say it. She'd never known. No word in it: words needed live ground, and she'd never been able to say them anyway. Only pulling. Pulling stone up through herself. Through her hands, from the reef, from the sea floor, from the long dead dark under it, up into Orrun and out through the shadows, so that what they lifted, she lifted.

The block moved.

---

It came up off the bottom.

She felt it come. The suck of the sand letting go. The weight. More weight than the lintel at Greyhold. More than the ridge at the Ysse, because the ridge had been made of ground that wanted to be a ridge and this was a single stone that wanted to lie where it was.

The water over it bulged. Went white. Broke.

And the block came up out of the sea.

---

Streaming. Grey. Square. The length of a chapel, and as tall as a man on its side. Hanging between the two shadow-lions with the sea pouring off it in sheets, back into the channel, roaring. The lantern-light ran over it.

And over the hooked writing on its top face.

On the *Kittiwake*, a cable off, the old man was shouting.

---

She could hear him across the water. Thin. Delighted.

"*Into the light!*" he was shouting. "*Into the light, and nothing!* No number! It's a shore-stone! Somebody carried a shore-stone out to the end of the road and stood it on the road's first pier! Maren! Maren, they carried it out!"

She couldn't answer. She was holding it up.

---

The sea moved.

Not the channel. Out beyond the reefs. A league off. More. She saw it over the block's edge, in the last grey of the dusk.

A shape.

Long. Low. Like a reef where there wasn't one. Like a line of rocks laid on the sea from left to right. And moving. Slowly. Going west. With the water piling up white along the whole length of it, breaking, as surf breaks on a sandbar, but on nothing.

Like weather. Like a storm walking.

---

Then the sound.

Once. Far. Under the water. She felt it through the reef before she heard it: a long deep note, so low it was almost not a sound, coming up out of the sea floor into her knees and hands.

A bell. Under the sea.

The shadows held. The block hung between them, streaming, and didn't drop.

---

In the boat, Kit Merrin said something.

Maren heard it because the sea had gone quiet after the bell. Kit was sitting at the tiller with her hands in her lap, looking out past the reefs at the long white line going west across the dark.

"That's my father's water," she said.

---

*Walk,* said Orrun.

The shadows walked.

Along the channel. Under the water to their shoulders, with the block carried between them clear of the surface, slowly, the length of a long throw, to where the reef rose on the channel's eastern side into a black shoulder above the high-water mark. They climbed it. They set the block down on its top.

On its face, so the writing looked at the sky.

It sat there. Out of the sea. Grey on black. She thought it would sit there now longer than anybody living.

---

The shadows went back into the reef.

Down into the rock, as they'd come. The rock closed over them like water. Gone.

She took her hands off the stone.

---

Her left hand didn't come.

She lifted her right, and it came. She lifted her left, and her arm lifted, but she couldn't feel it lift. She looked at it. Grey in the lantern-light. From the fingertips to the elbow. Grey and cold and someone else's.

She tried to close it. It closed, slowly. She couldn't feel it close.

The grey ended at the elbow, in a line, as if she'd dipped her arm in ash.

---

Jory was sounding the throat.

A lead on a line over the side, going down, and down, and down. He brought it up and looked at the marks and let it go again to be sure.

"Five fathoms," he said. His voice cracked. "Five, Kit. It's open."

Kit didn't say anything.

---

Jory held out an oar to Maren, to help her back into the boat.

She reached for it with her left hand. She couldn't feel it. She saw her fingers close on it and couldn't feel the wood.

She took it in her right instead.

She rowed back one-armed, beside Jory, so as to have something to do with the right one, and nobody said anything about it, and when they came alongside the *Kittiwake* the old man was standing at the rail with the blanket falling off his shoulders, shaking, beaming, and Kit was the first one up the side.

---

It didn't come back by morning.

She'd thought it would. The feet had come back after the ridge, in a day. She lay in the *Kittiwake*'s hold on the ballast with her arm on her chest and waited for the pins, and the pins didn't come. The grey stayed to the elbow. At dawn she could move it, and close the hand, and not feel any of it.

She put it in her coat.

*It's not coming back,* said Orrun. From the deck above her, where he lay along the whole length of the ketch, and his breath came down through the grating.

"I know."

*You didn't say.*

"No," said Maren.

*I knew anyway,* said Orrun.

---

Kit had the salt dish out at dawn.

In the stern cabin. The chipped clay dish, on the table, between the two of them. She took a single grain on her fingertip and touched it to her tongue.

"Done," she said. "The stone's out. That's the second thing."

"And the first?"

"Done with the seal." Kit wiped her finger on her coat. "The third's the crossing." She looked at Maren's coat. At the pocket. She didn't ask. "We go when we're loaded."

---

Fosse came at noon.

Along the walkway in his grey coat with the Ostrey cuff, with his round spectacles, soft and pleasant, and the paper in his hand. Ostrey's paper. With the Accord's seal on it, in wax, at the foot: a ring, and six lines going into it.

Maren was on the walkway with Kit. She'd come up to see the loading. She stayed.

---

"Mistress Merrin," said Fosse. "And the knight." He bowed to Maren, the same bow exactly. "I've come to present this for payment. As the house of Ostrey does."

"You know what it is," said Kit.

"I've had it read," said Fosse. He looked down at it. Pleasantly. "*The Accord, for the war's necessary costs. The lion's knight.*" He looked up. "The lion's knight. Who stood up at Ironhythe, the gull-hawks say, and asked every kingdom for a ship to sail west past the Shatters. And was told no."

---

Nobody said anything.

Fosse looked out past them. Through the gap between the stilt-houses, toward the inlet's mouth. Toward the reefs.

"And this morning," he said, "I looked out of my window at the reefs, as I do every morning, because I'm a dull man and I like to see the same thing every day. And there was a stone." He smiled. "A great grey stone. Standing on the eastern reef above the water, where there was no stone yesterday." He looked at Kit. "And the Merrins' channel, Jory tells the whole beach, has five fathoms in its throat."

---

He added it up.

Aloud. Pleasantly. As if he were doing a sum for a child.

"The Merrins have a road west. Past the Shatters. They've always had a road. That's where the rocks come from." He tilted his head. "*Rocks.* For eighty years. And now the lion's knight, who wants to go west, has paid the Merrins' debt with the Accord's money, for the war's necessary costs, and her lion has lifted the stone out of the Merrins' channel."

He looked at the seal.

"Why," said Fosse, "would the lion's knight pay a war's necessary costs to go and fetch rocks?"

---

Kit didn't answer.

"No," said Fosse. "Of course." He folded the paper along its crease, neatly, and put it inside his grey coat. "The house of Ostrey accepts the Accord's warrant. It can hardly do otherwise; the Tide Council signed the Accord. The debt against the *Kittiwake* is cleared." He bowed. "Good day, Mistress Merrin. Knight."

He went back along the walkway.

---

Within the hour the gull-hawk went up off his roof.

Maren watched it from the walkway. Grey and white, hook-beaked. It went up off the perch with a tube on its leg and turned once over the inlet and went west, low, along the coast, without circling. Toward Corsa. As if it could see where it was going from here.

Kit watched it too.

---

"That's what your seal cost," said Kit.

She said it without heat. To the gull-hawk, going away.

Maren didn't look away from it.

"You named the price," she said.

"I did." Kit was quiet. "I priced the debt. I priced the stone. I priced the lion's days to the copper." She watched the bird go out of sight. "I didn't think to price that."

---

They loaded on the eighty-fifth and the eighty-sixth.

Water in casks. Salt pork. Ship's biscuit in tins, against the damp. Lamp oil. Salt. The far-stone ballast taken up out of the hold and restowed, block by block, the long ones flat along the keel, so that the lion could lie on it all the way and the ketch would sit level under him.

Gannet worked well.

He worked better than anyone. He was everywhere. Lifting, stowing, swearing at the crew, laughing. He took the heaviest end of every block. He smiled at Maren whenever she came up out of the hold, and she didn't smile back, and he didn't seem to mind.

---

Lisska came at dusk on the eighty-sixth.

Out of the west. Low. Over the inlet's mouth, over the reefs, past the grey stone standing on the eastern one, and in. Green. The whole inlet saw her. Ladders went up again, a few, out of habit. She came down on the boathouse on the north shore, on its roofless walls, where the farmer had come down last winter, and folded her wings and looked across the water at the *Kittiwake*.

At the lion on its deck.

Orrun lifted his head.

---

They talked. Kin to kin. Across the water, in sight.

Maren heard it through him. Not Lisska's words. Orrun's of them. As he heard them and passed them through.

*He sent her,* said Orrun. *The farmer. A man in a tavern told him you were here. He sent her to find you. To tell you.*

"Tell me what?"

*The chain's up. At Corsa. The bell one went past Corsa this morning. Going west. Along the coast.* He listened. *She followed it. It turned in off the salt marshes. The Saltings. It's lying off them now. Close in.*

He listened again.

*She says it's going home. To where it slept. And then it'll come back out.*

---

"Tell her thank you," said Maren.

Orrun told her.

Lisska looked across the water a moment longer. At Maren, on the deck. At her left arm. Maren hadn't put it in her coat. She'd been coiling a line, one-handed.

Something went across the water.

*She says,* said Orrun, *he'd tell you to put it in your coat.*

---

Then Lisska went.

Up off the boathouse wall in one beat, and over the inlet, and out, west, low over the reefs into the dusk. Back to Corsa. To him.

Maren watched her go until she couldn't see her.

Then she put her arm in her coat.

---

Below her, in the hold, the old man was talking to the far-stone. She could hear him through the grating. Quietly. Reading it aloud, block by block, as Jory and Gannet set it down. *Into the light, thirty. Into the light, nine. Into the light.*

Behind them, the inlet. Ahead, the Merrins' channel, five fathoms in its throat, going out between the reefs toward the Shatters.

And the gull-hawk, somewhere ahead of all of it, flying west.
