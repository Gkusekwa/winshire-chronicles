---

The houses went at dawn.

He watched them from the Tidehall's roof. With Lisska on the copper beside him, back from Blackwater two days, still thin, saying nothing. The light coming up grey over the sea. And the houses' ships going out.

In a line. To the chain.

---

He'd known they would. Everybody had.

On the eighty-seventh Marrow had gone up to the Tidehall's little bell-tower herself, at noon, and rung the other bell. The one Corsa rang for fire and for nothing else, that nobody alive had heard rung. She'd rung it until her arms were tired, and then stood on the balcony and told the harbour front to go up the hill. Every street below the wave-mark. Now.

The docks had gone. Slowly, and then all at once.

And the houses had gone down to their ships.

---

They'd loaded all night.

Their books first. Then their silver, in chests, on barrows, down the quays in the torchlight, with house men in house colours walking beside each barrow with swords. Strand's green. Ostrey's grey. Quill's white. A dozen others. Their families. Their furniture, some of them. One house had loaded a harpsichord.

Rennick's three black ships had slipped out of the Bench's moorings at midnight without anyone seeing them go.

By dawn the houses' ships were full and waiting at the chain.

---

Cobb lowered it for them.

Calder watched it happen. From the roof, across the harbour, he could see the window in the eastern tower's foot with its iron grille. Each ship came to it. Each ship's master leaned over the rail and passed silver down, and the clerk inside took it and wrote it in the book. By tonnage. Every one.

Then the bell. The twelve men on the capstan. The long iron roar.

The chain went down into the sea, and the houses went out over it in a line, one after another, and turned east along the coast and away.

---

Then the chain came up again.

Roaring. Dripping. Black. A foot clear of the water across the harbour mouth, between the two round towers, with nothing waiting at it and nothing going out.

Cobb would be counting the links.

*They paid,* said Lisska. Beside him. *To leave.*

"That's Corsa."

*It's stupid,* said Lisska.

"Yes," said Calder.

---

The sea went out of the Saltings at mid-morning.

He saw it go. West of the city, along the coast, where the salt marsh lay grey and green and folded with channels for leagues. All the channels shining with water. And then not. All at once. As if somebody had pulled a plug out of the bottom of the world. The water went out of every channel of the Saltings in the time it took to breathe twice, and left the mud shining in the sun.

He'd seen that before. At Gullhaven. At the end of the winter, at noon.

He stood up on the copper.

---

The Bell came out of the west.

Across the Saltings. Over the shallows. Out of the sea at the marsh's far end, walking.

He'd seen it in the spring from a long way off, from Lisska's eyes, dragging itself over the Plains. He'd seen it go under the sea at Gullhaven at the end of the winter, with the reef breaking. He hadn't seen it like this.

A back like a reef. Like a whole island of black rock, wet, streaming, with weed on it and sea pouring off it in sheets. And on the back, broken open, the dome of the drowned temple, its columns sticking up out of the rock like the ribs of a wreck.

And in the dome, hanging, the bell.

---

Old bronze. Green. The size of a house. Hanging in the broken dome on its beam, swinging with every stride the thing took.

Ringing.

He heard it. Across the Saltings, across leagues of mud, across the city. A note so deep it wasn't a note. It came up through the Tidehall's stones into his boots. Every stride. *Down.* Like a door shutting somewhere under the world.

In front of the Bell, the sea stood up.

---

A wall.

He couldn't think of another word. Grey-green, glassy, with white along its top, going along in front of the Bell across the Saltings' shallows, east, toward the city, as high as the counting-houses. Higher. Growing.

Lisska had said it would go home and lie down where it had slept. It had gone home. It had lain off the Saltings two days.

Now it was coming back out.

East along the coast.

And Corsa was in front of it.

---

"Go," he said.

Lisska went. Off the roof, west, to see. He was already going down the roof's ladder to Kharrak on the sea-steps.

There were people on the sea-steps.

---

He hadn't known.

At the last. When Marrow rang the bell and the docks went up the hill. Not all of them had gone up the hill. The last streets, the ones nearest the water, the oldest people, the families who'd stayed to bring one more thing, had looked at the hill and looked at the Tidehall and run to the Tidehall.

Because it was stone. Because it had stood there three hundred years. Because stone was safe.

Two hundred of them. On the sea-steps. On the balcony. In the great room. On the copper roof, where he'd just come down.

And Marrow, in her blue silk, at the top of the sea-steps with her square hands up, sending them higher.

---

Ulric was already in the water.

On the sea-steps. Waist-deep, at the bottom, where the harbour lapped. The harbour was rising. The wall wasn't here yet. The harbour was rising anyway, as if it knew. Ulric stood on the bottom step with the water at his waist and his big grey-bearded face set, and handed people up.

Up to the pairs. The Clan's forty, hovering over the sea-steps one after another with the war-harness baskets slung under them, low, their wings beating the water flat. Sigge Brack's grey nearest. A basket coming down. Ulric lifting an old woman into it like a sack. The basket going up.

The next.

---

"Ulric!"

He didn't look round.

"Get up the steps!"

"When they're up," said Ulric. He lifted a child. "Get your red beast in the air, farmer. You're no use to me on the stair."

Calder got on Kharrak.

---

The wall reached the harbour mouth.

He was in the air when it came. Over the harbour's middle, on Kharrak, high enough to see everything. The chain across the mouth between the round towers, up, black, a foot clear of the water.

The wall hit it.

The chain didn't hold a breath. He'd thought it might hold something. It didn't. The links went with a crack like a whip the length of the harbour mouth, and the two ends flew back toward the towers, and the eastern tower's foot, with the window and the grille and the winch-house behind it, went into the water under the wall.

Cobb had been in there. With the drum. He wouldn't leave. He'd counted the links that morning and they'd been right.

---

Then the water came in.

Over the deep quays. Over the house ships' empty moorings. Into the counting-houses along the harbour front, one after another. He saw their shutters go under. Strand green. Ostrey grey. Quill white. Painted shutters in house colours along the whole front of the harbour, going under the brown water one street after another, as the wave went up the streets of Corsa toward the hill.

Up. And up.

To the wave-mark. The old people knew where it was. They'd always known. Nobody had built below it for a hundred years except the houses, because the houses' counting-houses had to be on the water.

The water stopped at the wave-mark.

---

Above it, on the ropewalk terrace, the hulks.

Forty-one of them. On their cradles. Upright. With their washing on their rigging and their people on their decks, looking down at the water filling the streets below them to the mark, and stopping, and not reaching them.

He saw Dorcas Kell.

On the *Merry Agnes*'s deck. At the rail. A pan in her hand. Just holding it, as if she'd come out to call the stonehorn and forgotten.

---

The Bell's head came into the harbour.

Through the mouth. Between the broken towers. A head like a cliff coming in out of the sea, black, streaming, with weed in the cracks of its plates, and eyes. He saw the eyes. Small for the head. Deep in. Old. Not angry. Nothing so small as angry.

It turned.

Not toward the Tidehall. Toward the hill.

Toward the terrace. Toward forty-one hulks on their cradles above the wave-mark, with two thousand people on them.

---

He didn't think about it.

He'd think about it afterwards, for a long time. He didn't now. He put both his hands flat on Kharrak's neck, where the plates met, where the heat was. Where it always was.

He didn't say a word. A word was nothing out here. Ada's hearth-word had done nothing over the reef at the end of the winter: the sea underneath, running, taking it. He'd learned that.

He didn't need a word.

*All of it,* he said to Kharrak. In the place where they talked. *Everything. Take mine too.*

Kharrak didn't argue.

---

It went out of him.

He felt it go. Out of his chest, down his arms, out through his palms into Kharrak's neck. Not fire. What fire came from. Whatever it was in him that Kharrak's fire had always been a little bit made of, since the cave, since the first day. He pushed it into the dragon, all of it, everything he had, and Kharrak opened his jaws and the fire came out.

A river of it.

---

It went across the harbour.

The whole width. Half a league of brown water, from the harbour's middle to the mouth. Farther than any dragon's breath had ever gone. Farther than any charm could carry. A river of fire laid down across the sea like a road, roaring, the water under it going up in steam as it went, white, boiling, a wall of steam behind it.

Into the Bell's face.

---

The head stopped.

In the steam. He couldn't see it. He could feel it, through Kharrak. A thing as big as a hill, with fire in its face, stopping.

Then turning.

Away from the hill. Back. Toward the sea.

He felt it go.

He didn't feel anything else.

---

He was lying along Kharrak's neck.

He didn't know how long. He couldn't sit up. His hands were stuck to Kharrak's plates and when he tried to lift them they came away and the skin stayed. His eyes were full of something warm and he couldn't see out of them. He couldn't breathe. He tried, and it was like breathing through wool, and then through nothing.

Kharrak was flying. Out of the steam. Low and slow, very carefully, with Calder lying on his neck like a sack.

*Breathe,* said Kharrak.

He tried.

*Again,* said Kharrak.

---

The sea went out after the Bell.

He didn't see it. He heard about it after, from everyone, and he put it together from what they said, and afterwards he could never tell what he'd seen and what he'd been told.

The Bell turned back out through the harbour mouth. And the sea went with it. Out of the city. Back down the streets it had come up, dragging, brown, full of everything it had picked up. Furniture. Barrels. Doors. Back down to the harbour and through it and out.

And on its way, it took the Tidehall.

---

The front wall first. The balcony over the water. The tall windows of the great room, all at once. The painted ceiling of ships, three hundred years under smoke, coming down into the brown water with the ships on it seeming to move, the last time.

The copper roof was empty by then. Nell Tallis's wyverns had plucked the last people off its edge, the riders leaning out of the saddles and grabbing anyone by anything, and the forty pairs had taken the balcony.

The great room wasn't empty.

---

Marrow had been in it.

Getting the last of the clerks out by the back passage. The passage where she'd stood by the window and told him she'd read his page and it had cost her a window. She'd sent them down it one by one, the clerks with their books, to the street behind and up the hill, and she'd stood at the passage mouth in her blue silk to see the last one through.

The last clerk got out.

She didn't.

---

And Ulric.

Nobody could tell him, that afternoon, where Ulric was.

---

The Bell went east.

Out at sea, in the afternoon. Along the coast. Slowly. Toward the end of the world under the mountains. Its back like a reef going away. The bell ringing, fainter, every stride. Lisska followed it a league and came back.

Behind it, Corsa.

The harbour a brown ruin. The quays gone. The counting-houses along the front stove in, every one, their painted shutters in the mud. The towers broken. The chain somewhere on the harbour floor in two pieces. Up the streets to the wave-mark, everything wet, everything broken, everything brown.

Above the mark, the hill. The terrace. Forty-one hulks.

And at the harbour's back, the low harbour.

Mud. Bare. Shining. With nothing on it.

It had been emptied for one copper. There'd been nothing on it to lose.

---

Sigge found Ulric at dusk.

He'd looked all afternoon. Alone. On the grey, low over the mud, up and down. He found him on the low harbour. Where the hulks had lain. In one of the long hollows in the mud where a hulk had sat on its side for thirty years, filling with water. The sea had put him there on its way out.

Whole. Lying on his back. His big grey-bearded face to the sky.

Sigge didn't move him. He sat down in the mud beside him and waited for someone to come.

---

Kharrak brought Calder.

He couldn't stand. They put him down on the mud from Kharrak's neck and he sat in it. In the cold mud of the low harbour, beside Ulric, with his hands in his lap because he couldn't put them anywhere else, and his eyes crusted, and his breath coming short.

---

"He had the boy," said Sigge.

Calder turned his head. He'd never heard Sigge say more than a word at a time. Sigge wasn't looking at him. He was looking at Ulric.

"A clerk's boy. Six. Held up over the water, so." Sigge put his hands up in front of him, empty, as if there were something in them. "Water at his chest. I brought the grey down. Low as a grey goes and not go in. I put my arm out." He put it out now, crooked, over the mud. "He put the boy in it."

He looked at his arm.

"I had him up in the saddle. Then I looked down."

Calder waited.

"The steps were going," said Sigge. "All the harbour going out past the hall's foot at once. Like a beck in spate. And him on the steps with the water round him."

He took his arm back.

"Then there weren't any steps," said Sigge.

That was all of it. Calder heard afterwards that he'd told it all night on the terrace, to anyone who asked, the same words every time, as if he'd learned them.

He looked at Ulric's face.

*When this is over,* Ulric had said. *When the bell one's been and gone. I'll take you to Coldtarn.*

---

He didn't remember the night.

Not much of it. He was in a cabin. A captain's cabin, in a ship, but the ship was on a hill. The *Merry Agnes*. Dorcas's bunk. Dorcas sitting on a stool beside it with a bucket of water, wetting cloths and laying them on his hands, and taking them off when they were warm, and wetting them again.

She didn't talk. She did it all night.

Lisska was on the *Merry Agnes*'s roof, the cabin-top. He could feel her there. Kharrak was lying along the terrace outside, the whole length of three hulks, with his head on the end of the ropewalk.

---

Once in the night he woke.

Kharrak was awake. He could feel it. Kharrak was lying on the terrace with his head up, very still, and turned. Away from the sea. Away from the city.

South-west.

He'd turned it that way before. On the eightieth day, at noon, on the signal-field, all at once, and Lisska with him, and the Clan's riders said the old one at the farm had too.

*What is it?* said Calder. In the place where they talked.

*Nothing,* said Kharrak. He didn't put his head down. *Sleep.*

---

There were people on the Track all night.

He heard them through the cabin's wall. Going past the terrace on the road behind it, the Saltreach Track, going inland. Out of Corsa. Carts. Feet. Voices. Children crying and being hushed.

A man's voice, a sailor's, outside the *Merry Agnes*, talking to someone. Calder only half heard it.

"...off a Blackwater coaster. We ran for Corsa. Fools." A laugh, not a happy one. "There's a ship at Blackwater fitting out for a long voyage. A salvage ketch. Loading water for a month. Who loads water for a month?"

The voices went on up the Track.

---

He lay in Dorcas's bunk with his hands in the wet cloths.

*Ask for a reef-pilot,* he'd said. *And don't pay what they ask first.*

He couldn't see. He couldn't breathe right. His hands were raw to the wrist. Ulric was lying on the mud of the low harbour with Sigge beside him, and Marrow was under the Tidehall, and Cobb was under the eastern tower with his drum.

And two thousand people were asleep on a hill in forty-one ships, above the wave-mark, alive.

Kharrak didn't put his head down all night.
