---

The houses came in on the eighty-ninth.

Out of the west, along the coast, at noon. Twenty-odd hulls. Fast ships, the kind the houses kept for running, with their sails full and their decks crowded. Kit saw them from the walkway. She'd been splicing a line and she stopped and watched them come round the point into the inlet, one after another, and drop anchor in the deep water in a long line, where the smugglers' ships had always lain.

Strand green on the pennants. Ostrey grey. Quill white. A dozen more.

Behind them, three black ships with no colours at all.

---

Blackwater had never had the Council in it.

Three harbour-masters in her father's life. Three chains. One floating in the spring. The Council had never come itself. It had sent men, and men could be lost.

Now it was here. In twenty-odd ships, anchored in Blackwater's own water, with the houses' families on their decks in good coats, looking at the stilt-houses and the black cliffs and the sellswords' fires on the shingle as if they'd come to the edge of the world.

They had.

All round the inlet's head, she heard the ladders going up.

---

The Bell went by on the ninetieth.

Far out. Past the reefs, past the inlet's mouth. A white line on the sea, moving from west to east, the water piling along it. The whole inlet came out onto its walkways to watch. The houses' people on their decks. The sellswords on the shingle. Nobody said anything.

Once, very faint, the bell.

The white line went east, toward the mountains' end. Then it turned. South. Out over the Shatters.

Out toward her father's water.

She watched it until it was gone.

---

The Council sat on the ninetieth, in the afternoon.

In the boathouse.

She enjoyed that more than anything else that week. The great tarred shed on the north shore with its roof torn off and its sea-door hanging, that nobody in Blackwater would rent because a dragon had opened it. The only building in the inlet big enough to hold the Council of the Tide.

They set out the houses' sea-chests in a row for a table. The Bench sat on the shingle outside the sea-door. Rennick put his boots up on an Ostrey sea-chest and nobody stopped him.

Kit stood at the back with the Blackwater people who'd come to watch, and looked at the sky through the roof.

---

They named a Speaker first.

Marrow was under the Tidehall at Corsa. Everybody knew. Nobody said it at the sea-chests. They just said the chair was empty and needed filling.

Mistress Celia Ostrey filled it.

Tall. Calm. Grey silk, Ostrey's grey. Kit had seen her once at Corsa, years ago, in a counting-house, buying a cargo of hides by the shipload without looking at a single hide. She'd been the one at the long table, the gull-hawks said, who'd stood up when the farmer asked for the low harbour emptied and said *it's herring season*.

Aubrey Strand seconded her. Softly.

Nobody else spoke.

---

Then Fosse read his report.

Kit had known he would. She'd known since the gull-hawk went up off his roof on the eighty-fifth. She stood at the back of the boathouse and listened to Orme Fosse read, in his pleasant soft voice, to the Council of the Tide, the thing her family had kept for eighty years.

*The Merrins have a road west. Past the Shatters. To a shore. They have always had it. The lion's knight is paying the Accord's money for passage on it, in the Merrin ketch, with a Merrin pilot.*

The boathouse went quiet.

Then everybody turned round and looked at her.

---

Celia Ostrey didn't.

She looked at Fosse. Then at the sea-chests. Then at a paper her clerk put in front of her, old, that the clerk must have brought from Corsa in a chest because he knew it would be wanted.

"The League's right over channels," said Celia Ostrey. Calmly. "Any channel, route or passage of use to the League in time of need passes to the Council for the duration of the need." She looked up. "It's a wartime clause. From the Reef Wars. Nobody's used it in a hundred and forty years." She looked at the sky through the roof. "The need is now."

She looked at Kit then. At last.

"The Merrins' road is the Council's," she said.

---

They sealed the house on the ninety-first.

Council wax on the door of the tall stilt-house with the ochre shutters. Council men inside. Kit watched from the *Kittiwake*'s rail as they carried things out along the walkway.

Her father's charts.

Not the book. The book was aboard; it never left the ship. But Hal Merrin had kept working charts in the house. Copies of the turns, pier to pier, that he'd drawn for himself over thirty years and pinned on the walls of the room where he'd slept. Older than the book's last corrections. Wrong in places. Right in most.

She watched them go along the walkway in a Council clerk's arms, rolled, with Council string round them, to the boathouse. Where Ostrey's pilots were waiting to look at them.

---

The board went up that afternoon.

On the boathouse door. Painted. Ostrey's clerks had painted it themselves, in a good plain hand, on a sea-chest's lid, and nailed it up where everybody on the shingle would see.

*PASSAGE TO THE FAR SHORE*

*Beyond the Shatters. A land without Titans.*

*By the Merrins' road. On the Council's fleet.*

*Three hundred gold a head. Children half.*

*Apply to the House of Ostrey.*

---

She read it three times.

*A land without Titans.*

She'd been there eight times. She didn't know if there were Titans there. Nobody did. What she knew was that the light came up out of the hills at night and the beasts didn't run from you and the stone hummed, and that her great-grandfather had made a rule and her father had kept it and died of a squall and not of the rule, and the rule wasn't on the board.

*Never sleep ashore over there.*

Nobody in the Council knew it was a lie. She could see that. Celia Ostrey believed the board. Fosse believed it. They'd sell it in good faith, at three hundred gold, because a shore past the edge of the world that nobody came back from must be empty, and empty must mean safe.

She didn't tell them.

Nobody had asked her.

---

The rich of Corsa queued on the shingle.

By dusk on the ninety-first. In a line down the beach from the boathouse door to the water's edge, with their chests and their servants and their children, in good coats gone salty from the run along the coast. Waiting to pay three hundred gold to Ostrey's clerks at a sea-chest table to be carried away from the Titans.

Two hundred berths sold by dark.

By the ninety-third, four hundred. And the board had been painted over, and said five hundred.

---

And the poor came in behind them.

On anything that floated. Out of Corsa, along the coast, on the same wind. Fishing boats. A barge with no mast, rowed. A raft of barrels. One man came in on a door, paddling with his hands, and walked up the shingle with his feet bleeding and asked for water.

Blackwater sold it to him.

---

That was Blackwater. It sold everything. It had never had anything else to sell but itself and it sold that too. Space on the shingle, a copper a night, a patch of stones the size of a body. A corner of a hold in a smuggler's ship, a silver. A place on a cart going inland up the cliff road, a gold, and the cart might go and might not.

Water.

She watched the price of a cask of water at the spring under the eastern cliff go from a copper on the eighty-ninth to a silver on the ninetieth. By the ninety-second they'd stopped selling it by the cask. They sold it by the cup.

---

And Ostrey paid claims.

On the shingle. At another sea-chest. Ostrey insured half the cargoes in the League and half the buildings, and the buildings were under the sea at Corsa, and the people who'd owned them came to the sea-chest and showed their papers and were paid.

In Council paper.

Paper with the Council's seal, that said the Council owed the bearer so much, to be paid at the Council's treasury at Corsa. The treasury was under the Tidehall. Nobody in Blackwater would take a sheet of it for a cup of water.

Ostrey paid every claim in full. Kit watched them do it. Every one.

---

Gannet went ashore every night.

For news, he said. He came back late each time across the inlet in the little boat, whistling, and told her what he'd heard on the shingle and at the boathouse and round the sellswords' fires. He was good at news. He always had been.

On the ninety-second he told her about the chain.

---

"They're stringing one," he said. "Across the mouth. Between the headlands." He was smiling. "Anchor-cables. Off the houses' ships. Shackled end to end on a boom of spars, with a black ship of Rennick's at each end of it." He shook his head, admiring. "Blackwater's fourth chain. And no harbour-master to drown."

"When?"

"Done by tomorrow night," said Gannet. "They're short of shackles."

---

They came for Jory that night.

The ninety-second. Late. Four of Rennick's men in a boat, and Fosse in the stern with his spectacles catching the lantern, and a paper. They came alongside the *Kittiwake* and Fosse stood up in the boat and held the paper up to the light so she could read it from the rail.

She didn't need to. She knew what it said.

He read it anyway. Pleasantly.

*By order of the Council of the Tide in the League's need: the Merrin pilot, Jory Merrin, is requisitioned for the Council's fleet, to pilot it by the Merrins' road.*

---

Jory was behind her at the rail.

She felt him there. Thin. Nineteen. Their father's long hands.

"They've the charts," she said. Low. Not turning round. "Father's. From the house. They've Ostrey's pilots. They'll go whether you're there or not."

"I know."

"If you don't go with them, they take her now." She meant the ketch. "Tonight. And nobody goes anywhere." She held the rail. "Go with them."

He didn't say anything for a while.

"And you?" he said.

"Go with them, Jory."

---

He went.

Down the side into Rennick's boat. They didn't touch him. He sat in the bows with his back to the *Kittiwake* and his hands between his knees.

At the last, as the boat pulled off, he looked back. Once.

She didn't wave. Merrins didn't.

Fosse touched his hat to her from the stern.

"He'll be well paid," he said. "The Council pays its pilots."

---

There was a second paper.

He handed it up to her on the end of an oar, as they pulled away. She read it by the stern lantern.

*The ketch* Kittiwake *to be held at the Council's mooring from dawn of the ninety-fifth day, to lead the Council's fleet by the Merrins' road. By order.*

From dawn of the ninety-fifth.

They needed the chain finished first. So she couldn't run.

---

She sat in the stern cabin on the ninety-third with the salt.

All night. Alone. The lamp swinging. The dish on the table in front of her: the chipped clay dish, with the coarse grey salt in it, that she'd held out to the lion's knight on the seventy-eighth.

And the other salt.

She couldn't put it on the table. No dish held it. It lay in the house's charter, at Corsa, under the Tidehall. Four generations ago, when Abram's father had come to Blackwater and wanted his salvage counted as a house's and not a smuggler's, he'd gone to Corsa and stood before the Council and taken salt from the Council's dish. All the houses had. That was what made a house a house.

A Council order in the League's need bound every house that had taken the Council's salt.

---

If she sailed tomorrow night, she broke it.

The Merrins' salt. Abram's father's. Broken by her, in the League's need, on the Council's order.

Unsalted.

She knew what it meant. Everybody in Saltreach knew. Nobody would make a contract with her again. Not a cargo. Not a berth. Not a debt. No house would honour a paper she held, and none would hold hers. No harbour would sell her water. Not in Corsa, not in Wendmouth, not here. Not for the rest of her life.

She'd be outside every contract in the League.

---

And if she didn't sail, she broke the other.

The pinch on her tongue from the chipped dish. *Salt between us. What's sworn is kept, or neither of us is fit to drink with.* With a knight of Greyhold who'd paid the Merrins' debt with a seal that sold the Merrins' secret, and whose lion had swum, badly, with its ears flat, to lift a chapel out of the Merrins' channel.

She'd priced the debt. She'd priced the stone. She'd priced the lion's days to the copper.

She'd not priced this either.

---

She looked at the dish a long time.

Then she took one grain on her fingertip and touched it to her tongue.

It tasted of salt. Nothing else. It always had.

---

She went down to the hold before dawn.

To tell Maren. She'd decided; there was no reason to wait. She went to the grating over the hold and looked down through it.

The hold was dark. One lantern, turned low, on a hook.

And the lion.

---

He was lying on the ballast. The far-stone, restowed flat along the keel, block by block, grey, humming. He lay on the whole length of it from the mast-step to the stern-post. His great head on his paws. His eyes half shut. Gold, in the lantern-light, under the lids.

Quiet.

The old man was asleep against his side. Curled up in a blanket in the angle of the lion's shoulder, with one shaking hand flat on the stone.

The lion didn't move. He lay there on the humming stone with the old man against him and the sea moving under the hull, very still, very warm, as a dog lies by a fire on a winter night in a house it's sure of.

She'd never seen anything so settled in her life.

---

Maren was awake.

Sitting on the ladder's bottom rung with her arm in her coat. She looked up at the grating.

"Tomorrow night," said Kit. Down through the bars. "Before they come at dawn."

Maren looked at her.

"They'll have the chain up," she said.

"Yes."

"And the salt?"

"I've had it," said Kit.

---

She went back up on deck.

The light was coming. Grey over the black cliffs. Out at the inlet's mouth, between the headlands, she could see the boom going across: spars and cables, low on the water, a black ship at each end of it with lanterns at their mastheads.

Blackwater's fourth chain.

On the shingle the queue had started again at the boathouse door. In the deep water the houses' ships rode at anchor in their line. On one of them, somewhere, Jory was asleep, or wasn't.

She went to see to the casks.
