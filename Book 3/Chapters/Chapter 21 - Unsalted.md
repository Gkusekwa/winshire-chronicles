---

There was no moon.

Kit had chosen that. Kit had chosen everything. The night, the ninety-fourth, because the moon was a day past dark and wouldn't rise till near dawn. The hour, an hour after midnight, because the ebb would be running hardest then, out of the inlet, and would carry them without a sail. The sails, furled and gasketed and ready to let fall all at once. No lights. Not a lantern. Not a pipe.

Maren stood in the bows in the dark and felt the *Kittiwake* come off her mooring.

No sound. The mooring-line slipped from the bow and went into the water without a splash because Nye was holding the end of it and let it go hand over hand. The ketch swung. The ebb took her.

They went down the inlet in the dark like a log.

---

The crew didn't speak.

She'd learned their names in the week. Ferris at the helm: the mate, grey, sixty, who'd sailed with Kit's father. Old Lorn on the mainsheet, deaf in one ear, who could feel a wind change through the rope before anyone saw it. Tansy, the cook, broad and red-armed, who sang in the galley all day and didn't sing now. The brothers, Nye and Sim, sixteen and seventeen, up in the rigging already, ready to let fall. Corrie, a Blackwater girl, twenty, quiet, with the forehatch.

And Gannet.

At the foot of the foremast. Smiling. She could see his teeth in the dark.

---

He'd told Kit about the shackles at dusk.

Maren had been there. In the stern cabin, with the book open on the table and Kit's finger on the boom's line drawn across the inlet's mouth in charcoal.

"Two joins," Gannet had said. "In the middle. Where they ran short. Rope. Not iron." He'd put his finger on the charcoal line, a little east of the middle. "Lashed. Good lashing, mind. Rennick's men. But rope." He'd smiled. "They've painted the spar over it white. So the black ships can see where the weak place is and watch it."

Kit had looked at him.

"How do you know?"

"I've been ashore every night," said Gannet. "For news."

---

They passed the houses' line at the inlet's middle.

Twenty-odd ships at anchor in the deep water. Dark, mostly. A lantern at a stern here and there. A watch on Strand's ship walking his deck, pipe glowing. The *Kittiwake* went by him on the ebb a long cable off, dark, silent, low in the water with the weight of the lion, and Maren watched the pipe glow, and stop, and glow again.

He didn't see them.

Behind her, the *Kittiwake*'s hold. The forehatch open a hand's width. Corrie kneeling by it. And below, on the ballast, the lion. Lying along the whole keel on the far-stone, with the old man wrapped in a blanket against his side.

*It's very dark,* said Orrun.

"Yes."

*I like it dark,* said Orrun. *It's like the hill.*

---

The boom was a line of lanterns.

Two. One at each masthead of Rennick's black ships, at either end, at the feet of the two headlands. And between them, low on the black water, nothing she could see. The boom. Spars and cables. Shackled. Lashed.

And somewhere a little east of its middle, a spar painted white.

She saw it at last. A pale line on the water. Faint. Like a bone.

"There," said Kit. Behind her. Very low.

---

"Let fall," said Kit.

The sails came down.

All at once. Nye and Sim let go the gaskets aloft and the canvas fell and filled with a crack she felt in her teeth, and the wind came into it off the land, behind them, and the *Kittiwake* leapt.

She hadn't known a ship could leap. The ketch had been drifting like a log, and then she was going. Fast. The water came up white at her bow. The pale line came at them out of the dark.

On the black ships, shouts.

---

They hit the white spar.

The stem. Square on. Ferris had put her there as if he'd drawn a line. The *Kittiwake* struck the boom where the white spar lay and Maren felt it come up through the deck into her boots, a shock, a grinding, wood on wood, the bow lifting.

Then a sound like a bowstring breaking.

The lashing. The rope. Parting under the ketch's weight and the wind's.

The bow came down. They were through.

---

And the boom closed on them.

She saw it come. Both halves. The spars and cables, freed at the middle, swinging in on their ends from the two black ships. Not away. In. Toward the ketch's sides. The black ships' crews were awake now, on their capstans, hauling, and the boom's two ends came sweeping in out of the dark toward the *Kittiwake* to take her on either side, under her counter, round her rudder, and hold her there like a fish in a net.

"Maren," said Kit. Not loud.

---

She couldn't reach stone.

She was on a deck. Wood. Over water. Running water. Nothing under her that Orrun could use. The headlands were rock, black rock, both of them, but they were a long cable off on either side, and she was on a deck.

Except.

---

She went down on her knees at the forehatch.

Corrie moved aside. Maren put her right hand through the hatch, down, into the dark. Her left was in her coat. Her right went down through the hatch, and down, until her fingertips touched something cold.

Stone.

The ballast. Far-stone. A block of it, under the hatch, at the head of the keel. Humming. Full.

Under her hand, the lion. Lying on it.

---

*The cliff,* she said. *The east one.*

*It's a long way,* said Orrun.

*It's rock.*

*I can feel it,* said Orrun. *Through this.* She felt him feel it. Through the block under her hand, through the stone he lay on, out across the dark water to the black cliff at the headland's foot, a long cable off. As if the far-stone were a coal he could blow on, and the cliff the fire.

*One,* said Orrun.

---

It came up out of the headland.

She saw it. In the lantern-light from the eastern black ship's masthead. At the foot of the cliff, at the water's edge, where the black rock went down into the sea, something stood up out of the rock.

Grey. Stone. The shape of a lion. Rising out of the cliff-foot as a swimmer comes up out of a pool. Shaking itself.

The boom's eastern end was sweeping in past it, on its cable, to the ketch's side.

The shadow took it in its jaws.

---

The boom stopped.

The eastern half. Held. Open. The spars and cables streaming in the ebb, held at their end in the jaws of a stone lion standing waist-deep at the foot of a cliff, while on the black ship above it twelve men on a capstan hauled and hauled and the capstan didn't turn.

She heard them shouting. She heard the capstan's pawls clacking, and stopping.

The western half came on.

---

It hit the ketch's side.

Low. On the port quarter. The spars scraped along her planking with a long tearing sound and the cable went under her counter, for the rudder. Ferris was at the helm. She saw him, by the masthead lanterns, lean on the tiller with his whole body and his grey face set, and the *Kittiwake* answer, and turn, a little, a little more, and the cable go under her and past and not catch.

Scraping. Tearing.

Gone.

They were through. Both halves behind them. The open sea in front.

---

Something went over her head.

A sound like a bird. A bolt. From the eastern black ship's bolt-thrower, a mast-length of iron and wood. It went through the mainsail above her with a crack and out the other side and into the dark sea beyond, and the sail had a hole in it the size of a door, and nobody was hurt.

She looked back.

On the eastern black ship's deck, by the bolt-thrower, a big man. Lantern-lit. A gold ring in one ear.

Rennick.

He was laughing. She could hear it across the water. He raised his hand to them. Not to stop them. As a man raises his hand to a horse that's won a race he didn't have money on.

He didn't loose again.

---

She took her hand off the stone.

At the cliff's foot, the shadow let go of the boom. Its end swung in, slow now, too late, and the black ship's capstan went round with a jerk and men fell over. The shadow sank back into the headland's rock. The rock closed over it.

Gone.

---

Her arm went.

She felt it go. The grey. Out of the line at her elbow where it had stopped since the reef channel, and up. Up the upper arm. Cold. Climbing. Like water rising in a cask. Past the bulge of the muscle. Up, toward the shoulder.

It stopped a hand's width short of the shoulder.

She felt it stop. And then she didn't feel the arm at all.

Not the hand, not the forearm, not the elbow, not the upper arm. She could move it. She moved it inside her coat. It moved, as it had since the reef channel. She couldn't feel it move. It hung from her shoulder like a sleeve with something heavy in it.

She stayed on her knees at the forehatch until she could stand.

---

Kit had the helm when she came aft.

Ferris had given it to her past the boom. Kit stood at the tiller with the wind behind and the ketch going fast and the inlet falling away astern, the black cliffs, the lanterns of the houses' line, the two black ships at the mouth with their lanterns swinging. Blackwater. Her house sealed with Council wax on its stilts.

She didn't look back at it.

"That's it," said Kit. To the sea in front of her. "I'm unsalted. As of the chain."

She said it as she'd said everything that week. As a price.

---

Gannet was at the foot of the mainmast, coiling down.

"And the crew?" he said.

Kit didn't turn.

"Crew's crew," she said. "You're mine, not the League's. You signed my articles, not the Council's." She looked at the sea. "If they want you unsalted they'll have to catch you first."

Gannet smiled.

He went on coiling.

---

The old man was on deck.

Somehow. He'd come up the ladder from the hold on his own, with his blanket, and his shaking hands, and stood holding on to the shrouds by the mainmast with the wind in his thin hair, looking back at the inlet.

"Was that it?" he said. "The chain?"

"That was it," said Maren.

"I missed it." He sounded heartbroken. "I was asleep. On the lion. I slept through the whole thing." He looked at her. At her coat. At the arm inside it. He didn't ask. "Was it very exciting?"

"Yes," said Maren.

He sighed.

---

Dawn came on the ninety-fifth.

Grey. Over the sea. And she saw the Shatters for the first time.

---

Rock.

Everywhere. In every direction. As far as she could see. Reefs. Teeth of black rock coming up out of the sea, alone and in lines and in heaps, with white water breaking on them. Channels of dark water winding between. The swell coming in long and slow from the south and breaking white on every rock it met.

A sea of broken stone.

The Shattered Sea. Named for the split, the old man said. For the day the old realm broke and this was the piece that went under.

---

Kit read the book.

At the helm. With the book open on a board across the binnacle, and her finger on Abram's page. Slowly. She didn't have Jory. Jory read the turns by eye, by the shape of the rocks, without the book. Kit read them from the book, a page at a time, with Ferris in the bows calling the soundings, and Lorn on the sheet, and the brothers aloft looking for the next rock.

Slowly.

"The first pier in two days," she said. "If the book's right."

"Is it?"

"It's been right eighty years," said Kit. "It was right for my father."

---

Orrun was quiet.

All morning. She went down into the hold to him at sunrise and he was lying on the ballast as he'd lain all night, the whole length of the keel, on the far-stone, with his head on his paws. His eyes open. Gold. Looking at nothing.

She sat on the ladder's foot by his head.

She'd never known him quiet like this. On the Plains, in the dead country, at the Ysse fords, he'd always been talking. Grumbling. Asking. Telling her things about stone.

Here he lay on the ballast and listened.

---

*There's nothing,* he said at last.

She waited.

*No stone,* said Orrun. *For a hundred leagues. Every way. Only water. Moving.* He was quiet. *There's this.* She felt him press, very lightly, on the far-stone under him. *And far off, the road. The piers. One at a time. Very far apart.*

He put his chin down again.

*It's like standing on a coin,* he said. *In a river.*

He didn't say anything else all day.

---

The cry came from behind them.

At sunrise, while she was on the ladder by his head. Far off. Behind them. North, over the stern, from the coast.

Long. High. Thin with distance.

A dragon. Crying.

---

She went up on deck.

Behind them, far back, the black cliffs of the mountains' end, and the notch of Blackwater's inlet in them. And on the cliffs above the inlet, against the grey sky, so small she could barely see it, a speck.

Green.

Crying. Over and over. The same long high note.

---

Orrun lifted his head in the hold. She felt him.

*It's her,* he said.

"Can you hear her?"

*No. Too far. It's only a sound.* He listened. *It isn't for us.*

"Who's it for?"

He didn't know.

She stood at the rail with her one arm and watched the speck on the cliffs until the sea took it. Something had happened. At Corsa. To him, or to someone of his. Lisska didn't cry for nothing. She'd never heard her cry at all.

She didn't know what. She wouldn't, now, for a long time.

---

At noon Kit found the first turn.

A rock like a broken tooth, with a second rock behind it like its shadow, and a channel between them no wider than a cart-track, dark, deep, going south-west. She looked at the book. At Abram's drawing of two rocks, a tooth and its shadow, in a careful square hand eighty years old. Then at the rocks.

"The book's right," she said.

She put the helm over, and the *Kittiwake* went into the Shatters.
