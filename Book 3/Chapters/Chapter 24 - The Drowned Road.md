---

The first pier came on the ninety-seventh.

Out of open sea. They'd left the Shatters' reefs behind them the evening before: the last tooth of rock gone astern at sunset, the water going from white and broken to long and dark and smooth. Blue-black. Deep. Kit had sounded and found no bottom. Nobody had found a bottom out here, she said. Not even her father.

And at noon, ahead, standing up out of the sea, a column.

---

Square. Grey. Old stone, faced as only one kind of mason had ever faced stone. Standing up out of the deep water alone, twice as tall as the *Kittiwake*'s mast, broken off at the top in a jagged line. White with birds. The swell going round its foot, lifting, falling, breaking a little.

And on its broken top, where the birds were thickest, a stone. Upright. Square.

Kit had the glass on it. She handed it to Idony.

He held it in his two shaking hands against the shroud and looked a long time.

"*Into the light,*" he said. "*Forty-one.*"

Kit looked at the book. At Abram's first page. She didn't say anything. She didn't need to.

---

They went on.

South-west. Pier to pier. Two in a day, sometimes three, a column out of the sea ahead. The same square grey stone. Broken at different heights. Some no taller than the ketch's rail, the swell washing over their tops. Some taller than any tower she'd ever seen, with the birds going round them in white wheels. Each with its milestone.

Each one counting.

*Forty.*

*Thirty-nine.*

*Thirty-eight.*

---

The old man read every one.

From the deck, through Kit's glass, as they came up on each pier. Before Kit found it in the book. He was never wrong once. He'd say the number, and Kit would turn the page, and there it would be in Abram's square hand, copied stroke by stroke by a man who couldn't read it eighty years ago.

After the tenth pier, Kit stopped looking in the book first.

She'd wait. The old man would say the number. Then she'd turn the page.

---

It was a road.

She understood it on the third day, standing at the rail with her coat fastened to her throat, watching a pier go by. Not a line of rocks. A road. The piers had held something up once. A causeway. A bridge. Something long and straight and old-realm, running out over what had been land then and was sea now, south-west, toward the margin of the chart.

The world had split. The land had gone down. The road had gone down with it.

The piers were what was left. Standing up out of the sea where the road had been, half a day apart or less, saying how far.

*Into the light.*

---

Orrun felt them come and go.

She felt him feel them. Lying on the far-stone along the keel in the hold, with the old man's blanket in the angle of his shoulder, his eyes shut. As a pier came up ahead, he'd lift his head. A little. As if he heard something. As they passed it, he'd hold his head up. As it went astern, he'd put it down.

*Stone,* he said. The first day. *Real stone. Old. Like the arch.*

Then, as it went behind: *Gone.*

*It's like stepping-stones,* he said on the fourth day. *In a river. One. Then water. Then one.*

---

At night they hove to beside a pier.

Kit wouldn't sail the road at night. Her father hadn't. Abram hadn't. You lay to beside a pier on a long line, with the swell lifting you and the birds settling on the stone above, and you waited for light.

The crew slept aboard. Everyone did. The rule was for over there, but nobody on the *Kittiwake* would have slept on a pier anyway. They were columns in the sea.

And at night, beside the pier, Orrun breathed.

She'd lie on the ballast beside him and hear it. Long, slow breaths. In and out. As if he'd been holding his breath all day between the piers and had come up for air.

---

The old man and Kit talked freight on the hundredth day.

Maren hadn't known they could. She came up from the hold at noon and found them on the afterdeck, Kit at the tiller and Idony on the coaming beside her with his blanket round his shoulders, and they were talking about ropes.

"Hemp," Idony was saying. "Always hemp. My grandmother wouldn't have anything else. Three-strand. A new rope every second spring, whether it wanted it or not."

"Every second spring," said Kit. "On a ferry? Who paid for that?"

"The Wardens," said Idony. "When they crossed. A copper a head for people, two for a horse, a silver for a Warden's lynx, because the lynx made the horses jump." He smiled. "She said the lynx paid for the rope."

---

His grandmother had kept the ferry across Glasswater.

Under Vael's towers. A flat scow on a rope across the lake below the College, three generations, his mother's mother's. He'd grown up on it. He'd never said. Maren had known him since the Ring and he'd never said.

"You're a ferry family," said Kit.

"I'm a ferry family," said Idony. "So are you. Near enough." He looked at the pier going by. "A road over water. Somebody has to know the way across."

---

They talked for an hour.

Freight rates. Per head. Per beast. Per hundredweight of grain and per barrel of salt fish. What a rope cost and how long it lasted and what wore it out. What a ferry charged a Warden, and a salvage ketch charged a house. What you did with a passenger who wouldn't pay. Whether you charged a dead man's coffin as freight or as a passenger.

"Freight," said Kit.

"Passenger," said Idony. "My grandmother always said passenger. Half fare. She said they'd paid enough."

Kit laughed.

Out loud. On the afterdeck. A short bark of it, surprised out of her. Maren, at the rail, turned her head. Ferris, in the bows, turned his. It was the first time any of them had heard Kit laugh since Blackwater.

Kit stopped.

"Passenger," she said. "Half fare." She shook her head. "Your grandmother was soft."

"She was," said Idony happily. "She drowned three Wardens in forty years for not paying, and she was very soft about the dead."

---

Maren didn't follow half of it.

She sat on the coaming with her coat fastened to her throat and listened to them anyway. The old man's thin delighted voice and the reef-pilot's dry one, going back and forth about ropes and prices over the long blue swell, while the piers went by.

She'd have liked to stay there all day.

Her arm ached. Where it met her shoulder. Not the arm. She couldn't feel the arm. Where the feeling stopped.

---

The grey had gone past the shoulder.

On the ninety-ninth. She'd felt it go. In the night, on the ballast, lying on her right side with the dead arm across her. A cold, creeping, up over the round of her shoulder and along toward her neck, like water filling a hollow. In the morning she'd undone her coat in the dark of the hold and looked.

Grey. Over the shoulder. Along the collarbone. Stopping a finger's width short of the notch at her throat.

She'd fastened the coat again. To the top.

---

On the hundred-and-sixth it was on her ribs.

A patch. Under the arm, on her left side. The size of her palm. Grey. Cold. She found it because she lay on that side one night and couldn't feel her own heart.

She always could. Lying on her left side, she'd always been able to feel it. Since she was a child. Her heart against the bed. Steady.

She lay on her left side on the ballast in the dark with Orrun's breath going over her, and couldn't feel it.

She turned over. On her right side, she could. It was still going.

She lay there and listened to it a long time.

---

She kept her coat on.

In the heat. The sun out here in the middle of the sea was fierce by noon, and the crew went about in shirts, and Nye and Sim aloft went about in nothing much at all. Maren kept her coat on, fastened to the throat.

Tansy asked. On the hundred-and-eighth. The cook, broad and red-armed, coming up from the galley with a bucket and seeing her at the rail in her coat in the full sun.

"You'll boil in that, knight."

"I'm cold."

Tansy looked at her for a long moment.

"Cold," she said. And went on with her bucket, and didn't ask again.

---

Orrun knew.

He didn't say. He'd stopped saying things. Out here, he said very little at all. But at night on the ballast he'd lie with his great head close to her left side, close, so his breath was on the patch under her arm. As if he could warm it.

He couldn't. She didn't tell him that either.

---

The Bell passed them on the hundred-and-fourth.

At dawn. A league off to the east. She saw it first, from the rail, because she'd been awake all night.

A shape on the sea where there wasn't one. Long. Low. Black. Like a reef where the chart had none, a line of rock laid on the sea from north to south. And moving. South-west. The same way they were going. With the water piling white along its whole length, breaking on nothing.

And under the water, so faint she felt it in the deck more than heard it, every stride: the bell.

---

Kit came up beside her.

She'd been asleep. She came up from the cabin with her coat half on and stood at the rail and watched it.

"It's going the same way," she said at last.

"It slept on this road," said Idony.

He'd come up too. All of them had. The whole crew at the rail, in the dawn, watching it go. The old man held the shroud with both hands.

"Its temple was on it," he said. "The Drowned Bell. The old ones built the road past its temple, or the temple beside the road. It's going back along it." He watched the white line go. "Toward the light. Like us."

---

It passed ahead of them.

Slowly. Crossing their bows a league off, south-west, the white water breaking along it, the bell ringing under the sea. Kit didn't change course. There was nowhere to change it to. The road was the road.

It went out of sight before noon. Ahead of them. Toward the margin.

Kit stood at the rail until it was gone.

"My father's water," she said again. As she'd said it at the reef channel. Then she went back to the tiller.

---

The ninth pier came on the hundred-and-tenth.

*Into the light, nine.*

Idony said it, through the glass, and stopped. He didn't say anything else. He'd seen the page. He'd read the note in its margin at the chisel-bench under the Merrin house. *Hal. Squall. The boat.*

Kit hove to.

Beside the pier. Close. Closer than she'd come to any of them. The swell lifting the *Kittiwake* up the column's side and dropping her. A plain pier. Grey. Not tall. The milestone on its top leaning a little, as if something had struck it once.

---

Kit went below.

She came back with the dish. The chipped clay dish, with the coarse grey salt in it.

She stood at the rail with it. She took a pinch of the salt between her finger and thumb, and held it a moment over the water. Then she let it go.

It went into the sea at the pier's foot. White. Then nothing.

---

Ferris took his cap off.

Old Ferris, the mate, who'd sailed with Hal Merrin for thirty years and been in the bows that day eleven years ago with the squall coming down. He stood at the helm with his grey head bare in the sun and looked at the pier.

Nobody else moved.

Then Kit took the dish below, and came back up, and took the tiller from Ferris.

"On," she said.

---

*There's nobody,* said Orrun.

The ninety-eighth day. The first day past the first pier. She'd been lying on the ballast in the night, and it had come to her slowly that something was missing.

All her life since the bond, since the river, there'd been something at the edge. Through Orrun. Faint. Always there. Other beasts. The hounds in Greyhold's kennels. The horses in the Long Barn. Later, the stonehorns, the wolves, the farm cats in the Marches, a fox. Lisska, when she was near. Kharrak, like a fire in the next room. Aurveth, deep and slow, like a well. Never words. Just the edge of other minds, there, as a room has other people in it even when nobody's speaking.

Out past the first pier, there wasn't anything.

---

*Nobody,* said Orrun. *Every way. Only us.*

She lay on the ballast and listened.

Nothing. Not a bird; the birds on the piers had nothing in them that came through. Not a fish. Not a reefwyrm. The sea, going away in every direction for a hundred leagues, and in it, nothing that could hear him, and nothing he could hear.

Only her. And him.

---

She'd never been alone in her head.

Not since she was twenty-seven. Not since an old grey lion on an island in a river had looked at her one last time and decided not to kill her. She'd forgotten what it was like. She'd forgotten it could be like this. A great quiet, all the way out to the edge of the world, with nothing in it.

She lay on her right side on the far-stone, with her heart going against the stone where she could feel it, and Orrun's breath going over her, and listened to nothing at all.

It went on for seventeen days.

---

The second-last pier came on the hundred-and-fourteenth.

*Into the light, two.*

Idony said it at dusk. His voice shook, and not from his hands.

Kit hove to beside it for the night. They lay on the long line with the swell lifting them and the birds settling, white, on the stone above.

And to the south-west, low on the sea, under the edge of the dark, there was a light.

---

Faint. Not a star. Lower than any star. Under the horizon. A glow, pale, spread along the edge of the sea, as a town's lamps glow over the brow of a hill at night when you're coming home along the road and can't see the town yet.

The whole crew came to the rail.

Nobody said anything. Kit stood at the tiller with the book shut under her hand. The old man held the shroud with both his shaking hands and looked at the light with his mouth open.

Maren stood at the rail in her coat fastened to the throat, with her dead arm inside it, and her heart going on the side she could feel.

*Into the light,* she thought. *One more.*

In the hold below her, on the far-stone, she felt Orrun lift his head.
