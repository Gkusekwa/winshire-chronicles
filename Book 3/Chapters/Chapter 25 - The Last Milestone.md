---

*Into the light, one.*

The old man said it at noon and sat down on the deck.

His legs went and he let them, and he sat on the planks by the mainmast with Kit's glass in his two shaking hands, looking at the pier, and didn't say anything else.

Kit didn't need the glass. She knew this pier. She'd seen it eight times.

---

It stood at the edge of the shelf.

The last of them. Taller than most. Grey. Square. Not broken at the top like the others: whole, or nearly, with its milestone standing upright on it under a ring of white birds. And beside it, going away to either side as far as she could see, the shelf.

Reef. Old stone. Lying under the clear water a fathom down, two fathoms, broken, in slabs and blocks and long straight edges that a reef didn't have. The road. What was left of the road where it had come down to the land. A league of it, from the deep water to the lagoon, with the sea breaking white along its outer edge where the swell met it.

And beyond the shelf, the lagoon. Pale green. Calm.

And beyond the lagoon, four leagues off, low, long, pale in the noon light, the shore.

---

She didn't look at the shore.

Her father hadn't. He'd taught her that. On the last stretch, you don't look at the shore. You look at the Needle.

The Needle was a gap. In the shelf, beside the last pier, on its western side. A cut through the reef, straight, narrow, a ketch's width and a little more, where two slabs of the old road had come apart when the world split and left a channel between them. Six fathoms in the Needle. At the top of the flood. Less, every hour after.

Abram had found it. It was on his last page but one. A drawing of the pier, and the gap beside it, and a line through the gap. And under the line, in his square careful hand: *top of flood only.*

---

"Top of the flood's in two hours," she said.

Ferris nodded. He'd been in the bows for thirty years of Hal's crossings. He'd gone in through the Needle with her father more times than she had.

"Two hours," he said. "Lie off till then."

They lay off. A cable from the pier. The swell lifting them and dropping them, long and slow and blue. The birds going round the pier's top. The old man on the deck with the glass in his lap, looking at the milestone, with his lips moving.

---

The Bell came up an hour later.

Half a league off. To the west. Along the shelf's edge, in the deep water outside it.

She saw the sea go strange first. A long smooth hump, rising, out where there'd been only swell. Then white along it. Then the hump broke open and something stood up out of it.

---

She'd seen it on the road. A league off, a black line on the sea going away. She hadn't seen it stand up.

It stood up out of the sea at the edge of the shelf as a cliff would stand up if a cliff could. Black. Streaming. Sheets of water pouring off it, off the plates of its back, off the broken dome and the columns that stood up on its back like the ribs of a burned ship. Weed hanging. And in the dome, the bell.

Swinging.

It rang once.

---

She felt it in her teeth. Her chest. The deck. The sea itself seemed to ring with it, a note so deep and long it wasn't a sound so much as the water remembering one. *Down.* It went out over the shelf and the lagoon and the shore and came back.

Then the thing stood still.

At the shelf's edge. At the end of the road. As if it had come all the way along the road, as they had, from milestone to milestone, and this was where the road stopped, and it had stopped too.

---

The swell came after it.

Off its body. Off the place where it had stood up out of the sea. A long hump of water, glassy, not breaking yet, running along the shelf's outer edge from west to east. Toward the pier. Toward the Needle.

Toward them.

"Ferris," said Kit.

He was already at the tiller. "We're not at the flood."

"We're not staying here."

---

She put the *Kittiwake* at the Needle.

An hour early. Four fathoms in it, not six. She knew that. She put her at it anyway, because the swell was coming and a cable off the shelf's edge was the worst place in the sea to be when it came, worse than the Needle, worse than anything. Her father had told her. *If anything comes up out there, get inside. Whatever it costs. Get inside.*

The ketch went in.

Between the slabs. Old stone on either side, a fathom down, pale through the clear water. The pier going by on her port hand, close, the birds lifting off it screaming. The gap ahead. Straight. Narrow.

And the swell came in behind her.

---

It lifted the *Kittiwake* from the stern.

She felt the ketch go up. Up. The bow going down, the stern rising, the whole ship tilting forward and down the face of the water into the Needle like a sledge on a slope. Faster than she'd ever gone in. The slabs on either side going by in a blur.

And sideways.

The swell wasn't running straight down the Needle. It was running along the shelf, and the Needle cut across it, and the water came into the gap from the side, and took the *Kittiwake* by her quarter and threw her.

At the eastern slab.

At the rock.

---

Ferris had the tiller hard over. It made no difference.

She saw the slab coming. Grey. Square-edged. Just under the surface, under the white water now, the old road, that had lain there since the world split. That had been under her father's keel eight times.

They were going to hit it broadside.

---

"Maren," she said.

She didn't know why. She didn't know what she expected. She said it.

---

The knight was at the forehatch.

On her knees. Kit saw her. In her coat fastened to the throat, with one arm inside it. The other arm, the right, was down through the forehatch, into the hold.

To the ballast.

To the far-stone, and the lion lying on it.

---

The sea round the *Kittiwake* stood up.

Not the swell. Something in it. Grey. On the slab ahead, on the eastern side, in the white water, a shape came up out of the stone itself. Out of the drowned road. As if it had been waiting under the slab all along, and had stood up.

A lion.

Stone. Grey. As big as Orrun. Standing waist-deep on the slab in the breaking sea.

It put its shoulder to the *Kittiwake*'s side.

---

She felt the ketch stop.

The whole length of her. As if she'd struck, but softly, on something that gave a little and held. The planking groaned. The mast shook. The swell went on past, under her, over the stone shoulder, white, roaring.

The *Kittiwake* hung there in the Needle with a stone lion holding her off the rock.

Then there was another.

---

On the western slab. Out of the stone. Rising. Shaking the water off. Another lion, as big, standing in the sea on the other side of the Needle, and putting its shoulder to the other side of the ketch.

Two.

Two grey lions standing in the sea on the drowned road, on either side of the Needle, waist-deep in the white water, with the swell breaking over their backs.

---

They passed her along.

From one to the other. Shoulders on her sides, as the swell went by and threw her and she came off one and onto the other, and the two of them walking with her along the slabs. Through the Needle. Along the gap. Hand to hand, as two men pass a bucket at a fire, except the men were lions and the bucket was her ship and the fire was the sea.

The *Kittiwake* went through the Needle between them.

Kit stood at the rail and watched.

---

She didn't think, for a long time.

Then she did.

She'd thought the lion was a beast. A big one. The biggest on the coast, the rumours said, bigger every time somebody told it. A beast the knight had tamed, or bonded, or whatever it was they did in Hearthmere. That moved stone, as a smith moves iron. She'd priced it as a beast. She'd priced its days.

Not a beast.

She watched the two of them in the sea, standing on the drowned road, made of it. Coming up out of the old stone her family had sailed over for eighty years and never known was anything but rock. Something the stone was doing. Something that had been here before the road, before the split, before Abram. Before anything.

Her father's water was full of grey lions.

He'd never known.

---

"Hal," she said.

She hadn't meant to. It came out.

In the Needle, with the stone lions passing her ship along, on the water her father had talked about at night in the stern cabin with the lamp swinging. The Needle. *Top of the flood only.* Where he'd always gone in. Where he'd brought her the first time, ten years old, and stood behind her at the tiller with his long hands over hers.

He'd drowned at the ninth. Not here. But this was his water. He'd talked about it more than any other.

"Hal," she said again. Quietly. And then nothing else.

Ferris, at the tiller, didn't look at her.

---

They came out of the Needle into the lagoon.

Calm. All at once. Out of the white water and the roaring and the grey shapes, into pale green water, flat, clear, with white sand under it a fathom down and the ketch sliding over it as if it were glass.

Behind them, the two lions sank back into the shelf.

She watched them go. Down into the stone, one and then the other, the water closing over them. Then there was only the reef, and the white water breaking on its edge, and the pier, and the birds settling on it again.

And beyond the pier, at the shelf's edge, the Bell. Standing. Not moving. The bell silent.

---

The knight was sitting against the forehatch coaming.

When Kit came forward. Her right arm out of the hatch, in her lap. Her head back against the coaming. Her eyes open.

Kit stopped.

---

She'd seen the coat. Fastened to the throat, every day, in the sun. She'd seen the arm inside it. She'd seen the knight's left hand on the brass seal in the stern cabin at Blackwater, grey at the fingertips. She'd not said anything. It wasn't hers to say.

She could see it now.

Above the coat's collar. On the left side of the knight's neck. Grey. Up from under the coat, up the side of the throat, under the ear. Up to the hinge of the jaw.

And the corner of her mouth.

The left corner. It didn't move. When the knight looked up at her and spoke, the right side of her mouth moved and the left stayed where it was, still, grey at its edge, as if it belonged to someone else.

---

"Are we in?" said Maren.

"We're in."

"Good."

Kit looked at her neck.

"What is that?" she said.

The knight didn't put her hand up to it. She didn't look away.

"It's paid," she said.

"By whom?"

"By me," said Maren.

---

They anchored a league off the shore at dusk.

In the lagoon. In four fathoms, on white sand. Kit had the hook down and the sails furled and the anchor-light up before the sun touched the sea, as her father had always done. As Abram had.

Then she called them all on deck.

The crew. Ferris and Lorn and Tansy and the brothers and Corrie. Gannet. The old man. The knight. And down through the hold grating, the lion, listening.

"Nobody goes ashore tonight," she said. "Nobody sleeps ashore, ever. That's the rule. You all know it. It's in your articles." She looked at each of them. "I don't know why. My father didn't. My great-grandfather made it and never said. But I've kept it eight times and so did he and we came home." She looked at the shore. "Keep it."

That wasn't all of it. She'd heard the rest once, from her father, at the tiller. She'd not say it with the knight listening.

Nobody said anything.

Gannet smiled.

---

The lights came up after dark.

She'd seen them eight times. She'd never got used to them.

Out of the hills behind the shore. Light coming up out of the hills themselves, out of the ground, pale, slow, as mist comes up off a field on an autumn evening. Moving. Rising and spreading along the ridges and sinking back, and rising again further along. White. Gold at the edges. Not bright. You could look straight at it.

And on the shore, in the light, shapes.

Long. Low. Square-edged. Half under the white sand. Buildings, or what had been. Columns. A line of something that might be a wall going down to the water.

And over all of it, so faint she felt it more than heard it, a sound.

Like breathing. Very far off. Very slow.

---

The whole crew was at the rail.

Nobody went below. Nobody talked. They stood along the rail of the *Kittiwake* in the lagoon and watched the light come up out of the hills and go down again, and up again, and the old man stood with both his shaking hands on the shroud and tears running down his face and didn't wipe them.

Kit stood at the stern by the anchor-light.

---

The knight was at the bow rail.

Alone. Her coat fastened to the throat. The grey on her neck pale in the light from the shore.

Kit went forward. She stopped a little way off.

The knight's lips were moving.

Kit listened.

---

*One. Two. Three.*

Under her breath. Very quiet. The right side of her mouth moving and the left not.

*Four. Five. Six.*

She was looking at the light on the hills.

Kit watched the light. It was rising. Spreading along the nearest ridge, slow, slow.

*...fifty-eight. Fifty-nine. Sixty.*

The light stopped rising. Held.

The knight stopped counting. Waited. Kit waited with her.

Then the light began to sink.

And the knight began again. Under her breath.

*One. Two. Three.*

---

Kit didn't ask what she was counting.

She went back to the stern, to the anchor-light, and sat on the coaming with her back against the rail and her father's book on her knees, shut, and watched the light breathe on the hills of the shore at the end of the road.
