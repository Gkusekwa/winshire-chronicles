---

The old one was going.

He'd known it since Coldtarn. He'd known it and not said it, as you don't say a thing out loud about a sick animal in the byre in case saying it makes it so. He'd watched her and said nothing and fed her mutton and she'd eaten less of it every day.

He said it to himself on the hundred-and-twelfth, on the black cliff above Blackwater, looking at her in the morning light.

She was going.

---

Her bronze had gone dull.

That was the first thing. Not the colour; the shine. Aurveth's plates had always had a shine to them, deep, like a copper pan rubbed with sand and vinegar till you could see your face in it. Now they looked like a pan left out in the yard all winter. Dull. Brown at the edges.

The plates along her spine stood up a little. Proud of her back. Because the flesh under them was going.

She slept facing south-west. Every night. She ate a sheep in two days now, where she'd eaten one every day for as long as he'd known her. The wing that held was weaker; he'd felt it on the flight down from the terrace, felt her drop and catch herself and drop.

*You're refusing it,* he'd said. On the flight. *The call. And it's taking you.*

She hadn't answered.

---

He hadn't come to Blackwater for her.

He'd come for the fleet.

Lisska had seen it. On the hundred-and-tenth, flying the coast east, looking for the Bell's wake. She'd come back to the terrace and said: in the black inlet where they keep the dragons in the shed, the Council's ships are fitting out. Building rooms in their holds. Loading water for a month.

Like the salvage one, she'd said. The one with the lion.

---

He'd left the terrace on the hundred-and-eleventh.

Dorcas had watched him go from the *Merry Agnes*'s rail. She hadn't said anything. The terrace was a town now. Forty-one hulks on a hill and a market between them and the gutters going down to the quays that weren't quays any more, to gut fish off boats that came in to the shingle.

The forty pairs were Harl's. The Gullhaven families were in his top field. The coast was holding.

And at Blackwater, the Council was selling berths on a road to a shore nobody came back from.

---

Brask was in Blackwater.

He found him on the hundred-and-twelfth, on the shingle, at a sellswords' fire, with what was left of a company round him. Coll Brask. Who knew everyone. Who'd once given up what he knew to Calder a piece at a time to keep his legs, and then kept his word. Who couldn't write, and paid clerks to write his letters, and never paid them enough.

He looked up at Calder from the fire as if he'd been expecting him all week.

"Farmer."

"Brask."

"I've a message," said Brask. "From the Merrin woman. Before she went." He held up a hand. "No price. She paid it."

---

He said it word for word.

He did everything that way. Exactly. As if a word were a coin and he'd been given a certain number to pass on and wouldn't be short.

"*Tell the farmer, if he comes. The Berths are a grave. Nobody sleeps ashore over there and wakes. My great-grandfather's brother did, the first time. They left him on the sand with his eyes open. That's what the rule's for.*" Brask paused, as a clerk pauses at a full stop. "*I've not told the knight. She'll keep it anyway.*"

He looked at the fire.

"That's the lot," he said.

---

Calder sat down on the shingle.

*Nobody sleeps ashore over there and wakes.*

He thought about the board on the boathouse door. He'd seen it from the air coming in. *A land without Titans.* Six hundred gold a head, now. Children half. Five hundred and forty people had paid it. The rich of Corsa. Their families. Their children.

On a shore where if you lay down and slept, you didn't get up.

---

He went to the Council on the hundred-and-thirteenth.

In the boathouse. On foot, with Kharrak on the roofless walls above, so that nobody would say he hadn't been heard. The sea-chests in their row. Celia Ostrey in her grey silk at the end of the row, in the Speaker's place.

He told her.

Brask's words. Kit's. The brother on the sand with his eyes open. The rule. And the Bell: that it had gone south over the Shatters on the ninetieth, along the road.

---

Celia Ostrey listened.

Calmly. All the way through. She didn't interrupt. When he'd finished she sat a moment with her hands folded on the sea-chest in front of her.

"A reef-pilot's tale," she said. "Told by a sellsword. For a salvage family that has kept a route to itself for eighty years and has just had it taken for the League's need." She looked at him. "You'll understand, Master Fenn, that the Council has heard a great many tales this week from people who would rather it didn't sail."

"And the Bell?"

"The Bell is south of the Shatters," said Celia Ostrey. "The fleet goes south-west. Ostrey's pilots have the Merrin charts." She stood. "The fleet sails on the hundred-and-eighteenth."

The board stayed up.

---

Jory came on the hundred-and-fourteenth.

At night. Up the rocks under the eastern headland where Calder was camped with the dragons, above the inlet's mouth and the Council's chain, which had been mended with iron this time, every shackle. Calder heard him before he saw him. A sound in the dark below the camp like a seal coming up out of the water onto the rocks. Breathing hard.

Then a thin shape, climbing. Wet through. Shaking.

Lisska put her head over the rock and looked at him, and he stopped dead.

---

"Don't," said Calder. To Lisska. "He's cold."

He went down and got the boy by the wrist and pulled him up the last of the rocks onto the ledge by the fire.

Nineteen. Thin. Brown. Long hands. His teeth going. He'd swum the inlet's mouth in the dark, under the chain, from Ostrey's ship where two of Rennick's men were supposed to be watching him and were asleep over their dice.

"You're the farmer," he said. Through his teeth.

"Yes."

"I'm Jory Merrin." He held his hands out to the fire. "Kit's brother. I'm the Council's pilot." He laughed, shaking. "They'll drown."

---

He told it at the fire.

With Calder's coat round him and his hands round a cup. The Council's two pilots. Ostrey's men. Quenby and Loy. Good pilots, he said. The best in the Saltings. They'd sailed the coast and the marsh channels forty years each.

They'd never been past the Shatters.

"They've my father's charts," said Jory. "From the house. The turns. Pier to pier, as he drew them for himself." He looked at the fire. "They read them off the paper. As if the paper were the sea."

---

"And it isn't."

"No." Jory shook his head. "The rocks move. In the Shatters. Some years. The book's corrected every voyage and Father's charts are older than the last corrections. Two of the piers have fallen since he drew them. One of the turns he got wrong his whole life and Kit fixed it in the book after he died." He drank. "They don't know you don't sail the road at night. They don't know about the Needle. *Top of flood only.* They don't know which rocks you can't see till you're on them." He put the cup down. "They don't know anything. They know paper."

He looked at Calder.

"Eleven ships," he said. "And Rennick's three. Five hundred and forty people. They'll drown. All of them. The passengers. Everyone."

---

"And you," said Calder.

"And me." Jory picked the cup up again. "They'll find me gone in the morning and come looking and take me back. And I'll go, because if I don't they'll sail anyway with Quenby and Loy reading paper. And I'll take them in as far as I can." He looked at the fire. "And when it goes wrong, it'll be my name. *The Merrin pilot.* On every board in the League."

He said it quite quietly.

"I'd rather it was my name and five hundred people alive," he said. "But it won't be."

---

Lisska flew south-west on the hundred-and-fifteenth.

He sent her. To see. Out over the Shatters, along the line the road took, as far as she dared. He wanted to know if they could find it from the air. Follow the fleet. Find the shore. Without a Merrin.

She came back at dusk. Tired. She'd flown a long way.

*It's not there,* she said.

"What isn't?"

*Anything. From up here.* She lay down on the ledge by the fire. *Past the rocks there's just sea. Every way the same. The stones stick up out of it, far apart, and from high up they look like nothing, like birds' rocks, and from low down you can't see the next one.* She put her head down. *I couldn't tell one way from another after the first day. No shore. No light. Not by day. If I'd gone on I'd not have found the way back.*

---

"You find it by the stones," said Jory.

He was by the fire, in Calder's coat. He'd not gone back. He'd hidden in the rocks all day and come back up at dusk.

"Island to island," he said. "Each pier's top is broken in its own shape. You learn the shapes. Each milestone's got a number. The bearing to the next changes at each one, a little. You go from stone to stone. You never see the shore till the last one." He looked at the fire. "Kit reads them with the book. I read them by eye. I've never needed the book."

He looked up at the dragons.

"You'd need someone who reads them," he said. "From up there. Or you'd be lost the first day."

---

He chose on the hundred-and-sixteenth.

At night. On the headland, by the dragons. Jory asleep by the fire in his coat. Kharrak along the ledge. Lisska on a rock above. And the old one, at the ledge's end, lying where she always lay now, facing south-west, thin, dull, her great head on her paws and her eyes open on the dark sea.

He sat by her a long time.

---

He thought about the coast.

The terrace. Forty-one hulks on a hill. Dorcas on the *Merry Agnes* with her pan. The gutters walking down to the shingle. The Gullhaven families in his top field, and Hessa Pane, and her mother with her tin cup. The forty pairs under Harl. The Council gone. Corsa gone. The Bell out there on the road.

He thought about Wren.

He thought about two thousand people on a hill and five hundred and forty people on eleven ships, sailing to a shore where you slept and didn't wake, with two pilots reading paper.

---

He thought about the old one.

Dying. On a ledge. Of a call he wouldn't answer. As she'd lain a year on the shore at Coldtarn with her head in the water after Ragnald, and not died, because she'd been waiting for something.

She'd found it. It was calling her by a name she hadn't known she had. And he'd said *no*, by a fire on Coldtarn's rim, *the coast needs me*, and she'd come with him anyway, and turned her head south-west every few wingbeats all the way down the Scarfell, and turned it back.

*You can wait a bit longer,* he'd said.

She'd waited. She was dying of it.

---

He'd let things happen to him all his life.

The thought came to him plainly, by the fire, with the sea dark below. The war. The ford. The dragons themselves, three of them, in his head, not one of them asked for. The outlawry. The coast. He'd done what was in front of him, every time, and called it duty, and mostly it had been. He'd never once stood on a road with two ways off it and picked one because he wanted to.

He looked at the old one.

"All right," he said.

---

She didn't move.

"I'll go," said Calder. "After the fleet. Over the sea. With the boy on your back reading the stones." He put his hand on her neck. The plates were cold. "If it goes wrong they'll need someone there who can lift. And you'll go where you're called. And I'll go with you, because I'm not letting you go on your own."

He felt her listening.

"I'm choosing it," he said. "Not because it's happening to me. Because I want to."

---

Aurveth lifted her head.

For the first time since the pour. Off her paws. Slowly. And turned it. Away from the sea. Toward him.

The great gold eye, the size of a cartwheel hub. That had come round on him once on Coldtarn's shore, in the autumn Haldor died, as if it were trying to remember a word in a language it used to know.

It rested on him now.

*Good,* she said.

That was all. Then she put her head down again, facing south-west, and closed her eyes, and slept. He felt her sleep. Properly. For the first time in eighteen days.

---

"On the old one?" said Jory, in the morning.

He was sitting up in Calder's coat, looking at Aurveth along the ledge as if he'd been asked to ride a mountain.

"She's the one who wants to go," said Calder.

---

Arn came on the hundred-and-seventeenth.

On Ildra. Down out of the north over the black cliffs at noon, with twenty pairs behind him. Calder had sent Lisska to Varrhold with a letter on the hundred-and-fifteenth, before he'd chosen, in case. Arn had come the day it reached him.

He'd never seen Arn fly. Arn came down on the headland on the young dark dragon with her broken horn, and got off her stiffly, as a man gets off a horse after a long first day, and stood with his hand on her neck.

"Your letter said the coast," said Arn.

"The coast's yours."

"It's not mine."

"It's not mine either," said Calder. "It's Harl's and Dorcas Kell's and Hessa Pane's and two thousand people's on a hill. Somebody has to fly over it."

Arn looked at him a long time.

"All right," he said.

---

He gave Calder a letter.

From inside his coat. Folded small. Calder didn't know the hand on the outside at first. Big. Crooked. In charcoal.

*PAPA.*

The D in it the right way round.

---

He opened it on the headland, with Arn watching and pretending not to.

Two lines. Big letters, crooked, each one made separately, as he'd made his own. She'd learned. In the caves, from Ada, or from Bel, or from Gorm Askell's ledgers, or from somewhere.

*Papa. Bring back something from the end of the world.*

*Wren.*

---

He folded it again. Small.

He put it inside his coat, against his chest. Next to the copper Marrow had given him across the table in the Tidehall's back passage, with the ship-and-wave worn almost off, that he hadn't spent.

"She made me promise to give it you," said Arn. "In your hand. Not to a clerk."

"Thank you."

"Don't," said Arn. And went to see to Ildra.

---

The fleet sailed on the hundred-and-eighteenth.

At dawn. At the top of the flood. Out through the Council's chain, which was lowered for them, and each ship paid at the window that had been built in a hut on the western headland, by tonnage. Eleven ships. Rennick's three black ones behind.

And on the deck of Ostrey's flagship, at the rail, between two of Rennick's men, a thin brown boy with long hands.

Jory had gone back himself. Before light. Swum out the way he'd come, so they'd not have to look for him and not search the headland. *I'll be on the deck at the first pier,* he'd said. *Two days out. Come and get me.*

---

They went out over the Shatters at the fleet's pace.

Low. Over the masts. Kharrak and Lisska and Aurveth, slowly, in the morning, over eleven ships and three black ones going south through the reefs in a long line with Ostrey's pilots in the bows reading paper.

On Rennick's ship, at the stern, a big man with a gold ring in one ear looked up.

At three dragons over his mast.

He raised his hand.

---

Aurveth flew steadier than she had in a month.

He felt it. Under him. Beside him. The wing that held, holding. Her great dull head up and pointed south-west, not turning any more, not turning back. Going where she was called. With him.

Behind them, on the black headland above Blackwater, Arn on a young dark dragon, watching them go.

And in his coat, against his chest, a letter in charcoal with the D the right way round.

*Bring back something from the end of the world.*

He thought he might.
