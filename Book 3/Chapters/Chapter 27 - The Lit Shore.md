---

The road came up out of the sea.

She saw it from the boat. They'd lain three days in the lagoon at anchor, nobody ashore, watching the light rise and fall on the hills at night and the sand go white in the sun by day, and on the morning of the hundred-and-nineteenth Kit had said *now*, and they'd put the boat over.

And as Ferris rowed them in across the shallows, she looked down.

Under the clear green water, a road.

---

The same stone. The same grey, square-cut, faced. The drowned road's causeway, that had held up the piers, coming up out of the deep water under the lagoon. Rising. Under the boat, a fathom down, then half a fathom, then a hand's depth, with white sand drifted on it and little fish going over it. Straight. Toward the beach.

It came up out of the sea onto the sand.

She watched it come. The water getting shallower over it. Then the stone breaking the surface. Then a paved way, dry, grey, a cart's width and more, running up the beach between the dunes and on, inland. Straight as a rule. Into the land.

---

Orrun was already on it.

He'd swum. He'd hated it. He came up out of the lagoon onto the end of the road where it left the water and stood on it, streaming, and shook himself, and stood still.

*Stone,* he said. *All of it. Everywhere.*

She felt him feel it. Through her. The whole shore. Under the sand, under the dunes, under the grass on the hills beyond. Stone. Full. Humming. As far as he could reach.

He didn't say anything else. He stood on the road with the water running off him and his eyes half shut, as he'd stood on the hill at Greyhold in the morning when the ground was good.

---

At the tide-line, a milestone.

Square. Grey. Standing at the road's edge where the sand met the paving. Waist-high. And on its face, the hooked letters, sharp, hardly worn.

The old man got out of the boat into the shallows before anyone could help him, and waded to it with the sea round his knees, and put both his shaking hands on it.

*Into the light.*

No number.

He stood there with his hands on it. Kit stood behind him in the water, quiet.

This was the stone Abram had copied. Eighty years ago. On his last page.

---

The city was on the sand.

Either side of the road. Under the dunes. She hadn't seen it from the ketch, not properly: shapes, at night, in the light. By day she saw what it was.

Stone. The same. Square-cut. Column-tops sticking up out of the white sand in rows, as if the columns were standing in the sand to their chins. Roof-lines, flat, with the sand drifted over them. A wall going down to the water, straight, its top just showing. Doorways, half full of sand, dark inside. And going up into the biggest dune, a flight of steps. Wide. Hundreds of them. Grey. Up and up into the sand, to where the dune's top was, and something on it.

All of it humming.

She could feel it in her teeth. In her boots. In the dead arm, almost.

---

There were no people.

That was what she noticed. After the stone. No people. No bones. No tools. No broken pots, no ash of old fires, no rubbish. Nothing anyone had left behind. She'd been in ruins all her life: the Ruin itself, Orrin, the Long Library. Ruins had things in them. Rubbish. The things people dropped when they ran.

This didn't.

As if everyone had got up one morning, and put everything away, and gone out, and not come back.

*Into the light,* she thought.

---

The old man's glass climbed to the top.

He set it out on the great steps, halfway up, in the sun. His best one, on its brass foot. He knelt beside it on the warm stone and watched.

It went up as if somebody had poured it. All the way. To the top of its column. And stayed there.

He looked at it.

"It can't go higher," he said. "There isn't any higher." He laughed, very quietly. "It's full. All of it. The whole shore."

His hands were on his knees. She looked at them. They were still shaking. But less. Much less. Ashore, on the steps, with the stone humming under him, they shook as an old man's hands shake, not as a burned man's do.

He didn't notice. Or he did, and didn't say.

---

The beasts didn't run.

That was the next thing. The deer first. Small, dun-coloured, with white under their tails, that came out of the dunes on the second morning while Kit was filling casks at the spring under the steps. Three of them. They walked up to her. They smelled her coat. One of them put its nose against her hand.

Kit stood very still with a cask in her other hand and let it.

"They don't know," she said. Very quietly. "They don't know what I am."

---

A hare sat by Maren's boot.

On the road, in the sun. It came out of the grass and sat down beside her foot and washed its face. She looked down at it. It looked up at her. Then it went on washing.

Birds came down on the old man.

Sea-birds. Grey and white. They landed on his shoulders while he sat on the steps beside his glass, and on his knees, and one landed on his shaking hand and stood there, holding on, riding the shake as if it were a branch in the wind. He held his hand very still for it, as still as he could. Tears ran down his face.

"They've never seen us," he said. "Not anybody. Ever."

---

A fox slept in a doorway.

Half under the sand, at the foot of the steps. Red. Curled up in the sun. It opened one eye when they went by, and looked at the lion, and closed it again.

On the dunes, a herd of small grey horses grazing. They lifted their heads when Orrun came along the road. They looked at him.

They went back to grazing.

---

They went back aboard every dusk.

Kit's rule. Ashore by day only. Back aboard before the sun touched the sea. The boat never left; one of the crew always in it, at the road's end. The crew came ashore by day in pairs to fill the casks at the spring under the steps: fresh water, sweet, cold, humming in the cask. Tansy said it was the best water she'd ever drawn. She said it tasted of nothing.

Gannet came ashore every day.

Every day. With a canvas bag. He went off up the steps and among the column-tops alone, and came back at dusk with the bag heavy, and his coat pockets heavy, and went down the hold without saying what was in them.

Kit watched him do it. She didn't stop him. Merrins took stone home. That was what Merrins did.

---

Orrun saw it on the hundred-and-twenty-second.

From the top of the great steps.

They'd climbed them that morning, all of them, the hundreds of wide grey steps going up into the dune, the old man on Orrun's back because he couldn't manage them. At the top, the dune's crest, and on it, half under the sand, a platform. Square. Paved. With a low wall round it. Looking inland.

At the hills.

---

Green hills. Short grass, close-cropped, as if sheep had been on them, though there weren't any sheep. Rolling away inland from the dunes, fold behind fold, a day's walk, two days', to the edge of sight. The road going away into them, straight, grey, a line drawn on the green.

And on the farthest hills, something moving.

---

She thought it was a cloud first. A cloud's shadow. Grey, going along a ridge.

Then she saw it had a shape.

Long. Low. Grey as old stone. Moving along the top of the far ridge, slowly, as a thing moves that has all the time there is. And then stopping. And lying down, in the hills, so that it was a hill itself, a long grey hill on top of a green one.

And then rising again.

A great cat's shape. Lying, and rising, and lying. As big as a hill. As big as the thing that had broken Greyhold. Bigger.

---

Orrun stood up.

He'd been lying on the platform in the sun. He stood up all at once, the old man sliding off his back, and stood at the low wall, and looked.

She felt it go through him.

She didn't know what it was. She'd felt him angry and afraid and grieving, and tired, and stubborn as a hill. She'd felt him at ten paces from his kin at the Hollow Ring, lying down in the grey grass, with the grief coming up out of him like water out of rock.

This wasn't that.

*It's...* said Orrun.

And nothing else.

---

She went with him on the hundred-and-twenty-third.

She told Kit at dawn. On the beach, at the road's end, with the boat in the shallows and the ketch behind it in the lagoon.

"He's going," she said. "Inland. To that. I'm going with him."

"Back by dusk," said Kit.

"I won't be back by dusk. It's two days."

Kit looked at her.

"You swore the rule," she said. "Salt on the tongue."

"I'll keep it." Maren looked inland, at the road. "I won't sleep. I'll stay awake."

---

"Two days," said Kit. "And two back. Four nights."

"I've stood watch longer."

Kit was quiet. She looked at the knight in her coat fastened to the throat, with the grey on her neck, and her dead arm inside the coat, and the left corner of her mouth not moving.

"If you sleep," said Kit, "I don't know what happens. My great-grandfather's brother slept. On the sand. Here." She looked at the beach. "They left him with his eyes open."

Maren looked at her.

"I didn't tell you," said Kit. "I'm telling you now."

"I won't sleep," said Maren.

---

The old man wanted to come.

He was furious when Kit said no. He stood on the beach in his blanket with the sea-birds on his shoulders and argued, and Kit said no, and he argued, and Kit said that he was the oldest man aboard and the only one who could read the stones and if he fell asleep in the hills she'd have to carry his body home, and she wouldn't.

He went back aboard. Grumbling. Maren heard him all the way across the lagoon in the boat.

---

They walked inland on the road.

Two days. Between the green hills, on the paved grey stone, with the short grass on either side humming. Milestones every league. Each with the hooked letters. Each with no number. *Into the light. Into the light. Into the light.* As if the whole land were the end of the road.

The deer came out of the hills and walked beside them a while and went away. Hares. Birds. Once, far off on a hill, something big and dark that might have been a bear, sitting in the grass, watching them pass, not moving.

Orrun didn't speak.

He walked. Steadily. Toward the far hills. His head up.

---

The light came up at night all round them.

Close now, where from the ketch it had been out on the hills a league off. Out of the grass on either side of the road. Out of the ground under her boots. Pale. White, gold at the edges. Rising slowly. Warm. Not hot. Like standing near a hearth in a room where the fire's been banked.

She didn't sleep.

She walked beside Orrun all night with her right hand in his mane. In the light. Her left side dead and cold under her coat. The light rising round them and spreading and sinking and rising.

She counted it.

Sixty, while it rose. A long hold. Sixty while it sank. As the old man's glasses had breathed at Liss's table, a lifetime ago. As the whole world had breathed once, before the six began to drink it.

*One. Two. Three.*

---

It was hard not to sleep.

The light was warm. The road was smooth. The hum came up through her boots into her legs, steady, patient. Twice in the second night she found her eyes closed and her feet still walking, and jerked awake, and bit the inside of her cheek on the side she could feel until it bled.

*You're tired,* said Orrun. The only thing he said all night.

"I'm awake."

*You're tired,* said Orrun. *Hold on to me.*

She held on.

---

They came to the hill at noon on the hundred-and-twenty-fifth.

The road went up it. Up the green slope, straight, to the top, where the grass was short and the stone of the road came out onto a flat crown of grey rock, like a table.

And on the table, lying, the mountain.

---

She stopped.

Orrun didn't.

---

Grey. Old stone grey, the grey of the Ruin's arch, of the milestones, of Orrun himself. Lying on the hill's top with her great forepaws stretched out in front of her and her head down on them. The size of a hill. Of the hill she lay on, almost. A great cat's shape. And round her head and shoulders, something like a mane, that wasn't hair. Grey. Moving a little. Like dust, or mist, or smoke from a fire that's nearly out.

Her eyes were shut.

The light came up out of the hill round her. Even at noon. Faint, in the sunlight. Rising round her, and sinking, slow, with the hill's breath.

Or hers.

---

Orrun walked to her.

Up the last of the road, onto the table of rock, toward her. Slowly. Steadily. Maren stood at the road's edge where it came out onto the rock and watched him go, and didn't follow.

He stopped ten paces from her.

As he'd stopped at the Hollow Ring, among the stones, ten paces from the thing that was his kin. He'd lain down there. With his head toward it. It hadn't turned its head.

He lay down here.

On the grey rock. Ten paces from her great grey paws. His own forepaws out in front of him. His head down on them. Facing her.

He was very small.

---

Her eyes opened.

Slowly. As if she'd been asleep a long time, and something had woken her, and she wasn't sure yet what.

Gold.

The size of cartwheels. Gold, with something in them deep and old and slow, as Aurveth's were, only older. Much older. They opened, and looked down along her own great paws, at the grey lion lying ten paces from them with his head on the rock.

And stayed.

---

It went through Orrun.

She felt it. Through him, into her, up from the rock, through the bond, through the place where his voice lived. A looking. Being looked at.

As a mother looks at a cub.

She knew him. Maren felt it. Not his name. Not what he'd done. Him. The shape of him, the smell, the weight. As a cat knows her own kitten in a heap of kittens in the dark by the smell of its fur. As if she'd last seen him three hundred years ago and he'd been small then, and she'd known he'd come back, and here he was.

---

Orrun made a sound.

Maren had never heard it. In all the time since the island. A small sound. From his chest. High. Thin. Not a growl, not a word.

The sound a kitten makes.

He lay on the grey rock with his head on his paws and made it, very quietly, over and over, ten paces from the great grey shape with the gold eyes, and the mountain looked at him.

Maren stood at the road's edge and didn't move.

She was crying. She hadn't known. She couldn't feel it on the left side of her face. She could feel it on the right.

---

They stayed on the hill that night.

The mountain didn't move. Her eyes stayed open, on Orrun. Orrun lay where he was, ten paces off, and didn't move either. The light came up out of the hill round both of them, white and gold, rising and sinking.

Maren sat on the road's edge with her back against a milestone and her right hand flat on the stone of the road, and stayed awake.

She was so tired she could hear the light.

---

She saw the ships after midnight.

She'd stood up to keep awake. Walked to the table's edge, the north-east side, where the hill fell away and she could see over the lower hills, and the dunes, and the lagoon, a long way off, and the dark sea beyond it.

At the edge of the dark, low, at the very rim of the world, lights.

Tiny. Strung out. In a line. Too many for stars, and too low, and too even. Moving. So slowly she had to watch them a long time to be sure.

Ships.

Sailing at night. On the drowned road. Where no Merrin would sail at night, because Abram hadn't and Hal hadn't and Kit wouldn't.

She stood on the hill-top at the end of the world and counted them. Ten. Strung out, low, and no more behind them.

The Council's fleet.

---

She looked at them.

Four hundred people and more, at five hundred gold a head. Coming to a land without Titans. To a shore where if you lay down and slept you didn't get up. Sailing at night through the Shatters with pilots reading paper.

She couldn't do anything. They were days off yet.

She went back to the milestone and sat down against it and put her hand on the road, and stayed awake, and watched the great grey shape on the hill-top look at her lion in the light, until it was dawn.
