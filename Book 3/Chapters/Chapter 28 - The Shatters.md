---

Jory came off the flagship at the first pier.

On the hundred-and-nineteenth, at dusk, as he'd said. The fleet had come up on *forty-one* in the last light, eleven ships in a ragged line with Rennick's three black ones behind. Ostrey's pilots hadn't hove to. They'd sailed straight past it.

Calder brought Aurveth down on the sea beside the flagship.

She came down on the water like a hill falling into it. The flagship rolled in her wash. Men on the deck shouted. And a thin brown boy at the rail between two of Rennick's men climbed up onto the rail and stood there a moment, looking at the great dull bronze shoulder coming up out of the sea beside him, and jumped.

Nobody stopped him.

---

He landed on her shoulder and slid and caught a plate and held.

Rennick's two men looked down at him. Then at each other. Then at the black ship behind, where a big man with a gold ring in his ear was standing at the stern rail in the dusk.

Rennick laughed. Calder heard it across the water.

The two men went back to their dice.

---

Jory rode her from then on.

Lying along her neck behind her head, where Calder had lain on Kharrak at Corsa. His long brown hands flat on her plates. Talking to her, sometimes, under his breath, as if she were a boat. And calling the stones.

From the air. Ahead of the fleet. Every pier. Its broken top coming up out of the sea, and Jory knowing it by its shape before Calder could see it was a pier at all.

"*Forty,*" he'd call. "*Bear west a point at it. There's a rock under the swell a cable south.*"

Calder would fly back to the flagship and shout it down.

Nobody on the flagship listened.

---

Rennick turned back on the hundred-and-twenty-first.

At the fifth pier. At dusk. The Council's pilots sailed on past it into the dark, as they'd sailed past every pier, day and night, because they had paper and the paper had the turns on it and the Council had paid for speed. Rennick's three black ships came up to the pier and hove to.

In the morning they were going north-east.

Back.

---

Rennick signalled once.

A flag at his masthead. Calder didn't know the signal. Jory did.

"*Turn back*," he said. From Aurveth's neck. "That's what it means. On the coast. *Turn back. Danger.*"

The fleet didn't turn back.

Calder flew low over Rennick's ship as it went. The big man was at the stern. He looked up.

"I don't sail at night where I can't see the bottom, farmer!" he shouted. "Not for the Council! Not for anybody!" He raised his hand. "Good luck to you!"

He meant it.

---

The *White Heron* went down on the hundred-and-twenty-third.

A Quill ship. White pennants. At night. Two piers had fallen since Hal Merrin drew his charts; Jory had said so at the fire on the headland. Quenby and Loy didn't know. They sailed the turn off the paper where the paper said the next pier was, and the next pier was a heap of stone under the swell, and the *White Heron* went onto it.

He heard it from the air. The crack. The screaming.

They got eleven off her rigging. The dragons. In the dark, by Kharrak's fire, hovering over the wreck as it went down, Lisska plucking people off the yards in her claws. Eleven.

Sixty went down with her.

---

Celia Ostrey wouldn't stop.

He went down to the flagship's deck in the morning. Kharrak on the water beside her, the whole ship rolling. He stood on her deck in front of the Speaker in her grey silk, salty now, and told her.

"Sixty people," he said. "In the night. Your pilots are reading paper. The boy's reading the stones. He told you about the fallen piers. Listen to him. Stop at night."

Celia Ostrey looked at him. Calm.

"The Merrins sail this," she said. "So can we. We have paid."

She turned away.

---

He'd stopped arguing after that.

He flew. Over them. Day and night. Jory calling every stone from Aurveth's neck, and Calder flying back and shouting it down to whichever ship was leading, and sometimes they heard and sometimes they didn't and mostly they didn't.

Eleven ships became ten.

They went on, south-west, fast.

---

The Bell rose on the hundred-and-twenty-seventh.

At night. Near the twelfth pier. He'd been flying over the leading ships, low, with Kharrak's fire lit in his throat for light. Jory ahead on Aurveth, in the dark.

He felt it first through Aurveth.

*Under,* she said. Deep. Slow. *Under us. Coming up.*

---

The sea rose under the fleet.

Between the piers. Under the leading ships. He saw it in Kharrak's light: the black water going smooth and round and lifting, a hill of water, coming up out of the deep under the four leading ships all at once. Then breaking open.

And the Bell came up through it.

---

Its back first. The broken dome. The columns. Streaming. Under the ships. The four leading ships were on its back. On it. He saw them, in the firelight, lifted up out of the sea on the Bell's back, tilting, men and women and children on their decks sliding, the masts going over.

The bell rang.

*Down.*

And the sea stood up all round it.

---

The four ships went over.

Off its back. Into the sea. On their sides. He saw one go over and over, hull up, masts under, and not come up.

The rest were flung out.

The water off the Bell's back went out in every direction, a ring of it, white, a wave, running out across the sea. It took the other six ships and threw them. Outward. Away from the road. Toward the reefs on either side of it, where the Shatters' rocks came up through the swell in the dark.

He heard them hit.

---

"*Calder!*"

Jory. From the dark ahead. From Aurveth's neck.

"*Here!*" Jory was shouting. "*Twelve to eleven! The road's whole! There's a stretch!*"

He turned Kharrak toward the voice.

---

Jory had told him at the fire on the headland. He'd forgotten. He remembered now.

Between the twelfth pier and the eleventh, the drowned road lay whole. Half a league of it. The causeway, a fathom and a half down, flat, unbroken, still lying where it had lain since the world split, with no rocks on it and no reef. The only stretch on the whole road where it did.

*If you go down on that,* Jory had said, *you go down on stone that's flat. Not on teeth. A boat'll ground on it and sit.*

*A fathom and a half,* Calder had said. *That's not shallow.*

*It's the shallowest thing on the road that won't break you,* Jory had said.

---

Two boats on the water below.

He saw them in Kharrak's light. The flagship's longboat and a little jolly-boat, launched by her crew as she went over. Full. People in them to the gunwales. Rowing, badly, in every direction, in the dark, on the heaving sea.

Away from the stretch.

*Lisska,* he said. *Kharrak. Get them on it.*

---

They herded them.

He'd never thought of it as that until afterwards. The two dragons came down low over the two boats, one on each side, Kharrak's fire lighting the water, and the people in the boats screamed and pulled their oars away from the fire, away from the dragons, toward the only dark there was.

Toward the stretch.

Jory, on Aurveth over it, shouting: "*Here! Here! Here!*"

The boats came onto the drowned road.

He saw it. The water changing over the stone. Pale, in the firelight. The causeway under them, grey, flat, a fathom and a half down. The longboat's keel touching it and grinding and sitting. The jolly-boat beside her.

On the road.

---

And the second wave came.

He saw it. Across the stretch. White along its top in Kharrak's light, rising, running at the two boats on the drowned road from the Bell's side. Taller than the first. As tall as the counting-houses at Corsa.

The boats on the road couldn't run. They were aground.

---

He didn't think about it.

Lisska was alongside. Flying, wing to wing with Kharrak, low over the stretch. He reached out. He put his hand on her neck.

Not Kharrak's. Hers.

*Lisska,* he said.

*I know,* she said.

---

He'd never asked her for it.

She had it. He'd felt it in her since the beginning. Not fire. She had a little fire, and used it, and it was nothing to Kharrak's. Under the fire, in her, something else. Something that moved. That she rode on, every day, without thinking. That she'd ridden through the gale at Corsa with a chain in her claws.

Wind.

He'd never asked. He asked now.

*All of it,* he said. As he'd said to Kharrak in the harbour. *Through me. Take mine.*

---

It came up out of her into him.

Through his hand. Up his arm. Into his chest. Not heat. Not fire. A pressure. A great breath, coming in, as if the sky itself were breathing into him through her. Filling him. More than he could hold.

He let it out.

Not through his mouth. Through her. Back out of him, through his hand into her, and out of her, all of it at once, ahead of them, across the water.

Between the wave and the boats.

---

A wall of wind.

He saw it hit the sea. The water flattened under it. White. A line of white running across the stretch where the wind came down, the sea's skin torn off in spray. And the wave came to it.

And its top went.

Torn off. Flattened. Thrown back on itself in a great sheet of spray, white, roaring, as the wind came down on it and held it. The wave was still coming. It was coming lower. Flatter. Its top torn off and torn off and torn off.

He held it.

He held it as long as he had breath.

---

He didn't have much.

He felt it going. The breath going out of him through her, out of him and out of him, and not coming back. He held it. The wind held. The wave came on, flatter, lower, spray, foam, a long low hump of water with all its height torn off it.

It came to the boats on the drowned road.

And went under them.

Lifting them. Setting them down. Grinding. On the stone. Still there.

---

He let go.

He didn't let go. It let go of him. The wind went out of him all at once, the last of it, and the wall over the sea wasn't there, and he was lying on Kharrak's neck, and he couldn't breathe.

---

Not like Corsa.

At Corsa it had been like breathing through wool. This was nothing. He opened his mouth and nothing came in. His chest moved and nothing came in. He lay on Kharrak's neck in the dark with his mouth open like a landed fish and Kharrak flying very slowly, very carefully, and he couldn't breathe.

*Breathe,* said Kharrak.

He couldn't.

*Breathe,* said Lisska. Beside him. Very close. Frightened. He'd never heard her frightened.

He couldn't.

---

Kharrak came down on the twelfth pier.

On its broken top. Among the birds, which went up screaming in the dark. He landed on a column of old grey stone in the middle of the sea with the farmer on his neck, and lay down on the stone, as flat as he could, so that Calder lay on his neck on the pier as if on a bed.

*Breathe,* said Kharrak.

And something went in.

---

A thread of it. Not much. A thread of air, into him, past whatever had shut. It hurt. He breathed it. Another thread. It hurt more.

He lay there until dawn.

Breathing like a man drowning slowly, a thread at a time, on a stone in the sea with a dragon under him and a dragon over him and the third far off over the stretch, on the water, holding a boy on her neck and watching.

He couldn't speak.

---

Most of the fleet went down.

He learned it in the morning. From Jory, who'd counted, and came to the pier on Aurveth and told him while he lay there unable to answer.

Nine of the ten ships. Four off the Bell's back. Five on the reefs either side of the road, flung there by the first wave. The tenth drifted away north, waterlogged, empty; they found her in the afternoon, and nobody aboard.

The Bell had gone down again. Back into the deep, under the road. Gone.

---

Sixty-three people in the two boats.

On the drowned road between twelve and eleven, aground on the stone in a fathom and a half of water, in the morning sun. From the flagship, and from two of the others whose boats had been swamped. House servants. Children, most of them. A few of the rich, in their good coats, salt-white. And Master Loy, the pilot, who sat in the longboat's stern with his chart in his hands and his face grey.

And the dragons had found more.

In the night and the dawn, off wreckage, off rigging, off the reefs. Lisska and Kharrak. Thirty-one more. Brought to the boats and to the pier's top. Ninety-four.

---

Celia Ostrey had gone down with the flagship.

At her rail. Loy told Jory. She'd stood at the rail in her grey silk when the Bell came up under them, and watched it, calmly, and not moved toward the boats. As if she'd paid for a passage and expected the passage to be honoured.

Aubrey Strand had gone down with his.

---

The ninety-four of them looked at him.

From the boats. From the pier's top, where some of them sat in a row along its broken edge with the birds. Looked at the man lying on the red dragon's neck on the stone, breathing a thread at a time, who couldn't speak.

The farmer. Who'd come to the Council in the roofless boathouse and told them it was a grave, and been told it was a reef-pilot's tale.

He couldn't say anything to them. He lay there.

---

They towed them ten days.

He spoke again on the hundred-and-twenty-ninth. Not much. A word. Then two. His voice came back cracked and thin, and stayed that way for weeks, and he never got his breath back all the way; he'd always be short of it after, on a hill.

The two boats, rowed by turns. Kharrak and Lisska towing on war-harness lines, low over the water, slowly. Aurveth ahead with Jory on her neck, reading the stones. Pier to pier. Nights tied to the piers, the people in the boats sleeping in the boats, the dragons on the piers' broken tops.

Water from the longboat's breaker and from rain. It rained twice. They caught it in sails.

Nobody died on the tow.

---

At the eleventh pier, Jory found the mark.

On the hundred-and-twenty-eighth. In the morning. The first pier after the stretch. He took Aurveth down to its top to read it and came back to Calder with his face strange.

"Kit," he said.

Calder looked at him. He couldn't ask. He looked.

"The milestone," said Jory. "*Into the light, eleven.* And under it, scratched in. With a knife." He drew it in the air with his long finger. "A ship. Small. With a K for its mast." He swallowed. "That's Kit's. That's her mark. She's been past."

He looked south-west down the road.

"Days ago," he said.

---

He could speak on the hundred-and-twenty-ninth, a little.

He used it for one thing. He made Jory get the boats close under the pier where he lay, all ninety-four of them in the boats and on the stone, and he said it, cracked and thin, as loud as he could.

"Nobody sleeps ashore," he said. "Over there. When we get there. Nobody. Ever."

They looked at him.

"You'll want to," he said. "You'll have been in a boat ten days. There'll be sand and grass and sun. You'll lie down." He breathed, a thread. "Don't. If you sleep ashore over there, you don't wake up. I had it from a Merrin. Her great-grandfather's brother. They left him on the sand with his eyes open."

Nobody said anything.

"That's the price," said Calder. "Of the berth you paid for."

He lay back down on Kharrak's neck. It was all the breath he had.
