---

The custom held.

Every day. Ashore by day, in pairs, two by two up the road to the spring under the steps with the casks. Back aboard by dusk. The boat at the road's end with one of them always in it. Nobody ashore after the sun touched the sea. Nobody lying down on the sand, not even for a moment in the noon heat, not even to rest their eyes.

Kit watched them keep it.

She watched herself keep it. She'd kept it eight times. She'd never kept it this long.

---

Her father's longest had been six days.

Six days at anchor in the lagoon, and then the casks full and the hold full of stone and the anchor up and the Needle at the top of the flood, and home. Six. She'd done four once, alone with Jory. Five.

This was the tenth day since landfall. The thirteenth since the Needle.

The knight was in the hills with her lion. Kit had watched her go inland on the road with her coat fastened to the throat and the grey on her neck, two days, two back, four nights awake. That had been five days ago.

She hadn't come back.

---

The crew were restless.

They didn't say. They did their work. Ferris at the anchor, Lorn on the gear, Tansy in the galley. Nye and Sim and Corrie on the casks, two by two. Gannet everywhere. But they stood at the rail at dusk longer than they needed to. They watched the light come up out of the hills and didn't talk. Tansy had stopped singing.

The fish came to Tansy.

That was the strangest thing. She went to the lagoon's edge on the second day with a line, and stood in the shallows, and the fish came to her. Swam up to her shins. Round her ankles. She put her hands down into the water and lifted one out, a fat silver thing, and it lay in her hands and looked at her.

She'd let it go. She'd stood in the water a long time. Then she'd caught another, and brought it back, and cooked it, and they'd eaten it, and nobody said anything.

---

Sim went on the hundred-and-twenty-eighth.

At dusk. He and Nye had the boat-watch at the road's end. Sim was seventeen. The elder of the two. Quick aloft, quicker with his mouth. He'd watched the light come up out of the hills every night for thirteen nights from the ketch's rail, a league off, and he'd said every night that he wanted to see it close.

Just once. Just to see.

He'd said it at dusk on the hundred-and-twenty-eighth, standing in the shallows by the boat with Nye in it.

"Just up the road," he'd said. "A little way. To see it close. I'll be back before it's properly dark."

---

Nye told it.

In the morning. On the beach. To Kit, with his face white and his hands shaking.

"I said don't," said Nye. "I said Kit'll have your hide. He laughed. He said he'd be back before you knew." He looked at the road. "He went up it. Running. I could see him in the light. I shouted. Once." He swallowed. "I didn't leave the boat."

"No," said Kit.

"The boat never leaves," said Nye. "You said."

"I said," said Kit.

---

They found him a quarter-league up the road.

At dawn on the hundred-and-twenty-ninth. She went up herself, with Ferris and Nye. Walking fast on the grey paving between the hills of short grass in the early light, with the hum coming up through their boots.

He was lying at the road's edge.

On his back. On the grass beside the paving, with one hand on the stone, as if he'd sat down at the road's edge to watch the light and lain back to see it better. His eyes were open. Looking at the sky. He was smiling a little.

He wasn't breathing.

---

Kit knelt by him.

She put her hand on his chest. Still. On his face. Warm. As warm as if he'd just lain down. No mark on him. Nothing. A boy lying in the grass in the morning with his eyes open, smiling, and not breathing.

*They left him on the sand with his eyes open.*

Abram's brother. Eighty years ago. She'd heard it once, from her father, at the tiller, the only time he'd ever said it. She'd told the farmer, by Brask. She'd told the knight, at last, at the road's end.

She hadn't told Sim.

She'd told him the rule. She hadn't told him why.

---

The old man came up the road behind them.

Kit had let him come ashore. He'd asked; she'd been too tired to argue. He came up the road slowly, leaning on Tansy's arm, with the sea-birds on his shoulders, and stood over the boy in the grass and looked down at him a long time.

"It took his breath," he said.

Kit looked up at him.

"The light," said Idony. "It drinks. Like the six drink, in the east. Only gently." He looked at the hills. "The breath of the world comes up out of the ground here and nobody's ever drunk it. It's full. And when it finds something lying still in it, breathing..." He stopped. "It drinks that too. Not cruelly. It doesn't know it's doing it."

---

"He wasn't asleep long," said Kit.

She didn't know why she said it. It came out of her.

"He didn't need to be," said Idony.

He knelt, very slowly, on the paving beside the boy, and with his shaking hand closed the boy's eyes. It took him a long time. His hand wouldn't keep still. He did it anyway.

---

She didn't take him home.

She'd said she wouldn't. In the stern cabin at Blackwater, with the lamp swinging, to the knight. *Then they stay there. I don't take them home.* She'd said it as a price. She'd not thought it would be one of hers.

They buried him on the beach.

Above the tide-line. Beside the milestone with no number on it. Nye dug it. He wouldn't let anyone else. He dug it with the ketch's spade in the white sand all morning, alone, and nobody helped, and when it was deep enough he got out and stood back.

Ferris said the words. He was the oldest. He'd said them for her father, at sea, without a body.

Kit poured the salt.

From the chipped dish. All of it. Into the grave, on the boy's chest, white on the dark wool of his jersey. Then Nye filled it in.

---

Gannet chipped stone every day.

Up among the column-tops, alone, with his canvas bag and a mallet and a chisel he'd brought from the hold. She could hear him from the beach. *Tap. Tap. Tap.* Splitting flakes off the old stone where it stuck up out of the sand. The bag heavier every day. His pockets heavier.

Merrins took stone home. That was what the road was for.

She didn't stop him.

---

He wrote at night, too.

She saw him at it on the hundred-and-thirtieth. Late. She'd come up from the stern cabin because she couldn't sleep, and she went forward along the deck in the dark, and the forehatch grating had light coming up through it.

She looked down.

Gannet. In the hold. Sitting on a block of the far-stone ballast with the lantern on a hook above him. A board on his knees. A sheet of Merrin paper on the board, and a stub of pencil in his thick fingers, writing. Carefully. Slowly. With another sheet beside him on the stone, held flat with a flake.

She could see the other sheet's hand from the grating. Square. Upright. Every letter set down separately beside the last.

The knight's.

---

"Gannet."

He looked up. The smile came first. It always did.

"Mistress."

"What's that?"

"For the news," said Gannet. He held up the board. "I keep a log. Of the voyage. What we see. What we find." He tapped the sheet beside him. "The knight's pages are the best record. The old man's. I'm copying the bits about the stone. So I'll know what I've got when I sell it."

He smiled up at her through the grating.

"Can't sell a thing if you don't know what it is," he said.

---

She looked at him a while.

It made sense. It was the kind of sense Gannet always made. A bosun who'd been a poacher, keeping a log of what the stone was worth, so as to know what to ask on Gallows Quay. She'd have done it herself, if she could have read the old man's pages.

"Don't let him see you," she said. "The old man. He's touchy about his notes."

"He'd not mind," said Gannet. "He'd be flattered."

She went back to the stern cabin.

She didn't look closer.

---

She wanted to leave on the hundred-and-thirty-first.

She'd wanted to since Sim. She sat in the stern cabin with the book shut on the table and the chipped dish empty beside it and wanted to leave so badly she could taste it, like salt.

Sixteen days at anchor. The longest a Merrin had ever lain in the lagoon by ten. A boy in the sand by the milestone. Corrie wouldn't go ashore now. Not at all. She sat on the forehatch all day with her arms round her knees and looked at the beach. Nye did the casks alone, and wouldn't take anyone with him, and came back at dusk with his face shut.

And the knight in the hills, not coming back.

---

"We could go," she said.

To Ferris. At the tiller, at dusk, with the light coming up out of the hills.

"Up anchor. The Needle at the top of tomorrow's flood. Home." She looked at the shore. "She's got her lion. Her lion can walk home under the sea for all I know. Nothing about that lion would surprise me any more."

Ferris didn't say anything for a while.

"You swore," he said.

"I know what I swore."

"Passage there and back," said Ferris. "Salt on the tongue. Your father would've stayed."

"My father would've gone home after six days."

"Your father," said Ferris, "never swore salt to a knight of Greyhold with a lion on his deck."

---

She stayed.

She went below and sat with the empty dish. She'd poured all the salt into the boy's grave. She went to the galley and got more from Tansy's sack and filled the dish again, and put it back on the table.

She stayed.

---

The old man went inland on the hundred-and-thirty-third.

He'd argued every day. Since the knight went. Every day, on the beach, on the deck, at the table. He wanted to go up to the hills. He wanted to see the thing on the hill. He wanted to read whatever there was to read.

Every day she'd said no.

---

"I can't sleep anyway," he said, on the hundred-and-thirty-third. On the beach, at the road's end. "I haven't slept more than an hour a night since the cellars. Ask Maren. Ask Liss." He spread his shaking hands. "And the light can't take what I haven't got."

"It took Sim."

"Sim was seventeen," said Idony. "Sim was full. Of everything. He was the fullest thing on this beach." He smiled. "I'm burned out, Mistress Merrin. Grey in a breath, under Greyhold, holding a Titan still with my hands. There's nothing left in me to drink." He looked delighted. "I'm the one man on this whole shore who's safe."

He laughed.

He meant it as a joke. She could see he did. He was enjoying it enormously.

---

A horse came down the beach.

One of the small grey ones off the dunes. Alone. She saw it come, between the column-tops, down the sand, unhurried. It came to the road's end and stood beside the old man in the shallows and put its nose against his shoulder, among the sea-birds, which didn't move.

He looked at it.

It stood there.

He put his shaking hand on its neck. It stood. He took hold of its mane, and Tansy, without being asked, came and made a step of her hands, and he put his foot in it, and she lifted him, and he went up onto the little grey horse's back.

It stood there with him on it. A wild horse. That had never seen a person in its life.

---

"You're not party to the contract," said Kit.

She said it because it was the only thing left to say.

The old man looked down at her from the horse's back. He was beaming.

"No," he said. "I'm a witness."

---

She let him go.

She stood on the beach at the road's end with Tansy and watched him ride inland on the grey paving, slowly, on the little wild horse, with his blanket round his shoulders and the sea-birds lifting off them one by one as he went, until there was only the old man and the horse, small, going up the road into the green hills.

"Witnesses can taste," said Tansy. Beside her. "That's what he said, at Blackwater. With the salt."

"I know what he said," said Kit.

---

Gannet asked on the hundred-and-thirty-fourth.

At dawn, on deck. Whether he might go after the old man. To see he didn't fall off the horse, he said. To see he ate. And there was stone up the road nobody had chipped, and it would be a shame to come all this way and leave it.

"You'll not sleep," said Kit.

"I never sleep," said Gannet. The smile came first. "Ask anyone."

She let him go. He was crew. He'd kept the custom fifteen days without a slip. He went up the road at a long easy walk with his canvas bag on his shoulder and his pencil, she supposed, in his pocket.

---

She went up to the platform on the hundred-and-thirty-fifth.

At the top of the great steps. On the dune's crest, with the low wall round it and the hills going away inland. She took her glass. She hadn't been up since the knight went.

She looked.

---

The far hill. A day off. With the road going up it, straight, a grey line on the green.

On its top, a grey shape. Long. Low. Lying in the light, even by day, a faint paleness rising round it. A hill on a hill.

And in front of it, smaller, much smaller, another grey shape. Lying. A lion.

And beside the lion, very small, so small she'd not have seen it without the glass, a woman. Sitting. On the grey rock. With her back against something. Not moving.

---

And at the hill's foot, on the road, going up, smaller than any of them, a grey horse with an old man on it.

She watched until the horse reached the top.

Then she put the glass down, and went back down the hundreds of steps to the beach, and past the milestone with no number and the grave beside it with the salt in it, and out in the boat to the ketch, and sat in the stern cabin with the book on the table and the dish beside it, full, and waited.

As she'd sworn to.
