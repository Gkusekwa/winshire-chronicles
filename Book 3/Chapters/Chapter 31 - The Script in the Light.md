---

Gannet came up the road at dusk on the hundred-and-thirty-fifth.

An hour behind him. On foot. With his canvas bag on his shoulder, half full already of flakes chipped off milestones on the way, and the smile coming up the road a long way ahead of him.

"Kit sent me," he said. "To see you didn't fall off."

"I didn't."

"I see that," said Gannet. He looked up the hill. At the great grey shape on its top in the rising light, and the lion against its paw, and the woman sitting by a milestone. He looked a long time. The smile stayed exactly where it was, which Idony had learned meant it was working very hard.

"Well," said Gannet. "That's not a rock."

---

He stayed awake.

All that night, by the little fire they made at the road's edge at the hill's foot, of dry grass and the dead branches of a thorn. He didn't sleep. He said he never slept, and apparently it was true. He sat on the far side of the fire with his bag between his knees and chipped flakes off a lump of grey stone with his knife, by the firelight, and whistled under his breath, and watched the light rise round the hill.

Idony didn't sleep either. He hadn't slept more than an hour a night since the cellars.

He watched the light.

---

It changed at dawn.

He saw it change. He'd been watching the light rise and sink round the great shape on the hill-top all night, slow, as it rose and sank everywhere on this shore, sixty in and a hold and sixty out. Maren had told him the count. He'd checked it. She was right.

At dawn it didn't sink.

It rose. Round the Grey Mother. And instead of spreading along the hill's crown and sinking back into the grass, it gathered. In front of her. On the air. A man's height off the grey rock. Drawing in on itself. Thickening.

Into lines.

---

He was on his feet before he knew it.

He'd left the horse at the hill's foot. He went up the road on his own feet, the last of the slope, as fast as his legs would let him, which was not fast. Maren met him at the top. She took his elbow. He didn't argue.

They stood at the road's edge on the hill's crown, ten paces behind the lion, and looked.

---

Letters.

In the air. Drawn in light. Hanging there in front of the Grey Mother's great lowered face, pale gold, steady, each one the size of his hand. Square. Spare. With odd little hooks at the ends of the strokes.

The hooked script.

The milestones' script. The arch's. The chains', on the insides of the links. The script he'd spent his life reading off stone, worn, half gone, in libraries and ruins and at the bottoms of drowned stairs, a word at a time, guessing at what the sea had eaten.

Here it was new.

Written while he watched. In light.

---

It held a breath's length. A long one. Sixty in.

Then it faded.

And the next line came.

---

"Can you read it?" said Maren.

Very quietly. Beside him. Her hand on his elbow.

He could feel her shaking. Or himself. He couldn't tell.

"Can you?" he said.

"No. I feel it. Through him." She looked at Orrun, lying ten paces ahead with his head against his mother's paw. "I know what it means. I don't know what it says."

"Then I'll read," said Idony. "And you write."

---

She had the paper. She'd brought it from the ketch: Kit's paper, at Kit's price. She knelt on the grey rock with it on her knee, one-armed, the dead arm inside her coat, and her pencil in her right hand.

He read.

---

*We were as they are.*

He said it aloud. Slowly. The line hung in the air in light, and he read it, and Maren wrote it in her square upright hand, every letter set down separately.

It faded. The next came.

*We woke, and drank.*

He read it. She wrote it.

*We stopped.*

---

He stood there a while after that one.

The light held it, and faded it, and he didn't read the next one at once. Maren looked up from the paper.

"Idony?"

"A moment," he said.

He was a tide-reader. He'd read the breath of the world in glass tubes his whole life. He'd watched the six wake and drink it, in the east, in the glasses, and the world go flat behind them. He knew what drinking was.

*We were as they are. We woke, and drank. We stopped.*

He read the next line.

---

*We put down what we were, and walked without it.*

*The small ones saw us walk, and gave us names.*

*They made us houses. They lit lamps to us.*

*They said we walked unbound.*

---

Maren's pencil had stopped.

He didn't look down. He knew it had. He kept reading. He was a reader. That was what he was for.

*We went into the light.*

*They kept the names.*

---

The light held that one a long time.

Then faded. And the air in front of the Grey Mother was empty, the light round her rising and sinking again as it did everywhere, sixty in, a hold, sixty out. As if she'd said what she had to say and was resting.

He stood there.

He didn't know how long.

---

"Ask her," he said at last.

Maren looked up.

"Ask her what she is." He was surprised how steady his voice was. "I can't. She doesn't hear me. She hears you. Through him. Ask her what she is."

Maren looked at him. Then at the lion. She didn't say anything aloud. He saw her face go still, as it went when she was talking to Orrun.

The lion's ear moved.

The light gathered.

---

One line.

Short. Three of the hooked letters, and a fourth.

He read it.

*What is left.*

---

He sat down on the grey rock.

Sat, as he'd sat on the *Kittiwake*'s deck at the last milestone. His legs went and he let them.

*What is left.*

---

He knew. He'd known since the second line. He'd known, if he was honest, since the chisel-bench at Blackwater, since *into the light* with no number. He'd known and put it away, as he'd put away his hands shaking less on the far-stone, to look at later.

He took it out now.

---

The gods in every chapel.

The hearthstone in Hearthmere's chapels that the old women knelt to and called the Grey Mother. The Lamp on the Sun Stair at Solenne, that Sarovel's priests sang to at dawn. The Lady under the water, that his grandmother had prayed to on Glasswater every night of her life before she drew the ferry's rope. The three-faced thing at every crossroads in the Marches, that farmers left a copper for on market day.

The old ones of the songs. Who walked unbound. Who went into the light.

Titans.

Titans who'd woken, as the six had woken, and drunk, as the six were drinking. And stopped. Who'd put down their bodies and the drinking with them, and gone on without either. As light. And people had seen them walk, and called them gods, and built them houses, and lit lamps to them.

And kept the names, when the light went.

---

And the seventh ring.

He laughed. He couldn't help it. It came out of him, sitting on the grey rock, short and cracked.

The College's rings. Seven. A plain iron ring on a worker's hand, notched for what he could do. First ring, a hedge-charm. Second, a Reader like himself, once. Sixth, the Keepers of the Script, two or three living, all in Isvaren, who could read the oldest stone.

And the seventh. That no person had ever worn. That the College wrote in its books as *the Unbound*, and defined as *old magic: working the Deep with nothing between*, and argued about for six hundred years: what it was, whether anyone could reach it, how.

Nobody could reach it.

It had never been a person's.

It was theirs. The things that had been Titans and stopped. That *were* the Deep, with nothing between. The Unbound. They'd been the seventh ring all along, and the College had been cutting a notch in a ring for a god.

---

"Idony."

Maren. Kneeling beside him. Her pencil still in her hand.

"Are you all right?"

"I don't believe in gods," said Idony.

She looked at him.

"I never have," he said. "I'm a tide-reader. Measure. Not prayer. My grandmother prayed to the Lady every night on the ferry and I sat in the stern and counted the stars." He looked up at the air in front of the Grey Mother. Empty now. "I don't believe in gods."

"I know."

"Write it down anyway," said Idony.

---

She waited, pencil ready.

He said it slowly. So that every word would be set down exactly, in her square hand, as the light had given it him, and not as he'd have liked it to be.

"*The gods,*" he said, "*are what is left of Titans who stopped.*"

She wrote it.

He watched her write it. Every letter, separately. *The gods are what is left of Titans who stopped.* On Kit's paper, on her knee, on a hill-top at the end of the world, in a soldier's hand.

"Write this too," he said. "*The seventh ring. The Unbound was always theirs.*"

She wrote that too.

Then he laughed again. At himself. A long time. Until his eyes ran.

Then he stopped, and sat there on the grey rock, and didn't laugh for a long time after that.

---

The light came again at noon.

He was still sitting there. He hadn't moved. Maren had brought him water from the skin and he'd drunk it without noticing. And the light gathered again in front of the Grey Mother, and he got up onto his knees to read it.

Three lines.

*Others of us are near.*

*Come to the hall.*

*Bring the other bond.*

---

He read the last one aloud and heard Maren go still beside him.

He looked at her.

Her face. The side of it that moved. He'd known her since the Hollow Ring and he'd seen that face hold up a hill. He'd never seen it look like that.

"The other bond," she said.

"Yes."

She didn't say his name. Idony didn't either.

---

"Where's the hall?" he said. Aloud. To Maren, to pass on. "Ask her where."

The light drew a line.

Not a letter. A line. Pale gold, on the air, going out from in front of the Grey Mother's face, low, south and west. Along the hill. Down. Toward where the paved road they'd come up by ran on over the green country and crossed a long ridge, far off, a day's walk.

And under the line, one word.

*Hall.*

"A hall," said Idony. "On the road. Where it crosses that ridge. A day, south-west."

---

His hands hadn't shaken all day.

He noticed it at dusk. Looking down at them in his lap by the fire at the road's edge. He'd read the light without his glass. He'd not needed it. He'd held his hands up in front of him all morning without thinking, as if to warm them at the letters, and they'd been steady.

An old man's hands, and steady.

He didn't tell Maren. She didn't tell him about the white in her hair. It seemed fair.

---

Gannet asked to see the pages that night.

At the fire. He'd come up the hill at noon and stood at the road's edge and watched the light write, and not said anything, and gone back down. Now he sat across the fire from them with the smile on.

"To know what the stone's worth," he said. "If I know what it says, I know what to ask."

Maren looked at Idony.

Idony shrugged. "Let him. It's only true."

She gave him the pages.

---

He copied them.

Across the fire. On his own paper, on a board on his knee, with a stub of pencil, carefully, by the firelight. His lips didn't move. He read Maren's square hand as easily as he'd read it upside down from the foot of a ladder at Blackwater.

Idony watched him. It was rather nice. Nobody had ever copied his notes before. Except students, under orders.

---

"What's *ring*?" said Gannet.

He looked up from the board. Pencil in his fingers.

"In the old tongue. Your notes. Here." He tapped the page. "*The seventh ring.* *The Unbound was always theirs.* What's a ring?"

"Oh," said Idony. He was pleased. Somebody was asking. "The College's word. At Vael. A worker wears a ring of plain iron on the hand he works with, with notches cut in it. One notch for each grade. Seven in all. The first is a hedge-charm. The second was mine, once." He held up his steady hand. Bare. "The sixth reads the oldest stone. The seventh..."

"Nobody's worn it," said Gannet. "It says."

"Nobody could. It was theirs." He nodded up the hill, at the light. "Working the Deep with nothing between. They were the Deep, with nothing between."

Gannet wrote it down.

"*Ring,*" he said, writing. "*Iron, notched. Seventh: theirs.*" He looked up and smiled. "Thank you, master."

"Not at all," said Idony.

He was very flattered.
