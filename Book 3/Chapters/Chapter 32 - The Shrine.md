---

They came to the last pier at dusk on the hundred-and-thirty-seventh.

Ten days of it. The two boats, rowed by turns. The dragons towing on the war-harness lines, low over the water. Pier to pier. Jory ahead on Aurveth's neck, reading. Ninety-four people sunburned and salt-cracked and silent in the boats, and the water rationed, and the rain twice. Calder had counted them every dusk for ten days from Kharrak's neck. Ninety-four. He counted them again now.

And at the end of the tenth day, *into the light, one*.

Standing whole at the edge of the shelf, with the white water breaking beyond it.

And west of it, on the shelf's edge, a black island where the chart had none.

---

The Bell.

Lying there. Where it had stopped. A back like a reef with the dome and the columns on it, still, the bell hanging silent in the broken dome. Not moving. Not ringing.

Waiting, or sleeping, or nothing at all.

Jory looked at it from Aurveth's neck a long time.

"Not the Needle," he said. "Not past that. Not with two boats full to the gunwales." He looked south, along the shelf's outer edge, where the white water went away into the dusk. "There's another way in. Abram drew it once. Kit's never used it."

---

It was in the book. Jory knew the book by heart, though he'd never needed it. On a page near the end, a small drawing in Abram's square hand: the shelf, and a gap in it, south, half a day along. And under it, three words.

*South way. Small boats.*

"Small boats," said Jory. "That's us."

---

They went south along the shelf in the night and the morning.

Outside it. In the deep water. The white water breaking on their right hand all the way. Kharrak and Lisska towing, Aurveth ahead. And at noon, Jory found the gap: a cut in the shelf, narrow, shallow, too shallow for the *Kittiwake*, deep enough for a longboat on the top of the flood.

They went in.

Into a bay. Small. Blue. With white sand round it, and dunes, and column-tops sticking up out of the dunes, and a road.

---

Another road.

Paved. Grey. Coming down out of the hills to the sea, as the farmer's lane came down to the Wend at home, and going into the water at the bay's head.

And at the tide-line, a milestone. Waist-high. Jory went to it first, wading.

"*Into the light*," he read. "No number."

He'd never seen it. Only Kit had. And their father. And Abram.

He put his long brown hand on it and stood there.

---

They came ashore on the hundred-and-thirty-eighth.

All of them. Ninety-four. Out of the boats into the warm shallows, up the white sand. Sunburned. Thin. Their good coats rotted with salt. Children, mostly, carried. Old Master Loy with his useless chart under his arm.

The beasts came down to look at them.

Small dun deer out of the dunes. A hare. Birds that landed on people's shoulders. The children screamed at first and then stopped and stood with their mouths open while the deer smelled them.

And then some of them lay down.

---

On the sand. In the sun. At once. As soon as they were up the beach. Ten days in a boat sitting up, and here was warm white sand and the hum coming up out of it like a cat's purr, and they lay down on it and closed their eyes.

"*Up*," said Calder.

His voice came out cracked. It hadn't come back. He didn't care.

"*Up. All of you. Up.*" He went along the beach to them, one by one, and pulled them up by their arms. Children. Women. A man in a coat that had cost more than his farm. "*Nobody lies down. Nobody. I told you on the pier. You're ashore. Don't sleep ashore.*"

They got up.

They looked at him as if he were mad.

---

They spent the night in the boats.

Anchored off in the bay. Everyone in them. Sitting up, packed in, sleeping in shifts against each other's shoulders, a few at a time, while the others kept them sitting. It was hard. The boats were full. The children cried.

Kharrak and Lisska lay in the shallows alongside the boats. Awake. Their heads up.

Calder stayed on the beach with Jory and Aurveth.

He didn't sleep. He didn't know if it mattered for him. Kit had said the rule was for everyone. He kept it. He sat on the sand with his back against Aurveth's dull bronze side and his eyes open and watched the light come up out of the hills.

---

One didn't listen.

A Corsa wine-merchant's son. Piers Calloway. Young. Handsome under the sunburn. He'd paid six hundred gold for his berth and said so, often, in the boats. He'd said it to Calder on the beach that afternoon, when Calder told them about the boats.

"I paid six hundred gold to come to a land without Titans," he'd said. "I'm not sleeping sitting up in a longboat because a reef-pilot told a sellsword a tale."

He'd walked up into the dunes at dusk with a blanket.

Calder had gone after him. He'd sat up in the marram grass with his blanket and laughed and told the farmer to go back to his sheep.

---

They found him at dawn.

In the marram grass. On his back. His blanket round him. His eyes open, looking at the sky. Smiling a little.

Not breathing.

Calder stood over him a long time. The light was gone off the hills. The sun was coming up over the sea. A dun deer came and stood beside the young man's body and smelled his face and went away.

*They left him on the sand with his eyes open.*

---

The ninety-three believed after that.

Nobody lay down. Nobody needed to be told. They carried Piers Calloway down to the beach and buried him by the milestone with no number, and Master Loy said something over him that wasn't the right words because there weren't any, and they went back to the boats.

"That's the price," Calder had said. On the pier. *Of the berth you paid for.*

He hadn't wanted to be right.

---

They walked inland on the hundred-and-thirty-ninth.

On the road. He couldn't leave them in the boats forever. Water from a spring under the dunes. Food: the fish came to people's hands in the shallows, as the deer did, and Jory said that was right, Kit had told him, and they ate them and felt strange about it. But they needed somewhere. And the road went inland, and roads went somewhere.

He took Jory and the three dragons and went ahead.

Half a day. On the grey paving. Between hills of short green grass, humming. The milestones every league, all *into the light*, none counting.

And at noon, a crossing.

---

Another road came in from the west.

Paved. Grey. Running on north-east, toward the hills. And their own road ran on north past the crossing. Jory said that one must go up to the lagoon. To the Needle. Kit's road, by another way.

Where the two roads met, a square.

Paved. Grey stone. A milestone at each corner. Four. And in the middle of the square, where the roads crossed, a shape.

---

He stopped.

He'd stopped for a great many things since the mill, with three dragons in his head. He'd never stopped like that. His feet stopped and the rest of him went on a little and came back.

Light.

Standing in the middle of the square. Taller than a tower. Pale gold, steady, with shapes in it. A body, long, with wings folded along it as a heron folds them. And from the body, three necks. Long. And on the three necks, three heads.

Looking three ways. Down three of the four roads.

---

He knew it.

He'd known it all his life. Every market day of his life. At the crossroads at Harrow's End, where the lane met the Way, in a stone shrine no bigger than a dog-kennel with a bowl in front of it for coppers. Cut in the stone of the shrine's back, worn smooth by a hundred years of rain and hands: a shape. Three heads. Three faces. Looking three ways. Down three roads.

The three-faced god.

He'd put a copper in its bowl on the way home from market every week since he was old enough to carry one. Some days for the gods. Some days for whoever emptied the bowl. He'd never asked which.

---

Aurveth lifted her head.

Behind him. He felt it before he heard it. She'd come down on the road behind them, slow, heavy, dull, with her sides going in and out short and shallow as they'd gone since the pour. Her great head low.

She lifted it. Toward the light.

And breathed.

---

Long. Deep. Slow.

He heard it. He felt it through the bond like a door opening in a shut house. Her sides went out, and out, further than they'd gone in a month. Filling. Then the breath went out of her, long, a sound like wind going out of a sail. And in again. Deep.

She breathed properly.

For the first time since Coldtarn.

---

He turned round.

She was standing on the road with her head up and her eyes half shut, breathing. The bronze hadn't come back. She was dull still, thin, the plates proud along her spine. But her sides were full. Going in and out, slow and deep, as a sleeping horse breathes in a warm stable.

*Oh,* she said.

That was all. *Oh.*

---

He had two coppers in his coat.

He found them without looking. One was Marrow's. Worn. The ship-and-wave almost gone off it. *Don't spend it.* The other was his own, the last of his own, from somewhere. A Hearthmere copper with a lamp on it.

He went to the milestone at the square's south corner.

It had a flat top. Like the shrine's ledge at home.

He put his own copper on it.

Out of habit. He didn't think. It was what you did at a crossroads, on the way home.

---

The light spoke.

In him. Through all three of them at once. He felt it come in through Kharrak and Lisska and Aurveth together, through three bonds, so that it came into his head as three voices.

And the three voices were one.

His marks went hot.

The three of them. The bond-marks on his chest, rust-red and green and bronze, that had come up on him at the mill in the first autumn when they chose him. Hot. As if somebody had laid a hand on each one fresh from the fire.

He put his hand to his chest.

***Farmer,*** said the Wayfarer.

---

He didn't know what to say.

He'd thought about it, in a way. Not about this. About gods. At the shrine every market day, with the copper going into the bowl. If you met one. What you'd ask.

He'd always known what he'd ask.

"Why me?"

---

He said it aloud. Cracked. At the light in the middle of the square.

"Why three of them. Why a farmer. I was cutting barley. I'd never touched a dragon. There were riders on the Scarfell who'd waited their whole lives for one and stood on the ledge every summer and been walked past." He thought of Arn, on the ledge, in a clean shirt. "And three of them came to me. In a mill. Dying. Why?"

The three heads didn't move. Looking down three roads.

The light was very still.

---

***Because you have room.***

Three voices. One.

He waited.

Nothing else came.

---

He didn't understand it.

He stood in the square with his hand on his chest where the mark burned and turned it over and didn't understand it at all. *Room.* As if he were a barn. As if he were a house with a spare bed.

But behind him, Kharrak made a sound.

Low. In his chest. Not a growl. And Lisska, on the milestone at the north corner, put her head on one side. And Aurveth, breathing, opened her eyes all the way and looked at the light.

They understood it. He felt them understand it.

He didn't ask them to explain. He thought they'd tell him when they thought he'd understand, and not before. They were like that.

---

He asked the other thing.

"Will you come?" he said. "Home. To fight them. There's six of them awake and drinking, and a coast with nothing left on it, and a lion's knight who said this was a song." He looked at the three heads. "Will you come back with us and fight?"

***No.***

The three voices. Gentle.

***I have only this left.***

He felt it look at the three dragons. Not at him. At them.

***It is for them.***

---

He didn't know what that meant either.

He was afraid he did.

---

It told him about the hall at dusk.

Through Aurveth. Slow. In her old deep voice, so that he heard it as her, and as the light, both.

*The others are gathering. In the hall. Where the road crosses the ridge. North and east.* A pause. *The other bond is there. Come. Two hands.*

*Two hands,* he said.

*Two hands,* said Aurveth.

He thought about the lock. Under Greyhold. *One hand shut it. Two hands open it together.* And a woman with her palm flat on a screaming door, ten paces from him, saying *not yet.*

---

He went to the square's north-east corner at dusk.

Where the western road went off toward the hills. He stood by the milestone there and looked along it. North and east. The hills going away green, fold behind fold, toward where the light was beginning to come up out of the grass.

On the ridge to the north-east, far off, a shape.

Long. Grey. Lying in the light along the ridge's crown. A hill on a hill.

And beside it, smaller, another. Grey. Lying.

---

The lion, he thought.

He stood at the crossroads with his marks still warm under his shirt and his copper on the milestone behind him and Marrow's in his coat, and looked at it a long time.

Behind him, Aurveth breathed. Long and slow and deep. As she hadn't since Coldtarn.

He didn't sleep that night either. He sat on the paving at the crossroads with his back against her side, and felt her breathe, and watched the light.
