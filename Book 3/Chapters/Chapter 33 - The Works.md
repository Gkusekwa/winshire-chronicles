---

The clock said six and Pike walked the yards.

Every morning. Tobin could have set his loaf by it. The clock on its hill above Ironhythe struck six over the Coldrun in the smoke, and before the last stroke had gone out over the water Master Engineer Agathe Pike came down the iron stair from her office at the Low Yards' gate, with her smoked spectacles pushed up on her forehead and Engineer Strake at her elbow, and walked.

The whole mile of it. Every shed. Every slip. Every forge.

She never asked a price. She looked at everything as if she were measuring it for something, and she was.

---

The Low Yards were a mile long.

Down the Coldrun from the clock-hill, along the canal, where Ironhythe built what Ironhythe built. Iron sheds as long as a street. Slipways. Cranes on rails, that moved along the yards on their own wheels hissing like kettles. Steam-hammers that went *down* so hard the canal shivered. The noise never stopped. It went on all night, under the green lamps. You stopped hearing it after a week and started hearing it again in your sleep.

Three thousand of Sarovel's hands worked in it.

His. Not his. He'd brought them. That was all. He'd walked them up the Sun Road and over the Iron Fells and down into this, and Moss had written every name, and Kessmoor had given every one a loaf a day and iron coin on the seventh, as Moss had promised. They carried. They hauled. They struck. They did what hands did.

They were alive.

---

The biggest yard was the Ring Yard.

At the Low Yards' southern end, where the canal turned. A field of cinders and iron plates, flat, a quarter-mile across. And on it, laid out on the ground like a drawing, the thing.

Twelve engines. Heartstone engines, each the size of a barn, on iron sledges, in a circle. Black. With fireboxes in their sides and brass in their guts and chimneys that weren't lit yet.

And between them, chain.

---

He'd never seen chain like it.

He'd seen chain. He'd heard about more. The crown's chain on its six wains, the links each as long as a man, bright, with the old hooked words struck on the insides where nobody looked, going out to the Ring and not coming back. The second lot, under Greyhold, running out into the river.

This was more.

Links as long as a man, and thicker. Kessmoor iron, black. Joining engine to engine round the ring, and going in across the ring from each engine to its middle, like the spokes of a wheel. Lying on the cinders. Waiting to be lifted.

The first chains again, in iron.

---

The hands called it the Bridle.

He didn't know who'd started it. Somebody in the first week. You put a bridle on a horse. You put this on something bigger. It had stuck.

Kessmoor didn't call it that. Kessmoor called it *the Accord's fallback*. On the drawings, on the clerks' boards, on the orders that came down from Pike's office every morning in Strake's exact hand. *If the elders fail. If the line breaks. The fallback.*

The hands called it the Bridle.

---

He struck at the fifth chain-forge.

There were twenty. Along the Ring Yard's south side, in an iron shed open on one side to the yard, so the links could be swung out on the cranes as they were made. Twenty forges, twenty smiths, and at each one, two or three hands to strike. Sarovel's hands. Big ones, for the sledges. He was all elbows, and always had been.

He struck anyway. He'd made them let him. If his hands were striking, he'd strike.

At the fourth forge, the one beside his, the smith was Tam Ashdown.

---

He'd known Tam at Millbrook.

Years ago. Before the lion. When Tobin was a student at Greyhold who couldn't get chosen and was loud about it, and Tam was a smith's boy at Millbrook with big hands and a slow smile who was drawn to the Restoration's promises, as Tobin was. They'd drunk together at the Kettle once. Tobin had talked; Tam had listened.

Tam had stood on a hedge-wain with a torch once, beside Odo. Tobin had held a barricade. They didn't talk about either.

Tam was a smith now. Broad. Burned at the forearms. With his sister's eyes, which Tobin had only noticed this summer, because Tam had got thin enough for his face to show them.

---

Tam had come up the Iron Road in the spring.

With forty Hearthmere smiths. The Accord had hired them. Hearthmere had smiths and nothing to feed them with; Kessmoor had bread and needed smiths. That was the Accord. It worked.

And with Tam, his master.

Odo Marl. Huge. Bald. Forearms burned from forty years at a forge. Laughing like a bellows. In a chair. A wooden chair on wheels, that Tam had built for him after the forge at Millbrook burned and Odo's legs broke under the beam, and that Odo drove round the yards by the wheels with his great burned hands as if it were a cart and he were a bad-tempered horse.

He couldn't strike. He could see.

---

Odo looked at the chain on the hundred-and-thirty-second.

He wheeled himself along it. The whole length of one spoke, from the outer engine in to the ring's middle, link by link, on the cinders. Tobin watched him from the forge. Odo stopped at a link and put his great hand on it and sat there.

Then he called up the yard.

"That one'll go."

---

Strake came.

Thin, young, exact. Her hands burned worse than they'd been. She'd been on the Candle Downs with the heartstone wagon, the hands said, and come back, and her crew hadn't. She came down the spoke to Odo's chair and looked at the link.

"Why?"

"Cold-shut," said Odo. He tapped the weld with a knuckle. "Poured too fast. Kessmoor iron, from the new furnaces. You can hear it." He tapped it again. "Hear that? It doesn't ring. It thuds." He wheeled on. Stopped. Another link. "That one too." On. "That one."

Seven links, on one spoke.

Strake had two of them cut out and tested. In the press at the yard's end. Tobin heard the press go. Both links parted at the welds like bread.

---

Pike thanked him.

The next morning. On her walk. She stopped at Odo's chair on the cinders and took her spectacles off her forehead and looked at him through them, which nobody had ever seen her do.

"Thank you, Master Marl," she said. "Very good. Seven links in a spoke. We'll have the new furnaces' pours checked."

On the hundred-and-thirty-third Odo was moved off the works.

To the Hearthmere smiths' barracks, across the canal. For his health, the order said. The chair couldn't manage the cinders. It wasn't safe.

---

"He frightens the hands," said Strake.

To nobody. At the fourth forge, on the morning Odo went, standing by Tam's anvil with a drawing under her arm. She didn't look at anyone when she said it.

Tam didn't look up from the link he was striking.

"No he doesn't," he said.

Strake didn't answer. She went on along the forges.

---

Tam asked on the hundred-and-thirty-fourth.

Between heats. The link in the fire, the bellows going. He stood back from the anvil wiping his face, and looked along the forges at the strikers, all of them, Sarovel's hands and Hearthmere's and Kessmoor's, and said it to all of them at once. Not loud.

"Anybody heard from my sister?"

They looked at him.

"Maren," said Tam. "Ashdown. The lion's knight." He looked at the fire. "She was at Ironhythe. In the spring. At the Hall. I was on the Iron Road. I missed her by two days."

Nobody had heard.

---

"She went to sea," said one of the Sarovel hands.

A big man at the sixth forge. He'd had it from a Kessmoor clerk who'd had it from a gull-hawk. "West. Past the Shatters. On a salvage boat. To find something."

Tam looked at him.

"She hates boats," he said.

He took the link out of the fire and put it on the anvil and struck it, and didn't ask again.

---

The order came on the hundred-and-thirty-fifth.

A drawing. In Strake's hand, from Pike's office, on a board. Strake brought it to the fourth forge herself and propped it on the anvil's horn and stood back.

Tobin looked at it over Tam's shoulder.

A ring.

Iron. Round. The height of two men standing one on another's shoulders. Thick as a man's waist. To sit at the Bridle's heart, Strake said, where all the spokes met at the ring's middle. Its outer face plain.

Its inner face cut.

---

Six hollows.

In a circle. Round the inside of the ring, evenly spaced. Each one the shape of an egg, cut into the iron a hand's breadth deep. Drawn to a template: Strake had the template, a piece of brass shaped like half an egg, and she put it on the anvil beside the drawing.

Six.

Tam looked at it for a long time.

---

He'd made collars once.

Tobin knew it. Everybody from Millbrook knew it. For the Restoration's binders, in the year before the lion, in Odo's smithy, when Odo was drunk on the Restoration's promises and Tam was drunk on Odo. Iron rings with hooked words struck on their insides. For beasts' necks. He'd made a dozen. He'd found out what they were for when he saw one on a beast.

He'd never talked about it. Tobin had heard him not talk about it, once, at the Kettle, for an hour.

---

"What's it for?" said Tam.

"The fallback," said Strake.

"What goes in the hollows?"

Strake didn't look away. "That's not the forge's business, Master Ashdown."

Tam looked at the drawing. At the six hollows in a circle. At the brass template on his anvil, the shape of half an egg.

He picked it up. He turned it over. He put it down.

"No," he said.

---

Strake waited.

"I'll make chain," said Tam. "Chain's chain. I can see what it's for. You put it round something and it holds." He didn't look at her. He looked at the template. "I'll not make that. I can't see what it's for." He wiped his hands on his apron. "I made a thing once I couldn't see the use of. For people who said it was for something else. I'll not do it twice."

---

Pike came herself.

That afternoon. Not on her walk. Down the iron stair at three, when nobody had ever seen her come down at three, and along the forges to the fourth, with her spectacles up and her sooty hands at her sides.

She stood by Tam's anvil and listened.

He said it again. The same words. He didn't add any. Pike listened to all of them, and nodded.

"Of course," she said.

---

Tam went on the hundred-and-thirty-sixth.

Off the works. With the wagons. Twenty of them, Kessmoor's, going down the Iron Road and on to the Marches, to the Accord's camp behind the Wend, with iron for the camp and lent engines for the Marshal. Tam on the fourth wagon's box with his tools in a roll. Paid to the day, Strake said. Every coin.

A Kessmoor smith took the fourth forge. A thin man with a quick hammer. He looked at the drawing for a minute and started on the ring that afternoon.

Odo came across the canal in his chair to see Tam off. He sat on the towpath and watched the wagons go and didn't laugh like a bellows at all.

---

Nobody asked.

That was the thing Tobin noticed. He kept noticing it. Twenty smiths on the forges, and fifty hands at them, and nobody asked why a smith from Hearthmere who'd eaten Kessmoor's bread all summer would turn down Kessmoor's pay for the best iron work in the yards and go off on a wagon to a camp in the Marches.

Everyone looked at their own anvil.

He looked at his. Then he looked at the fourth forge, where the thin Kessmoor man was cutting the first hollow into the inside of the ring with a cold chisel, to the brass template, carefully.

---

He was on the night shift on the hundred-and-thirty-seventh.

Hauling coal. The heartstone engines at the ring's edge had to be fired, for testing; their fireboxes took coal to start and the boxes in their sides took the other thing. The coal came in barrows from the canal, and the hands pushed the barrows across the cinders under the green lamps, and tipped them at the engines' feet, where the engine-hands shovelled.

The other thing came in crates.

Iron crates. Sealed. Up the canal at night on Kessmoor barges, and lifted off on the cranes, and carried to the engines on sledges, and opened only inside the engines' sides by the engine-hands, with the lids toward the engine so the hands couldn't see in.

*Old stock*, the hands said. Nobody had seen inside one.

---

He tipped his barrow at the seventh engine.

And the man shovelling at the seventh engine's firebox looked up.

---

Thin. Grey in the face. In a Kessmoor engine-hand's leather apron, too big for him. With his arms wrong.

Tobin saw the arms first. The shovel in them. The right forearm bent a little, halfway, where a bone had knit crooked. The left wrist thick and stiff, so the hand on the shovel's haft didn't turn properly. Arms that had been broken. Both. And set badly, by somebody in a hurry, or by nobody.

He knew who'd broken them.

He looked up at the face.

---

Callis.

Knight of the gate. At Greyhold. When Tobin was a student, all elbows, unchosen, loud about it. A knight with a hound called Grip and a seal he carried for the Restoration that nobody knew about. Who'd opened the river gate on a night two winters ago and let the Restoration in, and taken Cassian down with Thorne's key.

Maren had broken his arm, once. And his other wrist, another time.

Corvin's man.

---

Callis knew him.

Tobin saw it. The shovel stopped. The grey face went greyer.

They looked at each other across the barrow of coal, under the green lamp, by the seventh engine's firebox, with the yard's noise going on round them as if nothing at all were happening.

---

"Tobin," said Callis.

Very low. His voice had gone thin.

"Sir Callis."

"Don't." Callis looked round. At the other engine-hands. At the night. "Not *sir*. I'm nobody here." He held the shovel in his wrong hands. "I feed the boxes. They pay in bread. Same as yours."

"You opened the river gate."

"I'm *nobody*." Callis's voice cracked. "Please. Tobin. Please." He looked at the coal. "Grip died in the winter. In the snow. On the road. I've nobody. I feed the boxes. Please."

---

Tobin didn't say anything.

He looked at the man. At the crooked arms. At the leather apron, too big. At the iron crate standing open in the seventh engine's side, with its lid toward the engine, and Callis's back between it and Tobin.

He picked up his empty barrow.

He went back across the cinders to the canal for more coal.

He didn't look back.

---

He wrote to Rowan on the hundred-and-thirty-eighth.

By Moss's courier. Moss had a courier who went down the Iron Road every third day to the Accord's camp in the Marches, with the yards' reports for the Marshal. Tobin had never sent anything by it. He went to Moss's office at the yard gate in the dinner hour and asked for paper and was given it, and wrote, standing, at Moss's desk, with Moss writing names at the next desk and not looking.

He'd never been good at letters. He wrote as he shouted. Short.

*Marshal.*

*Callis is in the works. Corvin's man. The one who opened the river gate. He's feeding the heartstone engines in the Ring Yard. Seventh engine. Night shift.*

*I don't know who hired him.*

*Tobin.*

---

He folded it. He gave it to Moss.

Moss took it. He looked at Tobin over his spectacles.

"For the Marshal," said Tobin.

"For the Marshal," said Moss. He put it in the courier's bag without looking at it. "She'll have it in four days."

---

He didn't tell anyone else.

He thought about it. All that week. On the forges, striking for the thin Kessmoor man now, who was cutting hollows into the inside of an iron ring to a brass template. At night, hauling coal past the seventh engine without looking at it. Lying in the hands' barracks with three thousand people breathing round him in the dark.

He could have stood up in the dinner hour and said it. To the hands. *There's a Restoration man in the yards feeding the engines.* They'd have listened. They always listened when he shouted.

And on the seventh day there'd have been no iron coin.

And on the eighth, no bread.

---

Pike held the wages. Pike held the bread.

Moss wrote every name. Every one. Three thousand names, in a book, in Moss's office at the gate. The names that got bread. Kessmoor kept its word. It had kept it all summer. A loaf a day, every day, on the day it said.

It would go on keeping it as long as the hands did what hands did.

He'd brought them here. They were alive.

He said nothing.

---

He looked up at the clock instead.

On the hundred-and-fortieth, in the morning, at six. From the Ring Yard's edge, with his loaf in his hand. Up the hill, over the smoke, at the white face and the black hands, telling the whole valley it was six, as it had told the kingdoms of Winshire it was noon the day they signed.

It had never been wrong.

He wondered how it knew. He'd wondered that the first day, from the gallery in the Hall, with his hands round him. He still didn't know.

Below him on the cinders the Bridle lay in its circle, twelve engines and their chains. And at its heart, in a cradle of timber, the ring. Half cut. Three hollows done.

Three to go.
