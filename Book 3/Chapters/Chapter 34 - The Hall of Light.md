---

The Mother got up on the hundred-and-thirty-seventh.

Maren felt it before she saw it. Through Orrun. A shifting in the stone of the whole hill, as if the hill had taken a breath. She'd been sitting against the milestone in the dawn, keeping her watch while he slept, and she felt the rock under her move.

She looked up.

The Grey Mother was standing.

---

She hadn't seen her stand. In twelve days. She'd lain on the hill's crown with her paws out and her head on them, and the light rising round her, and looked at Orrun.

Now she stood. Grey. Enormous. Her great mane of something like smoke stirring round her head and shoulders. And the light round her didn't rise and sink with the hill's breath any more. It came with her.

She turned her head. South-west.

Then she walked.

---

Down the hill. Along the road. Slowly. Each paw coming down on the grey paving so softly the paving didn't crack, and the hum in it rising under her as she went, so that the road sang where she walked.

Orrun got up and went after her.

He didn't ask. He didn't look back. He walked at her shoulder, small beside her, grey beside grey.

Maren got on his back.

---

The old man followed on the grey horse.

Gannet behind on foot, with his canvas bag.

A day. South-west along the road, over the green country, with the deer coming out of the hills to watch the Mother pass and the birds going up in front of her in white clouds and coming down behind. Nobody spoke. The road went on, straight, grey, toward a long ridge that lay across the land ahead like a wall.

And on the ridge, where the road crossed it, a hall.

---

Roofless.

She saw it from a league off. Columns. Grey. A long double row of them along the ridge's top, tall, square, standing up against the sky with nothing on them. Whatever they'd held up had gone. Fallen, or never been, or gone into the light with everything else.

They came up the last of the slope at dusk.

---

A floor of grey stone, paved, between the columns. Clean. No sand. No grass in the joints. As if someone swept it.

Down its middle, the length of the hall, a pool.

Long. Narrow. Cut into the floor, edged with the same grey stone. Clear water, still, cold, so clear she could see its bottom a man's height down, grey stone, and a channel at its southern end where a spring came in, and the water went out at the north end into another channel under the floor.

And at the north end, over the doorway, an arch.

---

The Mother lay down outside.

Along the hall's eastern side. Outside the columns. She was too big to go in. She lay down along the whole length of the hall on the ridge's grass and put her great head in between two of the columns, so that it lay on the hall's floor at the pool's edge, and closed her eyes.

Orrun went in and lay down by her head.

Maren got off him.

---

The light came up over the arch.

She saw it come. It gathered on the stone over the doorway as it had gathered in the air before the Mother's face on the hill. Into lines. Pale gold. The hooked letters.

And stayed. Not fading. Not changing. Written there, in light, over the door, as if they'd been cut into it.

The old man was off the horse and in under the arch before she could take his elbow.

---

He read it.

She watched him. She couldn't read it. She knew the shapes. She'd know them anywhere. She'd copied them in charcoal into the back of a black book, kneeling on wet stone at the knight's lock under Greyhold with a dead man on a plank behind her. She'd carried them a year without knowing what they said.

The old man had read them aloud to her once. In the cavern. At the shut place. With the stone screaming.

He read them again.

---

"*One hand shut it,*" he said. Very quietly.

"*Two hands open it together.*"

"*What is opened together is kept together, or it is not kept.*"

---

He went very still.

He stood under the arch in his blanket with his face turned up to the light over the door, and his steady old man's hands at his sides, and didn't move at all.

"Idony."

He didn't answer.

"Idony. It's the lock's."

"Yes," said the old man.

He kept looking at it.

"They wrote the lock," he said at last. "The lock under Greyhold. The knight's lock. The shut place. They wrote it. The same hand." He swallowed. "*They* did. Not the crown. Not the first knights. They did."

He didn't say anything else for an hour.

---

The Lamp came at dawn on the hundred-and-fortieth.

Walking.

Up the ridge from the east, out of the sunrise. On the light. She saw it come: a brightness on the eastern slope, coming up through the long low sun, that was brighter than the sun and wasn't the sun. Walking. With legs. With a head.

With a horn.

---

She couldn't look at it long.

Nobody could. White-gold. Tall as the columns. A shape like a horse and not like a horse, long-legged, long-necked, with a mane that was light streaming off it backward in no wind, and on its forehead a single horn, long, straight, bright as noon. Like a spear of noon.

It came up into the hall at its south end, by the spring, and stood.

The pool caught it. The whole hall lit.

---

The old man had his hand over his eyes.

"Sarovel's," he said. "The Sun on the Stair. The priests sing to it at dawn. In Solenne." He laughed, behind his hand. "I've heard them. They're very bad. They're very bad singers."

The Lamp didn't move. It stood at the south end of the hall in its own light, and the pool lay at its feet like a sword.

---

The Lady came at dusk.

Up the spring's channel. Into the pool.

Maren saw the water change first. The clear cold water of the pool going pale, as if light had come into it from underneath. And then the light was moving. Along the pool. Coiling. Long.

A shape under the water.

---

Serpent-kind. Pale. Cold. Light under the water, the colour of the moon on a frost. Coiling the whole length of the pool, from the spring at the south end to the channel at the north, and further, so that she couldn't see where it ended, as if the pool went down further than its bottom.

And then a head came up out of the water.

On a neck like a river. Long. Slow. Rising out of the pool at its middle, streaming, pale, and a head on it like a heron's, narrow, long-beaked, with eyes the colour of deep water.

It looked at the old man.

---

He knelt.

She saw him do it. He didn't decide to. His legs went, on the paving by the pool's edge, and he knelt there with his steady hands on his knees, looking up at the pale head on its river of a neck.

"I'm sorry," he said. To Maren, without looking at her. "I can't help it. My grandmother prayed to her every night. On the ferry. On Glasswater. Every night of her life before she drew the rope. A hundred years of us. Praying to her." He looked up at the head. "I never did. I sat in the stern and counted the stars."

His voice cracked.

"I can't get up," he said. "Give me a while."

He didn't get up for a long time.

---

The Mother wrote on the hundred-and-forty-first.

In the air over the pool. In light. The old man got up off his knees at last and read it.

*We called.*

*Others did not answer.*

*They have gone out.*

---

Four.

The Mother along the hall's east side with her head on the floor. The Lamp at the south end, too bright to see. The Lady in the pool, coiled, her head lying on the water now like a long pale boat. And one more, that hadn't come yet, that she knew was coming, because Orrun knew it.

Four. Out of all of them.

She thought of the black plain in the showing. The old ones gathered on it, so many she couldn't count them. Going thin. Going into the light. A few left, going away west.

Four.

---

He came at noon on the hundred-and-forty-second.

Over the ridge from the south-west. Three shapes in the sky. A red one, big, so big she didn't believe it at first. A green one beside it. And behind them, slow, low, steady, a bronze one, dull, thin, but flying straight.

She'd have known them anywhere.

He'd waited two days at the crossroads, he told her afterwards. For Aurveth. Until she could fly a day's distance.

---

They came down outside the hall's western columns.

On the grass of the ridge. Kharrak first, landing heavily, folding his great red wings. Lisska light beside him, on a column's broken top, her head turning. And Aurveth last, slowly, settling onto the grass with a sound like a hill sighing.

And a man got down off the red one.

---

He walked into the hall.

Through the columns. Onto the grey floor. He stopped at the pool's edge and looked at it: the Lady in it, the Lamp at its end, the Mother's great head on the floor. He looked. His face didn't change much. It never had.

Then he looked at her.

---

She hadn't seen him since Ironhythe.

The yard. At dawn. With the clock telling everybody it was a quarter past five. *Ask for a reef-pilot. And don't pay what they ask first.*

He was thinner. Burned at the hands: she could see the scars, shiny, pink, up both palms to the wrist. His face was the same. Plain. Tired. Not giving anything away.

He was looking at her neck. At the grey up the side of it to the jaw. At the white in her hair.

At her mouth, where it didn't move.

---

"You came," she said.

Her voice came out of the side of her mouth that worked.

"The old one was dying," he said.

His came out cracked. Thin. As if he'd been shouting for days into a wind, and lost it. She'd never heard it like that. He'd always had a farmer's voice. Slow and flat and carrying.

He didn't say *song*.

She didn't either.

---

Nothing happened.

She'd thought something would. With both of them there. The elders in the hall, and the two of them at the pool's edge, and the old man standing between them looking from one to the other.

Nothing.

The light over the arch brightened. She saw it. When he'd walked in, the hooked letters over the door had flared, and stayed bright. And in the air over the middle of the pool, light was gathering. All four elders' light, coming together, white-gold and pale and grey, into a brightness the size of a cart.

It didn't make words.

It hung there.

---

"It's waiting," said the old man.

He looked at the light over the pool. Then back at the arch. At the sentence over the door.

"*Two hands,*" he said.

He looked at Maren. Then at the farmer.

"I think," he said, very carefully, as if he were reading it off a stone he couldn't quite see, "it wants you to stand together."

---

They were ten paces apart.

On either side of the pool's end. She looked at the farmer across the water. He looked at her.

She hadn't stood with him since the cavern. Ten paces apart, then. With the stone screaming. *You kept it.* *Not yet.*

He held out his hand.

---

Across the corner of the pool. His right hand. The palm scarred pink, shiny, from the wrist to the fingers. Held out. Not far. Just held out, as you hold out a hand to help someone over a stile.

She looked at it.

Her left hand was in her coat. Dead. She couldn't have felt his if she'd given it to him.

She went round the pool's corner, and took his hand with her right.

---

The light formed.

At once. Over the pool. Out of the brightness hanging there, lines. Pale gold. The hooked letters, drawn in the air, steady.

And she felt it. Through Orrun, lying by his mother's head. The meaning, coming. And beside her, through his hand, she felt the farmer feel it too. Through his. Three of them. Coming in through three bonds at once and one more, and all of it one meaning.

The old man read it.

---

"*Lion's bond,*" he said. "*Dragons' bond.*"

"*Together.*"

A pause. The light held.

"*Ask.*"

---

The farmer's hand went to his chest.

His other hand. The left. She felt him do it. He put it flat on his shirt over his heart, hard, as if something there had burned him, and held it there, and then took it away.

The light over the pool had changed. A new brightness in it. Not white-gold, not pale, not grey. Something that moved. That turned three ways at once.

The fourth one.

She hadn't seen it come. It hadn't come. It was there.

---

*It speaks through them,* said Orrun. From the floor by his mother's head. *The farmer's three. It doesn't need to come. It's in them.*

She looked at the farmer.

He was looking at the light, with his hand at his side again. As she watched, the turning brightness in the light moved, and his hand went back to his chest. Flat. Pressing. As if it burned.

And away.

*His marks,* said Orrun. *Every time it speaks. They go hot.*

---

Behind them, between two columns at the hall's western edge, a man sat on the grass with a board on his knee.

Gannet. With his pencil.

He'd come up the ridge at dusk on the first day and sat there ever since. Out of the way. Watching. Copying the light over the arch, and the light over the pool, onto his paper, carefully, a letter at a time, as the old man read them out.

He didn't look up when she looked at him.

---

She was still holding the farmer's hand.

She didn't let go. Nor did he. They stood at the pool's corner, side by side, the knight with her dead arm in her coat and her grey neck, the farmer with his scarred palms and his voice gone, holding hands like two children at a fair who'd been told not to lose each other.

And in the air over the pool, four gods who'd been Titans waited for them to ask.

---

"Well," said the farmer. Cracked. Low. To her, not to them.

"You ask," said Maren. "You've got the voice."

He looked at her.

It was the closest either of them had come to laughing since the day they met.
