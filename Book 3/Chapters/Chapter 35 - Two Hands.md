---

He asked.

With his voice gone to a crack and her hand in his, at the corner of the pool, in a hall without a roof, at the end of the world. He'd asked a great many things of a great many people in two years. Tide Councils. Crowns. Clans. A barley farmer's questions, in a barley farmer's words, and mostly they'd said no.

He asked four gods.

"Can you put them back to sleep?"

---

The Lamp answered.

He felt it before he saw it. A brightening, at the hall's south end, where the horned shape stood by the spring. The light over the pool going white. Harder to look at. The letters forming in it white-gold and sharp-edged, as if somebody had cut them out of noon.

The old man put his hand up to shade his eyes and read.

"*No.*"

---

Calder had known.

He'd known since the crossroads. Since the Wayfarer said *I have only this left*. He'd known since Aurveth lay on the ledge above Blackwater going dull. He'd known, if he was honest, since the Bell came in over the Saltings and he'd laid a river of fire across a harbour and the thing had only turned its head.

He stood there with her hand in his and let it be said.

---

More came.

"*The houses are broken,*" read the old man.

"That's the temples," he said, half to himself. "The six temples. The Ring, Vael's, the Scald, the Sun Stair, the Bell. Hearthmere's." He read on.

"*The stones are in the last one's back.*"

The relics. Six of them, grown into the Last Titan's plates. Five dark and one like a coal. Maren had seen them go in, one by one, across a whole year.

"*The sleeping cost us what we were,*" read the old man. Slowly. "*We have nothing left to pay with.*"

---

The light rested.

He felt it rest. As a man rests after saying a hard thing to someone he's sorry for. The letters faded and the brightness over the pool sank, and the four of them, the Mother's head on the floor and the Lamp by the spring and the Lady in the water and the thing that was in his three, were quiet.

He stood with her hand in his.

Neither of them said anything.

---

It came again at dusk.

The Lamp's light. The same white-gold. The old man got up off the paving where he'd sat and read.

"*Two of us will take a shape again,*" he said. "*And stand with you.*"

He looked at the Lamp. At the Lady in the pool.

"*Two of us will give what is left,*" he read. "*And go out.*"

A pause. The letters held.

"*To the lion,*" he read. "*To the three.*"

---

Calder felt Aurveth hear it.

Outside the western columns, on the grass, where she lay. He felt it go into her through the bond like a stone into a well. Down. And down. A long way. And at the bottom, something.

He knew her fear. This was something else.

She was very still.

Beside him, he felt Maren's hand go still in his. Through it, faintly, as if through a wall, he felt something on her side too. The lion. Lying by his mother's head. Hearing it.

---

They let go at dusk.

When the light sank. He didn't decide to. Her hand went out of his and his out of hers, and the moment it did, the brightness over the pool went out like a lamp when the door opens.

They sat down on the pool's edge.

Apart. A pace between them. Their boots by the water.

---

He didn't know how to start.

He'd written to her once. A whole page, nine lines, at Bel's table with Ada not helping. He'd said things in it he'd never said aloud to anybody. About his father. About hers. About Senna. And Lisska had taken it west and he'd never known what she'd thought of it.

He'd never had a night to say any of it. He'd said so, in the letter.

He had a night now.

---

"I got your letter," she said.

She said it first. To the water. He looked at her.

"At Orrin," she said. "At midwinter. Lisska came in over the glass at dawn." The side of her mouth that moved moved a little. "I read it three times. Twice so as not to laugh at the spelling."

"And the third?"

"For the rest of it."

---

He looked at the water.

"*My father payd the dues,*" he said. Cracked. He'd written it. He'd never said it. "Every year. To the Clan. He kept his head down and paid and died in his bed. I thought that was the best a man could do." He looked at his scarred hands. "I thought so till I was thirty."

"And now?"

"Now I don't know what the best a man can do is," said Calder. "I've done a lot of things. None of them were keeping my head down."

---

"Mine died in a week," she said.

He knew. He'd written that too. *Yours dyed when his hound dyed. Ada told me.*

"Poachers," said Maren. "They killed his hound in the Brackenwood. He was a knight. He came home and sat in his chair by the fire and didn't get up. A week." She looked at the water. "I was fourteen. I sat on the floor by the chair and watched him go. Nobody could do anything. One life between two."

She was quiet.

"I grew up knowing exactly what a bond costs," she said. "And I went and got one anyway."

---

"Senna died of fever," he said.

He'd never said it to anyone who didn't know her. He said it to the water.

"Seven years ago. Wren was four. She was ill nine days and then she wasn't. There was no bond." He stopped. "It felt like there was. It felt like half of me went with her. I kept waking up and reaching for the other half."

Maren didn't say anything.

"I wrote that," he said. "*It felt like there was.*"

"I know," said Maren. "I read it three times."

---

"*I have one life between four now,*" he said.

From the letter. He'd been proud of that line. He'd worked at it a week.

"*It's not like a quarter each,*" he said. "*It's like being a house with the doors open.*"

Maren looked at him for a long time.

"One life between two," she said, "is a house with one door. And somebody always standing in it."

He thought about that.

"Yes," he said. "I'd think it would be."

---

Her mouth.

He looked at it. In the light off the pool, which was low now, a glow under the water where the Lady lay. The left side of her mouth that didn't move. The grey up her neck. The white in her hair.

"What is it?" he said.

"It's paid," said Maren.

He didn't ask by whom. He'd asked his own hands that, at Corsa, on the *Merry Agnes*, with Dorcas wetting the cloths. He knew by whom.

---

She said the thing she was afraid of.

Late. When the old man had fallen asleep in his blanket by the arch, for an hour, for once. When Gannet was a dark shape between the western columns, not moving. When the Lamp's light at the south end was the only brightness, and the Mother's great grey head lay on the floor by the pool with Orrun against it.

"He'll be what his kin is," she said.

---

He waited.

"The Mother's going to give what's left of her to him," said Maren. "To Orrun. You heard. Whatever she has. Whatever she was." She looked at the lion, lying against his mother's head with his eyes shut. "She was a Titan. They all were. That's what they are. What's left of Titans who stopped."

She was quiet.

"If she gives it to him," she said, "he'll be one."

---

"I've seen what they are," she said.

Low. To the water.

"I saw it go out of its chains at the Ruin. I watched it at the Ring with the stakes coming up. I stood at the shut place under Greyhold with my hand on the door and the stone screaming and watched it come down the cavern toward me." She looked at the lion. "That's his kin. That's what it is when it's what it is. The size of a hill. Drinking."

She turned her head and looked at Calder.

"I'm afraid he'll become that," she said. "The thing under Greyhold."

---

He didn't tell her he wouldn't.

He didn't know that. Nobody did. He wasn't going to say a thing he didn't know to a woman who'd held up a hill.

He said the thing he was afraid of instead.

"The old one's going to die," he said.

---

She waited, as he had.

"She's old," said Calder. "Older than the Clan. Older than anybody knows. She's been dying since Coldtarn. Refusing it. The call. She breathed at the crossroads for the first time in a month and I thought, *good*, and then the light said *to the three*." He looked out between the western columns, at the dark shape on the grass that was Aurveth. "She's waited her whole life for something. Since before the Roosts. Sigrun said so. And she found it. And I think it's the last thing she was waiting for."

He looked at his hands.

"I think when she gets it she'll go," he said.

---

Maren didn't comfort him.

He was glad. He didn't want comforting. She sat a pace off on the pool's edge with her boots by the water and her dead arm in her coat, and she didn't say *she won't*, or *you don't know*, or anything at all.

He didn't comfort her either.

They'd each said the thing. They left it there, on the stone between them, in the light off the water.

---

The night went on.

He'd sat up with ewes on nights like it. Nothing to be done but be there. The dark not moving, and the breathing.

Outside the western columns Kharrak lay along the grass with one eye open on the Lamp. He didn't like it. He didn't like anything brighter than his own fire. Lisska was on her broken column with her head under her wing. Aurveth lay with her chin on the ground and her eyes on the south-west, toward the crossroads, a day off. She didn't close them. He could feel her not closing them.

---

"How long is she?" he said.

Maren looked at the great grey head on the paving. At the rest of her beyond the columns, a ridge lying along the ridge.

"Four hundred and twelve paces," said Maren. "Nose to tail. I walked it on the hill."

"You walked a god."

"Twice. I got four hundred and ten the first time. She'd moved her tail." She looked at the lion against his mother's jaw. "He's thirty-eight."

He didn't ask when she'd walked Orrun. He thought she probably walked him most days.

---

He thought about his father's byre.

Built for four cows. Dry-stone, turf-roofed, with a door you went through bent. One spring there'd been a fifth, a red heifer bought cheap at Cotter's Cross, and his father wouldn't knock a wall through for her. She'd stood in the yard all that winter with her back to the weather and the snow on her, and every morning his father had gone out and looked at the wall as if it might have moved in the night.

"You can't keep a beast in a place it's outgrown," he said.

"He's never been kept anywhere," said Maren.

"That's not what I meant."

"I know what you meant." She was quiet. "On the ketch he lay on the ballast with his head under the forehatch, so I could put my hand down through it and reach him. Every night out from Blackwater. Twenty-nine nights." Her right hand was flat on the pool's edge, on the stone. "Nobody puts a hand down a hatch to a hill."

He didn't have anything to say to that. He said something anyway.

"Rain after a dry month comes all at once," he said. "Flattens what you waited on."

She looked at him.

"That's no help," said Calder.

"No," said Maren.

---

"It came through me," he said. "At the mill."

He hadn't meant to say it. It was the thing under the other thing.

"Three of them dying. All in an afternoon. It came in through me, all three, and I lay three days in the stubble and couldn't have told you my own name. Ada says I kept asking her where Bramble was." He turned his scarred hands over on his knees. "At the crossroads I asked the old thing why me. It said *because you have room.*" He looked at the dark between the columns. "As if I were a barn."

"Is that all it said?"

"That's all."

Maren looked at the lion. Then at him.

"Then it'll come through you," she said. "Whatever it gives them."

"I'd think so."

She didn't say anything else. He saw her right hand move on the stone, toward her coat, where the dead arm was. It stopped before it got there and went back.

---

"Four," she said, after a while.

He waited.

"Four things it's had off me. Since the spring." She didn't say what they were. He could see three of them from where he sat. "I counted them on the ketch. I thought I'd stop at four."

"Will you?"

"I don't know what I'll stop at," said Maren.

He thought of Wren's letter, in his coat, against the marks. *Bring back something from the end of the world.* He'd thought she meant a shell.

---

The old man woke when his hour was up.

He sat up in his blanket by the arch all at once, as if somebody had called him, and looked at the two of them on the pool's edge a pace apart, and at the light lying low in the water.

"What have I missed?" he said.

"Barns," said Calder.

The old man looked at Maren.

"Barns," said Maren.

---

The Lamp wrote the plan in the morning.

On the hundred-and-forty-third. At sunrise, when its light was strongest. He took her hand again without either of them saying anything, and the brightness came back over the pool at once, and the letters came.

White-gold. Sharp. The old man read them with his hand over his eyes.

---

"*There is one stone over the six.*"

"The control relic," said the old man. Under his breath. "The one from Hearthmere's vault. The coal in its back."

"*We made it,*" he read. "*Only one of us can hold it.*"

---

"*If one of us takes it out of the last one's back,*" he read, "*the six will hear.*"

He stopped. He read it again, silently, his lips moving. Then aloud.

"*They will come into the light with the one who holds it.*"

"*They will stop,*" he read. "*As we stopped.*"

---

Nobody said anything.

Calder stood at the pool's corner with Maren's hand in his and looked at the white-gold letters hanging in the air, and thought about it.

Not put them to sleep. Not kill them. Nobody could kill them. Stop them. As the gods had stopped. Take the stone out of the Last Titan's back, the one that held all six, and walk into the light with it, and the six would come too. Like dogs after a man with meat. Into the light. And put down what they were, and go on without it, as light.

Or go out.

---

"*I will carry it,*" read the old man.

The Lamp's words. The horned shape at the south end, standing by the spring in its own brightness, too bright to look at.

*I will carry it.*

The old man let his hand fall from his eyes. He looked at the Lamp, squinting, his eyes running. He didn't say anything.

He sat down on the paving by the arch.

---

Maren's hand moved in his.

He looked at her. She was looking at the Lamp. Then at the lion, by his mother's head. Then at him.

"It has to get to it," she said. "The relic. In the Last Titan's back. On a battlefield." She was quiet. "Someone has to get it there."

"Yes," said Calder.

"Two of them fight," said Maren. "Two of them give." She looked at the dragons outside the columns, and at the lion. "To us. So we can get it there."

"Yes," said Calder.

He didn't let go of her hand.

---

Between the western columns, on the grass, a man sat with a board on his knee.

He'd sat there six days. He'd written every line the old man read. The lock's sentence over the arch. *We called. Others did not answer.* *No.* *The houses are broken.* *To the lion. To the three.*

*I will carry it.*

He was writing it now. With a stub of pencil, worn down to the width of his thumb, carefully, a letter at a time, his lips not moving.

He didn't look up.

Calder watched him for a while. Then he looked back at the light.
