---

He'd said it at Blackwater.

That was the first thing he thought, and the thing he went on thinking all day, under everything else. He'd said it on the eighty-second day, or the eighty-third, he couldn't remember which; he'd said it sitting on a block of far-stone in a salvage hold under a stilt-house, with his tide-glass standing a third full beside him and his hands shaking a little less.

Half to himself. Looking at the glass.

*It answers a breath that is unbound.*

And Maren had looked up from her pencil, and he'd said, *Write that. I don't know what it means yet. Write it down so I can find out.*

---

He found out on the hundred-and-forty-fourth.

At sunrise, in the hall. With the farmer and the knight holding hands at the pool's corner like two children, and the Lamp at the south end too bright to look at, and the letters coming up white-gold in the air over the water.

He read them. That was what he was for.

---

"*We made the stone,*" he read, "*when we laid them down.*"

The relic. The seventh. The coal in the Last Titan's back.

"*A safeguard,*" he read. "*If they woke, one of us could hold it, and call them into the light.*"

He stopped. Breathed. Read on.

"*The small ones found it.*"

He knew who the small ones were now. He was one.

"*They used it to hold the last one in its chains.*"

---

Hearthmere's vault.

He'd been there. In the cavern under Greyhold, at the shut place, with Corvin's hand on his shoulder. Behind that door the crown had kept it. The crown's. Three hundred years in the dark under the oath, keeping the chains fed, keeping the lamps lit. Never used for what it was made for.

Used to hold a Titan down.

---

The next line came.

He read it before he knew he was reading it. His mouth said it. Then he heard it.

"*It answers a breath that is unbound.*"

---

He stopped.

He stood by the pool's edge with his hand up to shade his eyes from the Lamp's light, and didn't say the next thing, because there wasn't one. The line held. White-gold. Over the water.

The words he'd said in the salvage hold.

The same words. Exactly. Not near. Not like. The same, in the old tongue, in the hooked script, in light, written by a god who'd been a Titan.

He'd said them at Blackwater before he'd known there was a god to write them.

---

"Idony," said Maren.

From the pool's corner. With the farmer's hand in hers.

"I said that," he said. "At Blackwater. In the hold. You wrote it."

"I know."

"I didn't know what it meant." His voice wasn't steady. His hands were; his voice wasn't. "I didn't know where I'd got it. I thought I'd made it up. Looking at the glass." He looked up at the line in the air. "I hadn't. I'd read it. Somewhere. Off some stone, years ago, at Vael, in a book, and not known I'd read it. And it came back."

He was frightened. A little. He'd never been frightened of a sentence before.

"*Write it down so I can find out,*" he said. "Well. I've found out."

---

He told them what it meant.

At noon, when the light rested and the two of them let go of each other's hands and sat on the pool's edge apart. He sat on the paving between them, with his back against a column, and told them, slowly, as he understood it.

"The relic answers a breath," he said. "That's the Deep. That's what the Deep is. A breath. You'll see in a minute." He held up his steady hand. "And it only answers one that's *unbound*. Nothing between it and the Deep. Nothing holding it. No chain. No body. No word."

He looked at the Lamp.

"Theirs," he said. "The Unbound. The seventh ring. A person can't hold it. A bound beast can't. A chained Titan can't, even with it grown into its back; it can only sit there, under the plates, like a coal. Only one of them."

---

"That's why Hearthmere never used it," he said.

The farmer looked at him.

"The crown had it three hundred years," said Idony. "In a vault. The most powerful thing anybody ever made. And all they could do with it was keep it in the dark under the oath and let it feed the chains. They couldn't *use* it. They didn't have the breath." He laughed, a little. "They thought it was theirs. It never answered them once."

"And the Lamp can," said Maren.

"The Lamp can," said Idony. "If it can get to it."

---

Gannet asked at noon.

From between the western columns. Where he'd sat three days with his board on his knee. He'd been writing all morning. He looked up from the board and called across the hall, politely, the smile coming first.

"Master."

Idony looked round.

"What's *breath* mean?" said Gannet. "In this. You keep saying it." He tapped the board. "*It answers a breath that is unbound.* What's a breath, when it's at home? I've got it down. I don't know what it's worth."

---

Idony was pleased.

He couldn't help it. Somebody was asking. Nobody had asked him anything properly since the tribunes' boy at Liss's table. He got up, slowly, and went across the hall to the columns, and sat down on the grass beside the bosun, and explained.

"The Deep," he said. "What everyone lives on. What Kessmoor calls the Bleed and Hearthmere called the crown's gift and the Wyldmarch calls the Mother's milk. The thing every word works on, every charm, every lamp."

"I know the Deep."

"It's a breath," said Idony. "Theirs. The six." He nodded at the light. "They were laid down asleep under the temples. A thousand years. Sleeping things breathe. Slowly. And their breath came up out of the ground, and the world lived on it." He spread his hands. "That's the Deep. The sleepers' breath."

---

Gannet wrote.

His lips not moving. His pencil going.

"And *unbound*?"

"Nothing holding it," said Idony. "No chain. No body." He pointed at the Lamp. "Like that."

Gannet wrote it.

Idony could see the board. Gannet didn't hide it. In a careful hand, squarer than Idony would have thought, under the line copied off the light: *breath = the Deep. The sleepers' breathing.* And under that: *unbound = nothing holding it.*

"Thank you, master," said Gannet.

"Not at all," said Idony. He was rather touched.

---

The Mother wrote in the afternoon.

Not the Lamp. Her. The grey light, slow, coming up in the air over the pool from where her great head lay on the floor. Grey letters. Heavier than the Lamp's. As if each one had been carried there.

They weren't for the farmer and the knight.

The farmer and the knight weren't holding hands. They were on the pool's edge apart. And still the grey light gathered and made letters, and Maren, who had the lion's meaning, looked up and then at Idony, and said, "It's for you."

---

He read it.

"*Small one,*" he read.

He stopped. He'd never been called that. Not even by his grandmother.

"*Your hands.*"

---

He looked at them.

In his lap. Steady. An old man's hands, with the knuckles big and the skin thin and spotted. They'd been steady since the hill. Since the far-stone, a little; since the shore, more; since the hill, all the way. He'd looked at them every morning and not told anyone.

They'd shake again. On the ship. On dead ground. At Liss's table. They'd shake as they'd shaken since the cellars, since he'd held a Titan still with his hands for the length of a breath while seventy people got out of the rock above.

He knew that.

He read the next line.

"*There is a little left in me. I can mend what you burned.*"

---

He sat very still.

He thought about it. He let himself. For a long breath, sitting on the hall's floor with the grey letters hanging over the water, he let himself think about it.

His hands. Steady. Always. A pen in them. His own hand, on paper, his own notes in his own writing, not Liss's round rope-tied letters or Davin's quick clerk's ones or Maren's square soldier's. Writing as fast as he thought, as he used to.

And the craft. *Hold.* The words that were only words to him now. Words that worked.

He let himself think about it for one breath.

Then he looked at the lion.

---

Lying by his mother's head. Against her great grey jaw, with his eyes shut. Small. The lion who'd carried him over half the world on his back with a strap Liss made. Who'd lain on far-stone in a ship's hold for a month with Idony asleep against his shoulder.

*To the lion,* the Lamp had written. *Two of us will give what is left, and go out.*

Everything she had. For him.

---

"No," said Idony.

He said it aloud. To the grey letters. To the great head on the floor.

"Thank you," he said. "No."

Maren looked at him.

"Every scrap of you goes to him," said Idony. To the Mother. "All of it. He'll need all of it. I don't know what for yet but he will." He looked at his steady hands. "I've only to read. I can read with my hands shaking. I've done it a year." He smiled. "I'm quite good at it now."

---

The grey letters faded.

The light over the pool sank. The Mother's great head lay on the floor. Her eye, the one he could see, the size of a cartwheel, gold, was open. On him.

"She's glad," said Maren. Quietly. From the pool's edge. "Through him. She's glad."

Idony didn't believe in gods.

He found he was glad she was glad.

---

The horse came at dusk.

The little grey one. That had come down the beach to him and let him on its back and carried him up the road to the hill, and then here. It had stayed with him. All three days, grazing outside the western columns on the ridge's grass, never far. Coming when he went out to it. Standing by him.

At dusk it came in.

Between the columns. Onto the grey floor of the hall. Its small hooves going *tock* on the paving like the clock's pendulum at Ironhythe. Past Gannet. Past the farmer's dragons, looking in. To where he sat against his column.

It stood by him.

It put its head against his chest.

---

He knew what it was.

He'd never had it. He'd seen it. In the farmer, arguing aloud with three dragons in a yard. In Maren's face when she talked to the lion. In Liss's stories of Maren's father, who'd died in a week when his hound died.

A choosing. Beginning.

The little wild horse that had never seen a person in its life, with its head against his chest, and its breath going in and out against his blanket, warm, choosing him.

---

He put his steady hand on its neck.

He held it there a while.

Then he took it away.

"No," he said. Gently. To the horse. "Go back to your herd."

It didn't move.

"I've seen what it costs," said Idony. He put his hand flat on its face, between its eyes, and pushed, very lightly. "I've seen it all my life. In other people. One life between two." He thought of Maren's line, at the pool, in the night: *a house with one door, and somebody always standing in it.* "I'm too old to be anyone's door."

He pushed again.

"Go on."

---

It went.

Slowly. Backing off a step. Then turning. Its hooves going *tock, tock* across the paving between the columns, out onto the grass. It stood there a while in the dusk, looking back at him.

Then it went away along the ridge toward the dunes, where the herd was.

He watched it till he couldn't see it.

He stayed unbonded. He'd chosen that too.

---

Maren was at the Mother's head that night.

Kneeling. On the floor of the hall, by the pool, in the low grey light. With the lion's great head lying in the crook of her one arm. His eyes shut. Her face close to his.

Idony was against his column. Awake. He was always awake.

He heard her.

---

"I'm not afraid," she said.

Aloud. Very low. To the lion. Her right hand on his mane, stroking, slowly, as you'd stroke a horse in a thunderstorm.

"I'm not afraid," she said again. "Whatever she gives you. Whatever you are after. I'm not afraid."

The lion didn't move.

Idony sat against the column with his steady hands in his lap and listened to her say it a third time, and knew she was lying.

So did the lion. He could see it in how still the lion lay.

Neither of them said so.
