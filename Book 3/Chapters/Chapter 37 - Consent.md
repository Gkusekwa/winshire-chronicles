---

He'd gone at dawn on the hundred-and-forty-fifth.

The farmer. With his three. To the lagoon first, for the boy, and then back to the crossroads. She'd stood under the arch with the lock's sentence in light over her head and watched him walk out between the western columns to the red one on the grass.

He'd stopped at the columns. Turned.

"Two hands," he'd said. Cracked.

"I know," she'd said.

That was all. He'd got up on the red one and they'd gone up off the ridge, three of them, slow, and away north-east over the green country, toward the lagoon.

---

The Lamp and the Lady went at dusk.

To take a shape, the old man said. The Lamp walked off the ridge's east end into the last of the light and went with it. The Lady sank down the pool and away along the channel under the floor, the pale light going out of the water a little at a time, like a tide.

Then there were the three of them on the ridge. And the Mother.

And Idony. And Gannet, at his columns.

---

The pool was dark that night.

No glow under the water. The Lady had taken hers away down the channel, and the pool lay black and flat to its edges, and the only light in the hall was the Mother's, grey and low, round her great head on the paving. Orrun lay against her jaw. Maren sat with her back to his side.

She was pacing him. In her head. Thirty-eight, nose to tail. She'd walked it that morning on the grass and got thirty-eight again, and stood at the end of his tail a while after with nothing to do with her feet.

*You're counting,* said Orrun.

*Yes.*

*What?*

*You.*

He thought about that. His side went up and down against her back, slow, as the river leans on a moored boat and lets it go.

*After,* he said. *Will you count me after?*

*If I can.*

*And if you can't?*

She didn't have an answer. She'd stood night watches on the river at Greyhold with nothing coming, and known what was out there by the sound of the water on the piles. She listened to him breathe. That was all she had.

*Then I'll tell you,* said Orrun. *How big. So you have it.*

She leaned her head back against him.

Across the dark floor the Mother's eyes were open. Gold. On both of them. They didn't close all night.

---

The Mother got up at dawn on the hundred-and-forty-sixth.

Out of the hall. Her head drew back between the columns, slowly, off the floor where it had lain eight days, and she stood up on the grass outside, and came round the end of the hall onto the open ridge.

And lay down.

Facing Orrun.

---

Orrun had followed her out.

He stood on the ridge's grass with Maren beside him, small, both of them, in front of the great grey shape lying on the grass with her paws out and her head up. The sun was coming up behind the hall. It made the columns' shadows long across the grass.

The grey light gathered in the air between them.

Letters.

---

She couldn't read them. She felt them. Through him.

The old man, under the arch behind her, read them aloud anyway. For her. So she'd have the words as well as the meaning.

"*Lion's knight.*"

---

"*He is yours,*" read Idony. "*You are his.*"

"*One life.*"

The letters held. Grey. Heavy. As if each had been carried.

"*What I give him goes through you.*"

A long pause.

"*Will you let it?*"

---

She understood.

All of it, at once, as she understood things through him, not in words. It was the bond. One life between two. What the Mother had, whatever she had left, the whole of what a Titan was when it had stopped, couldn't go into Orrun except through the place where he and Maren were one thing.

Through her.

If she said no, it couldn't go.

If she said yes, it would go through her. And change her. And change him more.

---

She thought about Hollin Knowe.

She hadn't in a long time. The edge of the Brackenwood. The bond-forcers with their smoke and their collar and their binding-lamp, and the lion in the middle of it all, held, going empty. Going *mine, mine, mine.*

And she'd walked into it.

Into the binding. She'd said *take me instead.* She'd offered her own life into the thing they were doing to him, in his place. And the lion had looked at her through the smoke and accepted.

And the bond had formed. And she'd heard his name.

---

A binding took.

It didn't ask. It came round you like smoke and took what it wanted and called it yours.

This asked.

The great grey shape on the grass, the mother of the old lions, a Titan who'd stopped, a god in every chapel in Hearthmere, lay with her paws out and her head up and waited for a knight with a dead arm and half a mouth to say whether she might give her son what she had left.

---

"Yes," said Maren.

Aloud. On the ridge. In the sunrise.

Then, to him. In the place where they talked.

*Yes.*

---

*Are you afraid?* said Orrun.

She'd lied to him the night before the farmer went. Three times. With her arm round his head, by his mother's head, in the low light. *I'm not afraid.* He'd known. She'd known he'd known.

*Yes,* she said.

He was quiet.

*So am I,* said Orrun.

---

The Mother put her head down.

Slowly. To the grass. Her great grey jaw on the ground in front of Orrun's paws. Close. Her gold eyes on him.

And the light went out of her.

Into the ground.

---

She felt it go.

Through her boots, first. The ridge's stone under the grass. A warmth going into it, from the great jaw on the ground, outward, spreading, as water spreads through dry earth. Then through the stone to Orrun's paws.

Then up.

Into him.

And through him, into her.

---

Cold.

That was what came into her. Cold. Down. Into her bones. All of them. The right side, that she could feel, and the left, that she couldn't; she felt it in the bones of her left arm that she hadn't felt in sixty days, and the bones of her neck, and her jaw, and her ribs. A cold like deep stone. Like the stone under the Ruin. Like the stone a long way down where nothing had ever been warm.

It went into her bones and stayed.

---

And into him, stone.

She saw it come.

Out of the ridge. Out of the ground under the grass, and the paving of the road, and the columns of the hall behind her. Grey. Flowing. Up into Orrun, as water goes up a wick. The ridge's stone running up into his paws, up his legs, into his body, grey, steady, as if he were drinking the hill.

He grew.

---

The size of a barn first. She'd known him that size. Then bigger. A house. Bigger. The hall: he was the hall's height, standing beside it on the ridge, his shoulder at the columns' tops. Bigger.

She was on the grass between his front paws. She looked up.

He went on.

The size of a hill. The size of the hill his mother had lain on. Grey. Grey as old stone. Grey as the Ruin's arch, the milestones, the Mother. His mane going like hers: not hair now. Something like dust, or mist, or smoke from a fire that's nearly out. Moving round his head and shoulders against the morning sky.

---

The ground moved for him.

She felt it. Under her, between his paws. The ridge's stone, flowing round his paws, as water flows round a stone in a river. As if the whole of the far shore's stone had become, for him, something that would go where he wanted it to.

She'd pulled stone through herself all her life since the island. Lintels. Ridges. A chapel out of the sea.

This didn't need pulling.

It came.

---

His eyes.

He lowered his head. Down. To her. A head the size of a house, grey, with that mane of mist round it, coming down out of the sky to the grass where she stood between his paws.

And his eyes were pale.

Not gold. They'd been gold since the island. Lion's eyes. Now they were the colour of the sky at dawn. Pale. Clear. Very old.

He had no wings.

She looked. The thing under Greyhold had wings. Stone and leather, great, folded on its back. She'd seen them open at the Ruin when it went out of its chains.

He had none.

---

The Mother was going.

Maren saw it over his paw. The great grey shape on the grass, with her jaw on the ground. Thinning. The grey going pale. As the old ones had gone thin on the black plain in the showing, pouring themselves into the sleeping things. Pale. Light coming through her.

Her mane went first. Into light. Then her body. Then her paws.

Her eyes went last.

They stayed on Orrun until the end. Gold. On her son, the size of a hill now, standing over her with his pale eyes.

Then they went too.

---

Like a lamp.

That was all. A light going out. The great shape on the grass was light, and then it was less, and then it wasn't, and there was grass where she'd lain, flattened in the shape of her, and nothing on it.

---

Orrun put his head down.

All the way. Out of the sky, past Maren, to the grass where his mother had been. A head the size of a house, laid on a flattened place the size of a field. He breathed in. She felt it go into him through the bond, long and slow: dust, and stone that has had the sun on it all day, and the light's warm smell that had been on everything for twenty-one days.

He breathed in again. There was less of it.

She counted his breaths. It was something to do with them. Nine. Ten. On the eleventh there was nothing on the grass for him but grass, and he breathed it in anyway, with his pale eyes shut.

---

Through the bond she felt the place in him.

Low down, under everything. Where something as warm as he was had lain against him since the hill. She'd stood in it once and had to walk to the edge of the hill-top with her back to it.

It was a hollow now. The shape of a paw.

She stood between his paws and didn't touch him. There was nothing of him small enough to put a hand on.

---

And the hall went quiet.

---

She heard it go.

Felt it. The hum. In the columns and the paving and the ridge's stone. That had been there since she landed on this shore, in everything, in her teeth, in her boots. It stopped. In the hall. All at once. As a bell stops when someone puts a hand on it.

The columns stood on the ridge, grey, in the sunrise, quiet.

Only one thing on the ridge hummed now.

Him.

---

She waited.

Between his paws, on the grass, with the cold in her bones and her face lifted to his. She waited for the thing under Greyhold. For the rage. For the drinking. For a hill with pale eyes to look down at her and not know her.

He looked down at her.

*Maren,* he said.

---

The same voice.

Deeper. Slower. Like stone settling, a long way down, at the bottom of a well. But his. The same. The voice that had said *you're walking strangely* on the long walk west with the clans and *I like it dark* in the inlet and *it's like standing on a coin in a river* on the ballast.

No rage in it. None. Not anywhere.

*I'm here,* said Orrun. *I'm still here.*

---

She wept.

She didn't know she was doing it. She couldn't feel it. Not on the left side, which had been dead since the reef. Not on the right, now, either: the cold had come into her face, and her skin was cold, and the tears ran down it and she couldn't feel them go.

She knew because she couldn't see.

She stood between his paws with her face up and the tears running and put her right hand flat on the great grey paw beside her, and couldn't feel that either, and left it there.

*You were afraid,* he said.

*Yes.*

*So was I,* said Orrun. *I'm not now.*

---

He tried the shadows at noon.

He didn't ask her. He just did it. She felt him do it, through the bond, and the ridge.

One rose out of the stone beside him. Grey. A lion.

As big as he was.

---

She looked at it. The shadow. Standing on the ridge beside him. A hill-sized lion of grey stone with pale eyes, standing in the noon sun, as big as he was, exactly.

Then another. On his other side.

Then a third.

She felt each one. Through him. Not as a shadow. Not as a thing he was making and holding. As him. Each one, all of him, the same, standing on the ridge in the sun.

*They're me,* said Orrun. *All of them. Now.*

---

She watched them.

She'd counted everything her whole life. Here was a number standing on the ridge in the sun, and she let it stand.

He let them go. Back into the ridge. The stone closed over them as water closes over something dropped into it. Gone.

He stood alone on the ridge again. One hill. Grey. Pale-eyed. With the hall's quiet columns at his shoulder like a fence.

---

The old man had watched it all from the arch.

He couldn't read anything in it. There'd been no letters. He'd stood under the arch with his steady hands at his sides and watched the Mother go out and the lion become a hill, and when Maren came back to him across the grass at last he was crying.

"I couldn't read it," he said. "There wasn't anything to read." He wiped his face. "I'm crying anyway. I don't know why."

"Nor do I," said Maren.

---

Gannet hadn't written anything.

She noticed it. He was at his columns, on the grass, with his board on his knee and his pencil in his fingers. The board was blank. He'd sat there all morning and watched, and not written a word.

He saw her looking.

For once, the smile didn't come first. It didn't come at all.

---

The grey at her temple came in the afternoon.

She found it by the pool. Looking at the water. The pool was clear and empty now, the Lady gone, the bottom grey stone a man's height down. She looked into it and saw her face.

The white lock over her left ear had gone. It had spread. A band of grey now, over the ear and up across the temple, iron-grey, like Rowan's. Like an old knight's.

She put her hand to it. She couldn't feel her hand, or the hair. She knew it was there.

She was cold. She'd be cold, she thought, for the rest of her life.

She was.

---

The screaming came at dawn on the hundred-and-forty-seventh.

Through the ground.

She was asleep. Against his paw, in the grass, with him awake above her. She'd slept. He'd watched. And she woke because he'd lifted his head.

*Listen,* he said.

---

She couldn't hear anything.

Then she could. Through him, through the stone. He could feel the stone of the whole far shore now, leagues of it, every hill and road and buried column, as she'd once felt the floor of a single room. And through it, far off, to the south-west, something.

Screaming.

---

Three voices.

High. Long. Torn. A long way off, through the ground, so far it came to her through him like a sound through a wall at night in an inn. Three. One deep. One high. One old, old, older than anything.

Not words. Pain. Or something past pain. Something being given that was too big for what it was going into.

*The farmer's,* said Orrun.

*The other gift,* said Maren.

She stood up. She stood on the ridge in the dawn with the cold in her bones, looking south-west, toward the crossroads, toward where the roads met, and listened through her lion to three dragons screaming, until they stopped.
