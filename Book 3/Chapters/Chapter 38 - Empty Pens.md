---

The pens were empty.

Gorm Askell's rider had come down from Varrhold on the hundred-and-thirty-ninth with spare harness and the news, and Arn had read Gorm's sour cramped hand by the fire on the coast that night and felt nothing much, and then later something.

*The pens stand empty. Half the freed stay on the ledges and the crags round the hold; they come to the mutton barrows and eat and go up again. Half have gone to the high glens above Coldtarn and not come down. Leir walks the pens every morning anyway. He says somebody should. The damp is in the oldest ledgers. G.A.*

Leir walked the empty pens every morning.

Arn folded the letter and put it in his coat.

---

The order had come from Rowan the day before.

By the Accord's courier, from the camp behind the Wend. The coast to be emptied inland. All of it that would come. The Corsa terrace. The salt-villages on the Saltings. The hamlets under the Ashlings' scrub. Wendmouth's outskirts, if Wendmouth would let them. Behind the Wend, to the Marches, to the camp.

At the bottom, in her plain square hand:

*Hold people. Not ground.*

---

He had the coast.

Since the farmer gave it to him on the black headland above Blackwater and went out over the sea with his three. *It's not mine either. It's Brede's and Dorcas Kell's and Hessa Pane's and two thousand people's on a hill.*

Somebody had to fly over it.

He flew over it.

---

He had Brede's forty pairs, that had been Ulric's. He had his own twenty, that had come down with him from Varrhold. And he had thirty more he hadn't brought.

The freed.

Out of the pens. He'd opened the gates on the seventy-ninth and they'd gone everywhere, and some of them had come to the coast. Thirty. Following Ildra. Nobody had asked them. They'd come down the Scarfell a day behind the twenty and flown along the coast after her, loose, all over the sky, not in pairs, not in lines, as the freed flew.

They'd carried nothing at first.

Then on the third day one of them, a young grey with nobody on its back, had come down beside a pair loading baskets on the Saltings and stood there. And when the pair went up, the grey had taken the basket's other rope in its jaws and gone up too.

---

The baskets were the Gullhaven baskets.

War-harness. Wicker and rope and leather, twenty people to a pair, as the Clan had flown them off the marked roofs at Gullhaven at the end of last winter. Inland. Over the Ashlings' grey-green scrub to Cotter's Cross on the Way, and the Wend's crossing at the Staithe, and down, and back.

Over and over.

The old first. Then the sick. Then the children. Then anyone.

---

The terrace went by cart.

The hulk-folk wouldn't fly. Dorcas Kell said so on the hundred-and-forty-first, on the *Merry Agnes*'s rail, with her arms folded over the scars. Two thousand people who'd been lifted onto a hill in their own hulls for one copper weren't getting into a basket under a dragon to be carried off it.

"We'll walk," said Dorcas. "The Track goes inland. We've carts. We've Patience." She looked at the blind stonehorn grazing among the hulks' cradles. "We've him."

She banged her pan.

The stonehorn lifted its white eyes and came.

---

The hulks stayed.

On their cradles on the ropewalk terrace above the wave-mark, forty-one of them, empty, with the washing taken down off their rigging and their deck-fires out. The carts went up the Track in a line a league long with the hulk-folk walking beside them. Patience at the head. The blind stonehorn behind her, with a rope on its plates and a child on the rope's end leading it.

Arn flew over them all the first day.

At dusk he looked back from Ildra's neck at the terrace. Forty-one dead ships on a hill, upright, empty, in the evening light.

---

The wyverns took the coves.

Nell Tallis's company first. Five, black-green, hooded on the quay, unhooded in the air. And two more companies she'd brought: Black Ruth's, three wyverns out of the Saltings' back-channels, run by a woman with one eye and a laugh like a gull; and the Crane brothers', four, two brothers who looked like the same man twice and never spoke to each other.

Twelve wyverns. Under the Accord's warrant.

Rowan's seal. At cost. Nell had shown him the paper. *The war's necessary costs.* The same words, she said, as the lion's knight had put on Ostrey's debt at Blackwater. The whole coast had heard about that.

---

The coves were under the Ashlings' cliffs.

Little ones. Narrow. Where a fishing hamlet sat on a ledge at a cliff's foot with its boats pulled up below and no way out but the sea or a goat-path. A dragon's wingspan wouldn't go into them. Kharrak's wouldn't have gone into the biggest. Even Ildra's, small as she was, scraped.

The wyverns went in.

Small. Quick. Barbed tails curled. A basket of four slung under each. Into the coves, down onto the ledges, four people, up, out over the sea, inland.

He watched Nell's lead wyvern, Sable, go into a cove no wider than a barn door and come out with four children and a grandmother in a basket under her, and turn inland, and he thought about what Ulric would have said.

Something rude, about wyverns.

Then he'd have watched it again.

---

The rocs came on the hundred-and-forty-third.

Out of the east. Along the coast. Low over the sea.

He saw them from the cliffs above the Saltings and didn't know what they were. Four shapes. Birds. Too big to be birds. Rust-brown and white, with wings so wide that when the first of them banked over the salt-flats its shadow went across a whole village.

The pairs on the flats stopped loading and looked up.

Some of the dragons hissed.

---

They came down on the cliff-top beside him.

Four of them. On the short turf above the Saltings, folding wings longer than a barn, each one. Rust-brown backs, white breasts, white heads with hooked beaks the size of a man's chest, and eyes like a hawk's, gold, fierce, looking at Ildra.

Ildra looked back. Very still.

*Big,* she said. In him. Wary.

---

On each roc, a rider.

Lean. Silent. In grey-brown felt, with their hair cut short and their faces burned dark by a sun he'd never stood under. Horse-clan people. From the Plains. He'd never seen one up close.

The first one got down.

A woman. Forty, perhaps. With a scar across both her lips, old and white, that pulled her mouth a little to one side.

"Yeva," she said. "Of Tova's clan."

"Arn Varr."

"I know," said Yeva. "Tova sent us. She heard about the coast. At the Marshal's camp." She looked down at the Saltings. At the salt-villages on the flats, and the mud between them, and the children. "She said you'd need more sky."

---

He watched a roc lift a child.

That afternoon. On the flats below the salt-villages, where the children had been sent out onto the mud to be clear of the houses while the baskets came and went. A girl of six or seven, alone, on a hummock of salt-grass, with a bundle.

Yeva's roc came down over her.

Its talons, each one longer than the girl's arm, hooked and black. Opening. Coming down round her, slowly, very slowly, one on each side, as you'd put your hands round a bird that had come into the house. Closing. Not on her. Round her. Round the bundle and the girl both, a cage of talons.

And lifting.

---

The girl didn't scream.

He'd have screamed. He was a grown man and the Clan's chief's son and he'd flown since he was five, and he'd have screamed. She didn't. She sat in the roc's talons with her bundle in her lap and her mouth open, looking down at the mud going away under her.

The roc went up. And inland. Slow, steady, its great wings going *whump, whump* over the Ashlings, with the child held under it as gently as a yearling elk lifted off the Plains.

Every Clan rider on the flats stood and watched it go.

Nobody had ever seen one. Not in the Clan's whole memory. Rocs were a story. Horse-clan nonsense.

---

"They're careful with calves," said Yeva.

Beside him, on the cliff. She'd watched it go too.

"They lift elk," she said. "On the Plains. The young ones. They don't kill them in the air. They take them home whole to the nest." She looked at him sideways, with her pulled mouth. "They don't eat these ones."

"Good," said Arn.

"Usually," said Yeva.

He looked at her. She didn't smile, exactly. The scar did something.

---

The surf came on the hundred-and-forty-fifth.

No storm. That was the thing. A calm sea, a blue sky, a light wind off the land, and the pairs low over the Saltings' outer spit with full baskets, coming in.

And out of the calm, off the open sea, a swell.

Long. Smooth. Coming in toward the spit from the south, from nowhere. Then another behind it. Higher. Then a third.

---

He saw it from Ildra.

He was over the inner flats. He saw the first swell come in and break on the spit, white, much higher than it should have been, and run up over the outer flats in a sheet. And the second behind it, higher, breaking further in. The pairs over the spit lifting. Going up. Climbing with their baskets.

Not all of them.

---

Two pairs were too low.

Ivo Brack, red-thread, from Skarrow, on his grey. And a grey-thread girl, Sunniva, seventeen, on her young red, the first year she'd flown the baskets. Low over the outer spit, coming in slow with the last of the Saltings' old folk, nine between the two baskets, and still too low because the baskets were wet and heavy.

The third swell came up under them.

He watched it. Higher than the first two. Glass-green, then white. It came up under Ivo's grey and took the basket, and the grey went down with the basket's weight, wings beating, into the white.

And Sunniva's red beside him.

---

He didn't see them come up.

Nobody did. The swell went over the spit and up the flats almost to the villages and drew back, and where the two pairs had been there was white water and wreckage and nothing else.

Nine people in the baskets. Two riders. Two dragons.

He hung over the flats on Ildra and looked at the white water drawing back, and Ildra in him was making a sound he'd never heard her make.

*Far,* she said. *It's the bell one. A long way out. Moving. That's its wave.* She was shaking. *It didn't mean to. It doesn't mean anything.*

---

They looked for them till dark.

The pairs, the freed, the wyverns, the rocs. Over the spit and the flats and the open water beyond. They found the baskets. Two of the nine people, alive, clinging to one. The rest, nothing.

Not the riders. Not the dragons.

The Clan would pour for them at Coldtarn, when there was a Coldtarn to go back to. Without ashes. The old priest had words for that.

---

They offered him the chair that night.

At the coast camp. On the Ashlings' edge above the Saltings, where the scrub met the cliffs, under the stars, with the fires going along the cliff-top. Red and grey round them, mixed, as they'd been since the black water. The rocs on the turf, heads under their wings. The wyverns on their chains, hooded.

The riders came to his fire together.

Gunna with them. Old, scarred, her clay pipe in her teeth. And Bjarke.

---

Bjarke spoke.

He'd changed. Since the pens. Since Gorm's barrow. Since he'd sat down in the hall with the count read at him and not argued. He was still loud. He was loud now. But he stood at Arn's fire with his hands at his sides like a man with something to say that he'd decided to say.

"The Clan wants a chief," he said.

Arn didn't say anything.

"The chair's been empty since Teo burned," said Bjarke. "Under a cloth. Nobody in it. And you opened the pens, and you poured for Ulric, and you're the one on the coast with the old ones' wives in baskets." He looked at the fire. "Red and grey. We've talked. Sit in it."

---

Arn looked at the fire a long time.

He thought about the chair. Black oak. Dragons carved up its legs and arms, one across its back worn smooth by three hundred years of chiefs' shoulders. Under its grey cloth at the top of the hall between the two tables. His father's. His grandfather's.

He'd wanted it since he was eight.

He'd poured for his father on the black stone with Ulric's hand on the urn's other side and Teo standing behind them. He'd poured for Ulric with the farmer's bound hand on the other side.

---

"The chair's for whoever pours," he said.

Bjarke looked at him.

"You poured," said Bjarke.

"Two of us poured," said Arn.

---

They looked at each other. Red and grey round the fire. Working it out.

Two of us. Arn and his uncle, for Haldor. Arn and the farmer, for Ulric. Two hands. Always two. Never one.

Bjarke opened his mouth. Shut it.

Gunna laughed.

Short and sharp, round her pipe. Like a fox. As Hulda Sarr had laughed at the old one's joke in the hall in the spring. She took the pipe out of her mouth and laughed into the fire until her eyes ran.

"Oh, that's Teo's brother," she said. "To the bone."

They took it as a no.

---

The crying came on the hundred-and-forty-seventh.

An hour after dawn. He was on the cliff with Ildra, watching the first baskets go up off the flats.

And every dragon on the coast turned its head.

---

All at once. The pairs on the flats, mid-loading. The freed on the cliffs and in the air. Brede's old blue, going up with a basket. Ildra, beside him, under his hand.

South-west.

Toward the sea. Toward nothing.

And opened their mouths and cried.

---

Not the sound at Coldtarn. Not the pour-sound, the long low one that went into the rock. This was high. Torn. A sound like something being pulled out of them. Every dragon on the coast, near ninety of them, crying the same cry at the same moment toward the empty south-western sea.

The rocs woke and screamed back.

The wyverns pulled at their chains.

The people in the baskets put their hands over their ears.

---

Ildra was shaking under his hand.

*It hurts,* she said. In him. Small. *It's not ours.*

"Whose?"

*His three,* said Ildra. *The farmer's. Far. So far.* She cried again, with the rest, her head up. *Something's going into them. Something too big.* She shook. *It hurts them and it's not ours and we feel it anyway.*

He stood on the cliff with his hand flat on her neck and felt it through her. Faint. A pull. A tearing, very far off.

He held on.

It stopped.

---

All at once, as it had started.

Ninety dragons on the Saltreach coast closing their mouths, and putting their heads down, and standing very still, breathing.

Then going back to the baskets.

---

The coast was empty on the hundred-and-fiftieth.

To the Wend. All of it that would come. The terrace's carts on the Track, a day short of the Wend. The salt-villages on the Saltings, flown. The coves under the Ashlings, emptied by wyverns four at a time. The children off the flats, carried inland in roc's talons and set down on the Marches' grass beside the Accord's camp, where they ran about telling everyone.

Wendmouth wouldn't come. Blackwater wouldn't come.

The last baskets went over the Ashlings at dusk.

---

Arn flew the coast once more alone.

On Ildra, at dusk, along the whole of it. Over the Saltings, empty. Over the outer spit where the white water had been. Over the Ashlings' coves with their boats pulled up below and nobody in them. Over the ropewalk terrace at Corsa, forty-one dead ships on a hill.

*Empty,* said Ildra.

"Yes."

*Like the pens.*

He thought about that.

"No," he said. "The pens were full. With things that couldn't go." He looked down at the empty coast. "This is empty because they went."

She thought about it.

*Better,* she said.
