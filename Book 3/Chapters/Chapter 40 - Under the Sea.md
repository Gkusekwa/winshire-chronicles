---

He carried them down from the hall in a day.

Walking. On the road. At a hill's pace, which was faster than she'd have believed, because a hill's stride was a furlong. The road sang under him where his paws came down. The old man rode between his shoulders with her, wrapped in his blanket, holding her coat with his steady hands. Gannet behind them in the mane, clinging on, with his bag.

The deer came out of the hills and watched them pass and didn't run.

---

Kit rowed in at the lagoon's edge.

At dusk on the hundred-and-forty-seventh. The *Kittiwake* at anchor behind her, low in the water, with people on her deck. Many people. Maren saw them from the beach, standing along the rail in the dusk, in rotted good coats. The farmer's ninety-three.

Kit grounded the boat at the road's end and got out into the shallows and looked up at the lion.

Up. A long way.

She didn't say anything for a long time.

"Well," she said at last. "That won't go in the hold."

---

Maren closed the contract.

On the beach. At the road's end, by the milestone with no number and the grave beside it with the salt in it. With the old man beside her and Gannet behind and a lion the size of a hill sitting on the sand, his pale eyes on the sea.

"Passage there," said Maren. "You did that."

"Passage there and back," said Kit. "It says."

"I'm going back another road."

Kit looked at the lion.

"Then I'm releasing you," said Maren. "From the back half. You've done what you swore. More."

---

Kit had the dish.

She'd brought it in the boat. The chipped clay dish, with the grey salt in it, that she'd refilled from Tansy's sack after she poured the first lot into Sim's grave. She held it out on the flat of her hand.

She took one grain on her fingertip and touched it to her tongue.

"Closed," she said.

Maren took a grain and touched it to hers. With her right hand. She couldn't taste it on the left side. She could on the right.

"Closed," she said.

---

Kit gave her things.

Out of the boat. A skin of water from the spring under the great steps. Ship's biscuit in a tin, against the damp. Salt pork in a cloth. She didn't ask where Maren was going or how. She looked at the lion again, and then at the sea, and seemed to work it out for herself, and didn't like it, and handed over the biscuit anyway.

And last, out of her coat, a twist of paper.

Small. Folded tight. Maren took it.

"Salt," said Kit. "From the dish." She looked at the knight's grey neck, her white-grey temple. "You'll want something to taste."

---

The old man stayed with the ketch.

He'd decided before Maren asked him. He said so on the beach. Ninety-three people aboard and seven crew and somebody had to read them the milestones on the way home, because Kit read them with the book and he read them faster. And there was a chart to finish. And he'd never been on a ship that was full.

"I'll be useful," he said. "It's so rare."

---

They said goodbye at the road's end.

She didn't know how. She never had. She stood in the shallows with the water round her boots and looked at him, small and grey in his blanket, with the sea-birds on his shoulders that had followed him from the hills and wouldn't leave.

"Count for me," said Idony.

"What?"

"Under there." He nodded at the sea. "Wherever he takes you. You always count. Count for me, and tell me when you come up how long it was." He smiled. "I'll want it for the chart."

"I will," said Maren.

---

He walked into the sea at dusk on the hundred-and-forty-eighth.

She was on his back. Between his shoulders. In his mane, which was mist and dust and smoke now and held her anyway. The skin of water across her. The tin of biscuit in her coat beside the dead arm. Kit's twist of paper in her right hand.

He walked into the lagoon.

Out across it. The water at his knees. His chest. His shoulders. Toward the shelf, and the last pier standing whole at its edge, and the Needle beside it, and the black water beyond.

At the shelf, he went down.

---

She held her breath.

She didn't need to. She found that out after a moment, with her eyes shut and her chest bursting. The water had come up over his shoulders and his neck and her, cold, black, and then it hadn't. There was stone round them instead.

She opened her eyes.

Dark. Nearly. And close round them, on every side, stone.

---

It had opened.

The sea-bed. In front of him. She felt it. Through him. The stone of the shelf, the old road's drowned causeway, under the sea, parting. Ahead of him. Opening out of his way as water opens round a boat's bow. A space. His size. Round him. Moving.

And behind them, closing. She felt that too. The stone coming together again where he'd passed, softly, as the sea closes over a stone dropped in it.

She'd seen this before.

Under the Ruin. The night they came out. The night the Titan went out of its chains, and she and the farmer and the lion had come up through the rock under the river, and the rock had opened in front of Orrun and closed behind, and they'd come up in the reeds below Redwatch at dawn.

He'd been the size of a cart then.

He was the size of a hill.

---

There was air.

Cold. Thin. Smelling of stone. Coming with them, in the space that moved.

*Where's it from?* she said.

*The stone remembers,* said Orrun. *The air that was over it. Before the sea came. It kept a little.* He walked. *I'm letting it out as I go.*

She breathed it. It was the oldest air she'd ever breathed. It tasted of nothing.

---

It was dark.

Not quite. His eyes. Pale, the colour of a dawn sky, and they gave a little light, very faint, on the stone in front of them. She could see the walls of the space as it opened. Grey. Faced. Cut.

The road.

He was walking along it. Under the sea. Along the causeway itself, the drowned road, inside it. She could see the cut faces of its stones go by in the faint pale light, and the joints between them, and once, a long way down the wall, the hooked letters. *Into the light.*

---

He felt the piers.

Overhead. Through the sea-bed. Each one, standing up out of the road above them, going up through the water to the air, with its milestone on its top. He felt them pass.

*One,* he said, the first night.

She didn't know if it was night.

*One,* he said. *Going the other way now. Counting up.* He walked. *Toward home.*

---

The stone wept.

She found it on what she thought was the second day. Water. On the walls of the space, where he passed. Drops, coming out of the stone and running down. Fresh. Not salt. She put her tongue to a wall and it was cold and sweet, like the spring under the great steps.

She filled the skin from the walls. She did that every day after.

*The road knows it's a road,* said Orrun. *Roads have wells.*

---

She ate little.

A biscuit in the morning, if it was morning. A piece of pork. She was cold. She'd been cold since the ridge, to the bone, and the cold took the hunger with it. She ate because she had to. She chewed in the dark on the side of her mouth that worked.

She slept in his mane.

Between his shoulders. In the mist and dust that held her. She slept a great deal. More than she'd slept since the island. He didn't sleep. She felt him not sleep. He walked.

---

She counted.

At first by sleeps. She slept, and woke, and that was a day. She did that eight times. Nine.

Then she wasn't sure if she'd slept twice between two meals or once. So she counted by the biscuit. There'd been thirty-two in the tin. She'd eaten so many. That was so many days.

Then she found she'd eaten some in the dark half asleep and not counted them.

So she counted by the drops. The skin. How many times she'd filled it.

Then she lost that.

---

She tried.

She lay in his mane with her eyes open on the faint pale light on the stone and tried. *The twelfth day. Or the thirteenth. Fourteen sleeps. Twenty biscuits. Twelve.* She'd counted things all her life. Steps on the river watch. Strokes on the practice ground. Breaths in a held breath. Links in a chain. Piers. Light.

She'd counted the farmer's dragons over the Ysse from Lisska's echo. She'd counted the dead at Tova's fire on Tova's fingers. She'd counted the Grey Mother's breath on a hill-top at the end of the world, sixty in, sixty out.

She didn't know what day it was.

---

On what might have been the twentieth day she stopped trying.

She didn't decide to. She woke in his mane in the dark and reached for the count and it wasn't there. There was nothing to reach for. A space where it had been, like the space in the stone that moved with them.

She lay there.

She'd never not known the count. Not once. Not since she was a child on the floor by her father's chair, counting the days he'd been sitting there without getting up.

*Seven,* she'd counted. *Seven.*

She didn't know now.

---

*I promised him,* she said.

*Who?*

*Idony. I said I'd count.*

Orrun walked.

*I'll tell him,* he said. *How long. I know.*

*Do you?*

*No,* said Orrun. *But I'll tell him something.*

She laughed. In the dark, in his mane, under the sea, on the side of her mouth that worked. The first time since the ridge.

---

He'd stopped saying the piers.

She noticed it after. He'd said them for days. *Twenty-eight. Twenty-nine. Thirty.* Counting up. And she'd held on to his count when she lost hers. And then at some point he'd stopped, and she hadn't noticed for a long time, and when she did she didn't ask.

She thought he'd stopped because she'd stopped.

She didn't ask.

---

The Lady was there.

She saw her first on what she thought was the fifth day. Through him. Not through her eyes: there was nothing for her eyes but stone. Through him. He could see through stone now. He could feel the whole sea-bed round them, the road, the reefs, the deep black water over everything.

And in the black water, beside them, a light.

Pale. Cold. The colour of the moon on frost. Long. Coiling. Keeping pace.

---

*Her,* said Orrun.

*The Lady?*

*Yes.* He walked. *She's going the same way. Home. To fight.* He was quiet. *She's keeping us company. I think.*

The pale light kept pace for days.

Then it wasn't there. Then it was again. Coiling, far off in the black, out beyond the stone walls of the road, as a heron flies beside a boat on a river, sometimes close, sometimes far, sometimes gone round a bend and back.

She looked for it every time she woke. Through him.

Mostly it was there.

---

She opened the salt once.

Kit's twist of paper. In the dark. On a day she didn't know the number of. She unfolded it with her right hand and her teeth, carefully, and took one grain, by feel, and touched it to her tongue.

Salt.

The only taste in the world. Grey and sharp and cold. She held it on her tongue till it was gone.

*Salt between us.*

She folded the paper again and put it back in her coat.

---

He began to climb.

She felt it. In his stride. The road going up. The stone in front of them opening not ahead now but ahead and up, a slope, and the air coming with them getting warmer. Less old. And the stone of the walls getting lighter.

Grey. Then paler.

Then there was light that wasn't his eyes.

---

Water first.

The stone opened ahead and up and there was water in it. Green. With light coming down through it from above. A long way above. She held her breath and this time she needed to. The water came round them and they went up through it, him climbing, her holding on, the light getting brighter, greener, warmer.

And then air.

---

Grey air. Morning. Wind.

He came up out of the sea onto rock.

Black rock. Wet. Low tide. Weed everywhere, and pools, and the sea draining off the rock in a hundred streams. And round them, standing up out of the rock, columns.

Broken. Grey. Old.

---

She knew them.

Not from seeing. From the farmer's letter, and Lisska's echo, and the old man's notes. A temple on a reef, two leagues off, or more, off a coast. Columns. A dome, broken in. And in the dome, once, a bell, old-realm bronze the size of a house, ringing as the water poured past it.

The Drowned Bell.

The dome had fallen. There was no bell. The Bell had taken its bell with it.

---

He stood on the reef.

A hill, on a reef, in the sea, with the columns of a broken temple round his paws like the stubble of a mown field. Water running off him. The mist of his mane drying in the wind.

She sat up between his shoulders.

North, low on the grey sea, the coast. Saltreach. Long, low, grey-green, folded. And on it, where the coast came nearest, a hill. Empty. With nothing on it.

Gullhaven had been there.

---

A fishing boat on the horizon.

The only one. A small brown sail, a long way off, between the reef and the coast. Standing still. As if whoever was in it had seen a hill come up out of the sea onto the Drowned Bell's reef at low tide, and stopped rowing, and was sitting there with their mouth open.

She looked at it a long time.

She didn't know what day it was.

---

Somebody told her later.

The fishing boat's man, as it happened. When he'd got his courage back, and rowed over, very slowly, and sat under the lion in his boat looking up, and asked her who she was. She told him. And she asked him the day.

He told her.

The hundred-and-eightieth.

---

Thirty-two days.

She thought about it, sitting on the lion on the reef with the fisherman looking up at her.

Thirty-two days in the dark under the sea, along a drowned road, with the stone opening in front and closing behind. And for the first time in her life she hadn't known.

She'd tell Idony.

*Thirty-two,* she'd say. *I didn't count it. Somebody told me.*

He'd write it on the chart anyway.
