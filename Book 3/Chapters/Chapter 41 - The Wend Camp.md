---

The camp was three leagues long.

On the Saltreach bank of the Wend. Between the river and the Way, in the Marches below Greyhold's fallen hill. Tents. Carts in lines. Pens of stonehorns and horses and cattle. Fires, a thousand of them at night, going away up the river-bank and down it as far as she could see from the barn's loft.

Smoke all day.

And the Wend between it and Hearthmere.

---

She'd put it there on purpose.

Running water. Every soldier knew it. A word said across a river didn't land. Nobody knew if a Titan cared about rivers. Nobody knew anything about Titans that she could use. But it was something. A wide brown river between ninety thousand people and the thing lying in the Mere.

It was what she had.

---

Sixty thousand from Hallowmere.

They'd come down the King's Road after the Downs, and crossed the Wend at Liss's ferry, and at the Staithe, and on the new pontoon bridge Kessmoor had laid below Greyhold in eleven days out of barges chained side by side. They'd been coming in for fifty days. They were still coming. Liss ran her ferry day and night and had stopped asking anyone for coppers.

Ten thousand clansfolk.

Tova had brought them out of the Greenwold on the hundred-and-twentieth, when the Titan lay down in the Mere and didn't get up and the oaks were too near it. Across the downs. Across the King's Road behind the last of the Hallowmere columns. Over the Wend at the Staithe, with their herds swimming. Their stonehorns grazed the water-meadows below the camp now and the Marches farmers came to look at them and went away shaking their heads.

And now the coast.

---

The coast came in by every road.

Arn's baskets over the Ashlings, set down on the camp's southern meadows. The wyverns' baskets of four. Children out of rocs' claws, running about telling everyone, until the whole camp knew what a roc was and half the camp's children wanted one.

And on the hundred-and-forty-eighth, down the Saltreach Track, the carts.

A league of them. The Corsa hulk-folk, walking. A red ox with one horn at their head. And behind the ox, on a rope, led by a child, a blind stonehorn with moss on its plates.

A big woman with scarred forearms banged a pan when they reached the camp's edge, and the stonehorn stopped.

---

She saw Tam at a camp forge on the hundred-and-forty-sixth.

He'd come down the Iron Road with Kessmoor's wagons. He was shoeing a horse at a field-forge by the pontoon, broad, burned, with his sister's eyes in his thin face. He looked up when she went by on her greyhart.

She nodded. He nodded back.

He went on shoeing the horse.

---

The dispatches came on the hundred-and-forty-fifth.

She read them at her table. A door on trestles in a barn at the camp's middle, with a lamp on it and Davin at the end of it with his writing-case, counting. She read every dispatch twice. Once for what it said and once for what it didn't.

---

*Vael.*

Nothing. That was the dispatch. Davin read it to her from the College's courier book: no owl from Vael since the hundred-and-twelfth day. None. The College had sent out forty owls in the spring. None had come back since the hundred-and-twelfth.

And one bird. A Warden's, a snow-hawk, that had come down into a farmyard on the Iron Fells' north side on the hundred-and-twentieth with a scrap on its leg in a Warden's hand.

*The coil is round Glasswater. The lake is ice. In summer.*

That was all.

Master Quell's chair at the Accord's table had been empty since Ironhythe.

---

*Solenne.*

In Lady Ismay's own hand. Upright, plain, a schoolroom hand. The Horned Sun had come up the Gilt again on the hundred-and-twentieth, out of the bay where it had risen last autumn. The low city had gone over the water to Sorrel Isle in the fishing boats in a night. The boy-king was with them. The tribunes. The olifants had swum.

*Solenne is empty. The Sun is on the Stair again. It sits there in the day and burns.*

*We are on the island. We have fish.*

---

*Kessmoor.*

In Kaye's quiet hand, by the Iron Road courier. She read it three times.

The Furnace had come down out of the Scald on the hundred-and-twenty-sixth, west along the Coldrun, toward the Gearmouth foundries at the river's mouth. The Assembly had met it there. Engines on rails. Bolt-throwers on the foundry walls. The chilled canals of the old cage, rebuilt. A day and a night.

It had turned.

Gone back east. To the Scald. Lain down in the glass.

*Kessmoor is the only kingdom to turn a Titan,* wrote Kaye. In those words.

---

She put it down.

Davin looked at her from the end of the table.

"It's true," she said. "I think."

"Kessmoor wouldn't write it if it wasn't," said Davin. "They'd measure it first."

She almost laughed.

---

The rest she knew.

The Antlered in the dead Thornwood. Tova's scouts had seen it: lying among the trees, which weren't trees any more, which were grey sticks on grey ground, with its great branching crown up among them like another forest.

The Bell on the sea. Somewhere. The coast had felt its surf.

And the Last Titan in the Mere. Where it had lain down on the ninety-fifth day, in the boiling water, with the steam going up. Not moving. The steam had stopped after three days. It hadn't.

Fifty days. It hadn't moved.

She didn't know what it was waiting for.

---

The council sat on the hundred-and-forty-seventh.

In the barn. Round the door on trestles. The Accord, what was left of it, and what had come.

---

Pike.

Down the Iron Road on the hundred-and-forty-sixth with twenty wagons of iron and two lent engines, and Strake beside her with her burned hands and her face shut. Pike sat at the table in her smoked spectacles and looked at everything as if she were measuring it.

Thale. For the crown. Old, thin, careful. The chain of office on his chest. He'd come over the pontoon in a carriage, the last of the council, and taken a farmhouse.

Tova. In her felt, with her cropped grey hair and her root-knotted hands flat on the door.

Gunna Tarne. For the Clan. Arn was on the coast. Gunna sat with her clay pipe unlit in her teeth and half her face scarred and looked at Pike as she'd look at a stoat in a henhouse.

---

Mattias. For Sarovel. The young tribune with ink on his fingers who'd said *and be paid as knights* at the round table. Lady Ismay was on the island. He'd come up the Sun Road with the last of Sarovel's hands who weren't at Ironhythe.

And Hal Rennick.

For the Bench. And for the Tide Council, which was him now, more or less. Celia Ostrey had sailed with her fleet past the Shatters, and Aubrey Strand with her; Rennick had turned his three black ships back at the fifth pier and brought word home on the hundred-and-thirtieth that the fleet had sailed on into the dark without him. Nothing since. The houses that hadn't sailed were in Blackwater selling things to each other.

Rennick sat at the end of the table with his boots on the floor, for once, and his gold ring in his ear, and didn't laugh.

---

Pike gave the report.

"The fallback is finished," she said.

She said it flatly. As she said everything. A measurement.

"At Ironhythe. On the hundred-and-forty-fourth. Twelve engines. The great chain. The hub." She didn't look at her papers. "It comes down in parts. By barge to the Coldrun's head and the Iron Road. It will be assembled where the Marshal says."

"To hold the Last Titan," said Thale.

"If the elders fail," said Pike.

---

Nobody said anything for a moment.

The elders. The whole camp had heard. A gull-hawk out of Blackwater, garbled, from a Saltreach captain who'd had it from somebody who'd seen a salvage ketch come home, or been told about one. Old gods. On the far shore. The lion's knight with them. Something about light.

Nobody knew what it meant. Kessmoor had already built for it failing.

---

Rowan spoke against it.

She'd known she would. She'd lain awake in the barn's loft the night before, thinking how.

"I've held a chain on that thing twice," she said. "For the crown. At the Ring, on six wains. Under Greyhold, on the drums." She looked at Thale. "It took the first one and the stakes and the stones and went where it was going. I let the second one go to get people off a hill."

She looked at Pike.

"Chains on a Titan are what started this," she said. "Three hundred years ago. Under Greyhold. The crown chained it, and fed its lamps on it, and it woke, and here we are." She put her hand flat on the door. "I'll not put another chain on it."

---

"You're making the thing that made it," said Tova.

Slowly. Weighing each word. Her hands flat on the table.

Pike looked at her through her spectacles.

"We're making the thing that held," said Pike. "Once. The only thing that has." She turned her head to the table. "Kessmoor's cage held the Furnace for forty years. Kessmoor's engines turned it at Gearmouth. Nobody else at this table has turned a Titan." She didn't raise her voice. "Without the engines, you have knights and stonehorns. With them, you have a fallback."

---

They voted.

Thale for. He said why: plainly, as he did everything. The crown had no grain, no iron and no army but what the Accord fed it. Kessmoor fed the Accord. "I'll not vote against the bread, Marshal. I'm sorry."

Mattias for. He looked unhappy about it. "Three thousand of Sarovel's hands eat Kessmoor's loaf every day at Ironhythe," he said. "And three thousand more on the island eat its fish money. I can't."

Rennick for. "Kessmoor pays," he said. "That's all I've got to say. Kessmoor pays."

Tova against.

Gunna didn't vote.

"I'll not vote on chains," she said, round her pipe. "Arn can. He opened the pens. He'll know." She looked at Pike. "I'd not trust it, myself. But I'm old and I don't trust much."

Rowan against.

Three to two. With Pike, who voted for her own engines, four.

---

Rowan looked at the door on its trestles a long time.

Kessmoor's leverage was plain. Pike didn't hide it. Engines or nothing. Bread or nothing. Iron or nothing. The Accord ate because Kessmoor fed it, and Kessmoor had built a thing, and the Accord was going to have it.

She could stand on her vote and lose the bread.

She could do the other thing.

---

"Then it's the Accord's fallback," she said.

They looked at her.

"Not Kessmoor's," said Rowan. "The Accord's. And it fires on one word only. Mine. The Marshal's. Nobody else's. Not the Assembly's. Not the Master Engineer's. Not the crown's." She looked at Pike. "If the elders fail, and the line breaks, and I say so. Then. Not before. Not on anybody else's say."

---

"Agreed," said Pike.

Before Rowan had finished saying it. She'd said *nobody else's* and Pike had said *agreed* over the end of it, at once, flat, as if she'd been waiting for it.

"It's a fallback, Marshal," said Pike. "It should fire on the commander's word. Of course. Write it so."

Davin wrote it.

In the council's minute. *The Accord's fallback, called by the hands the Bridle, shall fire on the word of the Marshal of the Accord and on no other.* Rowan read it over his shoulder. Then she took the seal out of her coat, the small brass one with the ring and the six lines, and pressed it into the wax at the foot.

---

She believed it.

She'd believed every seal she'd ever pressed. She'd believed the crown's on the outlaw's paper and Thale's on the road. A seal was a seal. A council that put its seal to a thing had bound itself, whatever it thought, and the binding didn't come off because the person who'd pressed it was afraid, or clever, or Kessmoor.

She put the seal back in her coat beside Davin's list of four hundred and twelve.

---

She asked Pike about Callis after.

Alone. In the barn, when the others had gone out into the dusk. Pike was rolling her papers. Strake was by the door with her burned hands folded.

Tobin's letter was on the table. It had come on the hundred-and-forty-second by Moss's courier. Short. Like Tobin. *Callis is in the works. Corvin's man. The one who opened the river gate. He's feeding the heartstone engines in the Ring Yard. Seventh engine. Night shift. I don't know who hired him.*

Rowan put her finger on it.

"Who hired him?" she said.

---

Pike had the answer ready.

Rowan saw that later. She didn't see it then. Pike looked at the letter for exactly as long as it took to read it, and then she looked up, and the answer was there, whole, in the same flat voice as the report.

"A Greyhold man," said Pike. "Deserter. He came up the Iron Road in the spring with the Hearthmere smiths' wagons asking for work. Arms broken, badly set. Couldn't lift a hammer. We took him for bread. We've taken hundreds." She put the papers under her arm. "He shovels coal into a box at night. He's no more Restoration than the ox that pulls the carts."

---

Strake, by the door, didn't look at anyone.

Rowan looked at Pike a long time.

She thought about the heartstone wagon on the Candle Downs, and the smell on her hands, and *cut before the Accord, Marshal*. She thought about four hundred and twelve names. She thought about twelve engines and a great chain coming down the Iron Road in parts, and ninety thousand people behind a river, and a thing lying in the Mere that hadn't moved in fifty days.

She needed the engines.

She needed them more than she needed to be right.

"Thank you, Master Engineer," she said.

---

She wrote to Tobin that night.

By Moss's courier, going back up the Iron Road in the morning. Short. Like him.

*Tobin.*

*Noted. Pike says he was hired for bread. Watch him.*

*R.*

She sealed it with her plain wax, not the Accord's. Her business, not the Accord's.

---

The mountains came at dusk on the hundred-and-forty-ninth.

She was on the barn's roof. She went up there at dusk most days, to see the camp's fires come on along the river. Wynn was below, with Ash and Sorrow.

The hounds began to howl.

---

She looked where they were looking.

South. Over the Saltings, a long way off, low on the southern sky where the coast was. Three shapes. Coming up out of the south. Over the sea, out of the dusk.

Clouds, she thought.

Then they moved against the wind.

---

They came on. North-east. Across the southern sky, high, slow, over the Marches, over the Saltreach bank, a long way west of the camp. Toward the Scarfell.

Huge.

She had no word for how huge. People in the camp were coming out of their tents to look and pointing and shouting. Somebody near the barn said *mountains*. Somebody said *the gods*. Somebody said *Titans*, and a woman screamed, and people began to run, and Wynn was shouting at them to stop.

They didn't come near the camp.

---

She had her glass on them.

She couldn't tell what they were. Too far. Too big. Wings, she thought. Wings like the sails of a fleet. Three. One deep red, like the inside of a forge. One bronze, catching the last of the sun. One smaller, green, a long way behind the other two, turning lazily, as if it had all the time in the world.

Red, she thought.

She'd seen a red one before. In the Crown's Square at Hallowmere, with a farmer on its neck. It had been the size of a hay-barn.

These were the size of hills.

---

They went over the Scarfell's edge into the dark.

North-east. Gone.

The camp stood about in the dusk for a long time, talking, and then went back to its fires.

Nobody knew whose they were.

She put her glass away.

---

The last of the coast came in on the hundred-and-fiftieth.

Davin counted them. Davin counted everything. He sat at the end of her table in the barn with his writing-case open and his ink-stained fingers going down the columns, and at midnight he looked up.

"Ninety-one thousand," he said. "Behind the Wend. Today."

She looked at the number.

Ninety-one thousand. Behind a river. With a Titan lying in the Mere on the other side of it, and a chain coming down the Iron Road in parts to hold it, on her word.

"Write it down," she said.

He'd already written it.
