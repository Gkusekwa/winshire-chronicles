---

He walked out of the sea at low tide on the hundred-and-eighty-first.

Off the reef. Across the shallows between the Drowned Bell's broken temple and the coast. A league and more of grey water with the bottom showing through it in places, sand and weed and rock, and his paws coming down in it and the water going round them in long slow rings.

The fisherman rowed ahead of them.

The whole way. In his little brown-sailed boat, rowing, looking back over his shoulder every third stroke at the hill walking behind him, as if he couldn't decide whether he was leading it or running from it.

His name was Saul Trent. He'd told her that much, and the day, and then not said anything else.

---

She rode on Orrun's head.

Between his ears. In the mist of his mane, that held her. It was the only place she could see from now. On his neck she saw mane. On his back she saw back, a long grey slope of it going away like a hillside. On his head, between his ears, with his pale eyes below her, she could see what he saw.

The coast. Low and grey. Coming up out of the sea.

And on it, below the empty hill where Gullhaven had been, a road.

---

The Coast Road.

He walked out of the sea onto it. Stone, for him, though it was only gravel to anyone else. She felt it hum when he put his paw on it. Faintly. The coast was dead ground, every Saltreach charm-seller would have told you. It hummed for him anyway.

*It's all stone,* he said. *Under. All of it. The whole coast.*

She'd felt the far shore through him that way. Leagues of it. Every hill, every buried column.

Now she felt Saltreach.

---

The village ran.

Redshank. A dozen huts on stilts at the western edge of the Saltings, where the salt-marsh began. She saw it from his head: smoke, boats drawn up on the shingle, nets on poles. People.

They'd come back. The coast had been emptied fifty days ago, Saul Trent said; the Clan had come with baskets and flown everyone inland. But there were fish here and nothing at the camp, and some had walked back.

They saw a hill walk out of the sea.

She watched them run. Out of the huts, down the ladders, across the salt-flats, inland, in every direction, small and dark against the grey mud. They left their pots on the fires. A dog ran after them, barking, and then stopped and came back and sat on the shingle and looked up at the lion.

The dog stayed.

---

The Clan came on the hundred-and-eighty-second.

Two pairs. Out of the north-east, low over the Saltings, on coast-patrol from the Accord's camp. She saw them coming from a long way off. A blue dragon and a red.

They saw Orrun.

They stopped in the air. Hung there. Then came down on the Coast Road a quarter-league off and didn't come any closer.

---

She waited.

Orrun lay down on the road. Slowly. As a hill would lie down if a hill could. He put his chin on his paws, and she was very high still, between his ears, but less high, and she stood up there in the mist of his mane where they could see her.

The old man on the blue dragon looked a long time.

Then he got down and walked up the road toward them.

---

Brede.

She knew him. An old man of the ledges, with a white beard and a blue dragon as old as he was. He'd sat the heights over the peat road above Varrhold in the winter, the farmer had written, while the Clan's young ones went past below. She'd never met him.

He stopped at Orrun's paw. He looked up.

At her. At her coat. At her grey neck, and the iron-grey at her temple.

"Knight," said Brede.

---

He told her the news at a fire on the shingle.

She came down off Orrun's head for it, by his nose, walking down his face as down a hillside. Brede had a fire going on the shingle at Redshank with the villagers' abandoned driftwood, and his young red-thread rider, a girl of twenty with a cut lip, sat on the other side of it looking at the lion and not at either of them.

The dog came and lay down by Maren.

Brede told it plainly. In the Clan's short words.

---

The camp. Behind the Wend, in the Marches, below Greyhold. Ninety thousand people. Rowan was Marshal. She'd held the Candle Downs for an hour and Hallowmere was empty, broken, and the thing lay in the Mere.

Corsa had fallen.

The Bell had come in over the Saltings on the eighty-eighth and the chain had snapped and the Tidehall gone. The farmer had turned its head with fire. Marrow was dead.

"And Ulric," said Brede.

---

She didn't say anything.

"On the Tidehall's steps," said Brede. "Chest-deep. Handing people up. A clerk's boy, last. Sigge took the boy. The sea took Ulric." He looked at the fire. "We burned him at Skarrow. Arn and the farmer poured him, at Coldtarn. Together."

She sat on the shingle with the dog against her leg.

The cry.

At sunrise, on the ninety-fifth, from the stern of the *Kittiwake*, with the Shatters ahead. A green speck on the cliffs above Blackwater. Crying. Over and over. The same long high note.

*It isn't for us,* Orrun had said.

It had been for him. For Ulric. Lisska had flown all the way to Blackwater to tell her and been too late and stood on the cliff and cried.

---

"And the farmer," said Brede.

She looked up.

"Home," said Brede. "Three days back. Over the Marches at dusk. The whole camp saw." He looked at Orrun, lying along the Coast Road like a hill that had been there forever. "On dragons the size of hills. Like that." He nodded at the lion. "The red one. The green one. The old one with both wings straight."

He looked back at the fire.

"I never thought I'd see the old one fly straight," he said. "I've known her sixty years."

---

The villagers came back on the hundred-and-eighty-third.

Slowly. Across the salt-flats, in ones and twos, when Brede had flown out over them on his blue and called down that the hill was the lion's knight's and wouldn't hurt anybody. They came back to their huts. They stood at the bottom of their ladders and looked at the lion.

The lion looked back. Not moving.

An old woman came up the shingle to Maren.

---

Small. Bent. Brown as a walnut. She stood in front of Maren with her hands on her hips and looked her up and down.

"You're the knight," she said.

"Yes."

"Your beast," said the old woman. She pointed at Orrun. "Moves stone. They say."

"Yes."

The old woman pointed down the shingle. At the foot of her hut's stilts. At a heap of stones.

"Then he can put my wall back," she said.

---

It had been a sea-wall.

Low. Rough stones, piled, a man's height long, at the foot of the stilts, to keep the winter sea off them. The Bell's surf had come over it on the hundred-and-forty-fifth, the old woman said, out of a calm, three waves, and knocked it flat. Her husband had built it. He was dead. She couldn't lift the stones.

Maren looked at it.

It was a small thing. It was the smallest thing she'd been asked in a year.

She knelt on the shingle and put her right hand on the heap.

---

The stones came up.

She felt them. Through her hand, through Orrun, lying along the road above the village. She thought *wall*, as she'd thought *ridge* at the Ysse and *lift* in the reef channel, and pulled, gently, the smallest pull she knew how to make.

The stones came up off the shingle. Shifting. Turning. Finding each other. The wall began to stand.

And the cliff moved.

---

Behind the village. Behind the stilt-huts and the salt-flats' edge. The low grey cliff where the Coast Road ran along the top, where Orrun lay. She felt it move. Through him. A creak. Deep. Going through the rock from end to end, like a beam taking a load it wasn't made for.

She looked up.

A crack opened in the cliff's face.

Long. Black. From the top to the bottom. With dust coming out of it. Above the village. Above the huts and the old woman and the dog.

---

She stopped.

She took her hand off the stones so fast she fell back on the shingle.

The wall fell down. The stones dropped back into their heap with a clatter. The old woman jumped.

The creak in the cliff stopped.

The crack stayed.

---

She sat on the shingle and looked at it.

A black line down the face of the cliff above Redshank. A man's width at the top. Not moving. Dust still coming out of it, a little, in the wind.

She'd pulled the smallest pull she knew how to make. To put a wall back. A dead man's wall, a man's height long, for an old woman on a salt-marsh.

And the cliff had come with it.

---

*Too much,* said Orrun. From the road above. Very low. *It's all too much now. I'm too big. You're too big. Everything comes.*

She knew.

She'd felt it on the far shore, when the ridge flowed round his paws. It hadn't mattered there. There'd been nothing to break.

Here there was a village under the cliff.

She couldn't mend a wall.

She could drop a cliff.

---

The old woman was looking at her.

At the crack. At the knight sitting on the shingle with her dead arm in her coat and her one hand flat on the ground beside her, not touching anything.

"Well," said the old woman. "That's no good."

"No," said Maren.

---

They built the wall by hand.

All that day. Maren and the old woman and Brede's red-thread girl with the cut lip, who'd got down off her dragon without being asked, and then the villagers, one at a time, coming down their ladders to help. Stone by stone. Lifting them off the heap and setting them, rough, one on another, as the dead man had set them.

Maren lifted with her right hand. Her one hand. She couldn't feel the stones in it. She lifted them anyway.

It took the day.

At dusk the wall stood. Low, rough, a man's height long, at the foot of the stilts. The old woman put her hand on it.

"That'll do," she said.

---

She told Orrun that night.

On the shingle. Lying against his paw, the size of a house, at the bottom of the cliff with the crack in it. The dog asleep against her.

*Nothing small,* she said. *Not with it. Not now.*

He waited.

*Anything small, I do with my hands,* said Maren. *Like today. The wall. A door. A gate. I lift it. With this.* She held up her right hand in the dark. *Not with you. Not with the stone.*

*And the big things?*

She was quiet a long time.

*When there's nobody under the cliff,* she said.

---

He thought about it.

She felt him think. Slowly. Stone settling at the bottom of a well.

*That's hard,* he said.

*Yes.*

*Harder than the big things,* said Orrun.

*Yes,* said Maren. *That's why.*

---

She counted again on the hundred-and-eighty-fourth.

Walking north. On his head, between his ears, up the Coast Road, along the Saltings' edge, toward the Wend's mouth and the river, and up the river to the camp. Brede's two pairs over them in the air.

She counted out loud.

---

She didn't decide to. It came out of her. The first stilt-hut they passed at the next village, empty, its ladder pulled up.

"One," she said.

The next.

"Two."

The boats drawn up on the shingle below them. "Three boats. Four." The nets on their poles. The posts of a fish-drying rack. Gulls on a sandbar, more than she could count, so she counted them anyway, until she lost them, and started again.

---

She counted his steps.

Each one half a league. She counted them aloud, one after another, sitting between his ears in the mist. The Coast Road going by under him, grey, humming for him.

"The hundred-and-eighty-fourth," she said. To nobody. To the sea. "The hundred-and-eighty-fourth day."

She wasn't sure. She'd lost thirty-two days under the sea and been given them back by a fisherman. She wasn't sure of anything she counted now.

She counted anyway.

---

*Forty,* said Orrun.

She stopped. He was counting with her. His steps. In the place where they talked, slow and deep, at the bottom of the well.

*Forty-one,* he said. *Forty-two.*

She laughed. On the side of her mouth that worked.

"Forty-three," she said.

---

The dusk came on the hundred-and-eighty-fifth.

On the Coast Road, on a rise above the Saltings, with the salt-flats going away grey and silver on their left and the sea beyond, and on their right the low grey-green scrub of the Ashlings going away inland. The Wend's mouth a day ahead. Brede's two pairs settling on the rise behind them for the night.

Orrun stopped.

He lifted his head.

---

She felt it before she knew what it was.

Through him. Through the stone. Not the coast's stone. Further. Through the coast and the Ashlings and the Marches and the downs beyond, the whole long dead grey body of Winshire's stone, a hundred leagues and more, north-west, toward the middle of everything.

To the Mere.

---

He turned his head toward it.

Slowly. North-west. Away from the sea. Toward Hearthmere.

*There,* he said.

She felt it through him. Lying. In the water of the Mere, a hundred leagues off, in the middle of Hearthmere. Huge. As big as he was now. Bigger. Lying in the water, still, its head on the Mere's bottom.

Awake.

And on its back, among five dark shapes, something burning. Like a coal.

His kin.

---

He looked at it a long time.

She felt him want to go. Not to fight. To go. As he'd gone to the Hollow Ring when it was standing over the stones, and lain down at ten paces with his head on his paws. As he'd gone to his mother on her hill.

*It's my kin,* he said.

*I know.*

*It's lying in the water,* said Orrun. *Alone. Burning.*

She didn't say anything. She put her hand on his head, between his ears, in the mist.

---

He didn't go.

He put his head down. Slowly. Turned it back from the north-west, to the north, to the road. To the Wend's mouth a day ahead, and the river, and the camp.

And walked on.

She counted his steps in the dark, out loud, until she fell asleep between his ears.

He stayed awake.
