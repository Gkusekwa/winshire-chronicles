---

They came home on the hundred-and-seventy-second.

Twenty-four days from the lagoon. By day, pier to pier, as her father had sailed it and Abram before him. Hove to at night on a long line under every pier's broken top, with ninety-three people asleep in the hold and on the deck and in two boats astern, and the birds settling.

Nobody died.

She'd thought about that, the night after *forty-one*. Lying in the stern cabin with the book shut. Ninety-three people from a fleet that had sailed to sell her family's road, and the Bell somewhere under the sea, and the Shatters, and a ketch with a crew of seven that sat so low she shipped water over the rail in a swell.

Nobody died.

She didn't know what to do with it. She put it away.

---

The chain was up when they came in.

Across Blackwater's mouth. Iron this time. Every shackle. Mended. With a hut on the western headland now, where somebody had built a window with a grille in it, as at Corsa, and a clerk behind the grille with a book.

She brought the *Kittiwake* up to it and called across the water.

The clerk came out of his hut. Looked at her ketch. At her.

He went back into the hut.

---

He wouldn't take her money.

She had it. Coppers, in a purse. Enough for chain money for a ketch. She held it up. He came out again and stood on the headland's rock with his book under his arm and called down to her across the water, quite politely.

"The *Kittiwake*," he said. "Merrin."

"Yes."

"Unsalted," said the clerk. "Since the ninety-fourth. By the Council's finding." He looked at his book. "The chain can't take money from an unsalted hull, mistress. It's not a contract. It can't be."

He went back into the hut.

---

She lay off the chain a day.

At anchor, outside the mouth, in the swell, with ninety-three people on her deck looking at Blackwater's black cliffs and its stilt-houses a mile inside the chain and not able to get to them.

They looked at her.

She looked at the chain.

That was unsalted. That was what it meant. She'd known. She'd said it at the tiller with the boom behind her. *I'm unsalted. As of the chain.* She hadn't thought about what happened when you came home.

---

Fosse came out the next morning.

In a boat. Four oars. His grey coat with the Ostrey cuff. His round spectacles. He came alongside the *Kittiwake* and stood up in the stern of the boat and looked up at her rail, and at the ninety-three faces along it, and smiled.

"Mistress Merrin."

"Master Fosse."

"These good people," said Fosse, "are the House of Ostrey's passengers. They paid Ostrey for their berths." He spread his soft hands. "The house is honour-bound to see them home. The house will pay their chain money."

He looked at her ketch.

"They happen to be aboard your vessel," he said. "So the chain will have to go down for your vessel. Won't it?"

---

The chain went down.

Ostrey paid. Kit heard the clerk ring his bell. The capstan in the headland hut turned and the iron roared and the chain went down into the black water, and the *Kittiwake* went in over it with ninety-three of Ostrey's passengers on her deck.

Unsalted. Paid for by Ostrey.

She didn't look at Fosse as they passed his boat.

---

Master Loy went ashore first.

At the walkway below the houses' line, where Fosse's boat put him. The old pilot with his useless chart under his arm. He'd sat in the *Kittiwake*'s stern for twenty-four days looking at the piers going by and the farmer's milestones and saying nothing. He went up the ladder and along the walkway without looking back.

The rich went to Ostrey's counting-house.

In their rotted good coats, with their salt-white faces. Up the walkways in a line to the Ostrey house's grey shutters, where Fosse's clerks were waiting with books.

The servants and the children went anywhere. Into the stilt-houses. Onto the shingle. Into the town. One little girl stood on the walkway after the others had gone and looked back at the *Kittiwake* a long time.

Then she went too.

---

Nobody sold her water.

That was the next thing. She'd known it too. *No harbour would sell her water. Not in Corsa, not in Wendmouth, not here.* She'd said it to herself in the stern cabin with the dish on the table, the night she chose.

The spring under the eastern cliff had a keeper. A man with a tally-stick who'd sold her father water for thirty years. Tansy went to him on the hundred-and-seventy-fourth with the casks.

He turned his back.

---

Tansy filled the casks at night.

From a seep under the north shore. A trickle out of the black rock by the roofless boathouse, where the peat-water came down off the mountains and went into the inlet. Brown, bitter. Nobody's. Tansy took the little boat across at midnight and filled the casks a cupful at a time, and came back at dawn with her red arms aching and her face set.

She didn't sing. She hadn't since Sim.

Nobody would buy far-stone from Kit either.

---

The Merrin house was still under Council wax.

The tall stilt-house with the ochre shutters, peeling. The wax on the door. Her father's charts gone. She lived aboard, as she always had. She'd always slept aboard. Now she ate aboard and washed aboard and did everything aboard, because there was nowhere else in Blackwater that would let her.

It was the custom, she told herself. *Never sleep ashore.*

It wasn't funny any more.

---

Fosse came again on the hundred-and-eighty-sixth.

Along the *Kittiwake*'s side, in his boat, with a paper. Of course with a paper. She met him at the rail.

"The Council has met," said Fosse.

"Which Council?"

"The houses that remain," said Fosse. "In the boathouse." He smiled. "It finds the ketch *Kittiwake* forfeit to the Council. For breach of the requisition of the ninety-second day: *to be held at the Council's mooring from dawn of the ninety-fifth, as lead ship of the Council's fleet.*" He held up the paper. "You broke the chain the night before. The Council's fleet sailed without its lead ship." He paused. "We all know how that went."

Kit didn't say anything.

"The Council has assigned its claim to the House of Ostrey," said Fosse. "For the losses. A Council marshal will take her on the hundred-and-ninety-sixth." He looked at her. "The house of Ostrey would like to say it's sorry."

"It isn't."

"No," said Fosse. "But it would like to say it."

---

Jory came home on the hundred-and-eighty-seventh.

On a green dragon.

She saw it from the walkway. Out of the north-west, over the black cliffs, low, in the morning. Green. The size of the boathouse. Bigger. Coming down onto the roofless boathouse on the north shore as the farmer's green had come down on it once before.

Except that once before it had sat on the walls.

This time the walls cracked.

---

The whole inlet came out to look.

Onto every walkway. Every ladder. The houses' people at their grey shutters and green and white. The sellswords on the shingle. Fosse at his counting-house door. They watched a green dragon the size of a hall settle onto Blackwater's boathouse with its walls cracking under it, and put its head down to the shingle.

And a thin brown boy slide down its foreleg.

---

She didn't run.

Merrins didn't. She stood on the walkway outside her sealed house and watched him come. Across the shingle. Down to the water. Into the boat at the boathouse's sea-door. Rowing. Across the inlet, toward her, with his long brown arms.

He ran.

Up the ladder. Along the walkway. And she had him. She held him. On the walkway, in front of the whole inlet. She held him a long time.

---

"The farmer brought me," said Jory. Into her coat. "Over the sea. On the old one. In a day."

"I know."

"He said bring him home." Jory let go. Looked at her. His face was brown and thin and his eyes were wet. "Lisska. He told Lisska. *Bring him home.* So she did."

Kit looked across the inlet at the green dragon on the boathouse.

It looked back at her. Sideways. As if it were pleased with itself.

---

The old man went with it.

He'd come along the walkway in his blanket, with the sea-birds on his shoulders that had followed him from the far shore and wouldn't leave, and Corrie at his elbow with his papers. He looked up at the green dragon a while.

"I'll tell them," he said. To Kit. "What I read. On the shore. In the light."

"Will they believe you?"

"No," said Idony happily. "Not at first. Then they will."

Jory rowed them over. The dragon put its foreleg down for them, as it had for him, and the old man went up it slowly, with Corrie pushing, and the birds lifting off him and settling again.

Then it went. Up off the boathouse in one beat that sent a wave across the inlet and rocked every boat in it, and away north-west, over the cliffs.

---

The inlet had seen.

Everyone. Every house. A green dragon the size of a hall bring the Merrin boy home. The unsalted Merrins. The boy the Council had taken for its fleet's pilot. Brought home from beyond the edge of the world by the farmer's dragon, and set down on Blackwater's boathouse, and left.

Nobody said anything to her about it.

But the spring's keeper didn't turn his back on Tansy that night. He didn't sell her water. He looked at the ground while she filled the casks, and didn't stop her.

---

Gannet went ashore every day.

For news. As he always had. In the little boat, across the inlet, whistling. And back at dusk. He brought news. He always had. The Council in the boathouse. The houses. What Ostrey was paying for. The camp up the Wend, and the Accord, and a rumour out of the Marches about three mountains in the sky.

She saw him on the hundred-and-eighty-ninth coming out of Ostrey's counting-house.

---

On the walkway. In the afternoon. Fosse's door, grey shutters. Gannet came out of it and stood on the walkway a moment in the sun, putting something into his coat. A purse. She saw it go in. It was heavy.

He saw her across the water, on the *Kittiwake*'s deck. The smile came first.

He raised his hand.

---

Flakes, she thought.

He'd chipped far-stone every day on the far shore. A canvas bag of it, heavy, every day for weeks, among the column-tops. He'd brought it home. Ostrey bought stone. Ostrey would buy anything. Gannet was crew, not house; he wasn't unsalted. He could sell. Of course he'd sold it to Ostrey.

She'd have done the same.

She didn't ask him. She thought about that afterwards.

---

The letter came on the hundred-and-ninety-second.

By a Kessmoor courier. Up from the Wend, from the Accord's camp, on a horse that had never been bonded to anything, with a brass clasp at his collar. He rode down the cliff path into Blackwater and along the shingle to the *Kittiwake*'s mooring and called out for Mistress Merrin, and handed her a paper sealed in wax.

She knew the seal. A ring, and six lines going into it.

The Accord's.

---

The old man had written to the Marshal.

She found that out when she opened it. On the hundred-and-seventy-third, the day after they came home, he'd sat in her stern cabin and said a letter and Corrie had written it, because Corrie wrote a good round hand and the old man couldn't hold a pen. To Ilse Rowan, Marshal of the Accord, at the camp behind the Wend. About Kit. About the ninety-three. About the chain and the water and the wax.

This was the Marshal's answer.

---

*The house of Merrin carries for the Accord, under the Accord's warrant, by the war's necessary costs.*

*Its ketch and its people are the Accord's carriers, and no other claim lies against them while the war lasts.*

*I. Rowan. Marshal.*

And the seal. Pressed hard.

---

She read it three times.

Then she took it to Fosse.

---

He read it at his counting-house door. On the walkway. In the sun. He read it once, carefully, with his round spectacles on his nose, and then he looked at the seal for a long time.

"The Tide Council signed the Accord," he said. "At Ironhythe."

"I know."

"Its warrant stands over the Council's finding." He folded the paper and handed it back to her. Pleasantly. "Of course. The house of Ostrey withdraws its claim on the *Kittiwake*." He bowed. The same bow. "For the duration of the war."

"For the duration," said Kit.

"Wars end," said Fosse. "Mistress Merrin."

He went back inside.

---

The price was at cost.

That was the warrant. *By the war's necessary costs.* The Accord would pay what it cost to carry and not a copper over. Not a profit. Not a percentage. Not a chain-money. Cost.

She sat in the stern cabin with the warrant on the table beside the dish and thought about it.

A Saltreach house carrying at cost. Nobody had ever heard of such a thing. There was no word for it in the League. Her grandmother would have spat. Her father would have laughed. Abram would have...

She didn't know what Abram would have done.

She took it.

---

The first job was barges.

Rowan wanted every flat-bottomed hull on the coast. The courier said so: up the Wend to the camp, with food, with salt fish, with people, back down for more. The camp was ninety thousand and growing and the Iron Road couldn't feed it alone.

The *Kittiwake* drew too much for the upper Wend.

The warrant bought two old salt-barges off the Saltings. Flat, broad, ugly, their timbers white with salt. An unsalted house couldn't buy a barge in Saltreach. The Accord could. The Saltings bargemaster looked at the seal and took the Accord's paper for them, and spat on the shingle after, and took it anyway.

Ferris and Lorn rigged them. Two days.

---

The first barge went up on the hundred-and-ninety-fourth.

With Nye and a Saltings hand to work her, and a hold of salt fish from the Saltings for the camp.

Nye cast off. The barge went out across the inlet toward the mouth, toward the chain, which the Accord's warrant lowered now, and the clerk in the hut took the Accord's paper and wrote it in his book and rang his bell.

---

She went up on the cliff on the hundred-and-ninety-fifth.

At dawn. The path she'd watched the knight come down on the lion. She went up it in the dark and stood at the top with the wind off the sea, where the black rock stopped. To look east.

The sun was coming up out of the sea.

East. Along the coast. Under the mountains' end, where the Worldsend came down to the water in great black cliffs. Red, then gold, then white.

And something walking up out of it.

---

Out of the sun's light on the sea. Where the light lay on the water in a long road from the horizon to the shore. Something on the road. Walking.

White-gold. Tall. Taller than the cliffs. Too bright to look at. Four long legs. A long neck. A head.

A horn.

It walked up out of the sea's light onto the land, onto the black rocks at the mountains' foot. As if the road of light on the water had been a road. Up the rocks. Inland. North-west, over the mountains' lower slopes, toward the Marches.

It left the light on the sea behind it.

---

Below her, the inlet woke.

She heard it. The ladders, going down. People coming out onto the walkways in the dawn, all round the water, and turning to look east, and standing still. The houses' people at their shutters. The sellswords on the shingle. The keeper of the spring. Fosse, at his door, in his nightshirt, with his spectacles in his hand.

All of Blackwater, looking east at a god walking inland out of the sea.

Nobody said anything.

---

Kit stood on the cliff with the wind in her face.

She'd seen it at the end of the road. At the hall, in the knight's hand, in the old man's reading. *The Lamp.* Sarovel's Sun on the Stair. A Titan that had stopped. Walking home, out of the far shore's light, up the sun's road on the sea.

To fight.

She watched it go over the mountains' foot until it was gone.

Then she went back down the cliff path to the shingle, to her ketch, to the warrant and the dish on the table, and the barges to rig.

At cost.
