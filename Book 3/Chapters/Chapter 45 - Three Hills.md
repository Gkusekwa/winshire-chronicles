---

They were too big for the yard.

He'd known that the night he came down, on the hundred-and-forty-ninth. He'd known it better every day since. The hatching caves' yard, the flat of trodden turf before the cave mouth with the burn across it, was a step for Kharrak now. A doorstep. He put one forefoot in it and the rest of him lay on the moor above.

The herdsmen called them the three hills.

He heard it on the second day. Two of the Clan's high-moor herdsmen, bringing a flock down past Coldtarn's rim at dawn, stopping on the track to look at the shapes lying round the tarn in the mist. Red. Bronze. Green.

*There's the three hills,* one said. *Back again.*

---

He lay in the cave mostly.

The first ten days. By the warm spring, on a pallet Ada made, with Wren beside him with her milestone stone in her lap and her plait done up again. His chest pulled. The scars. Three ridges, bronze and rust and green. He coughed. His breath was short. He couldn't climb the slope to the rim without stopping twice. Sixty leagues south-west the Last Titan lay in the Mere, and he couldn't climb a slope.

Ada fed him broth. She didn't sing. She didn't talk much.

Bel came up from Varrhold with eggs and sat with him and talked a great deal, about the hold and the market and Gorm's ledgers and who'd said what to whom. He listened. It was the most restful thing that had happened to him in a year.

---

They ate.

That was the next thing. A flock a day, between the three of them. The Clan's high-moor flocks. He'd paid for them, or meant to. The herdsmen came up to the caves with their tally-sticks and their faces long, and he said put it on the account, and they said what account, and he said Gorm's, and they went away.

Kharrak hunted.

The Scarfell's eastern edge, where the moor went down to the wild country before the Plains. Moor cattle, the shaggy black ones that ran wild there, as big as aurochs. Wild stonehorns, come over from the Plains in the spring. He took them like hares and came back to the rim with his jaws red, pleased with himself, and the herdsmen on the eastern edge sent word to Varrhold that the farmer's red one was eating the wild herds, and somebody should do something.

Nobody did.

---

Lisska fished.

Coldtarn first. The black water. She lay on the rim with her head in it, as Aurveth had once lain for a year after Ragnald, and fished. Then the other tarns. One by one. They went empty.

Aurveth ate least.

She lay on the pouring-stone at the tarn's edge, bronze, shining, both wings straight, and ate a sheep in two days and looked. West. Mostly west. And south, sometimes. Through things.

---

The freed came up to look at them.

From the ledges round Varrhold and the high glens above Coldtarn where they'd gone when the pens opened. Young ones. Old ones. A hundred, perhaps, over the days. They came up onto the crags round the tarn's bowl and sat there.

On the crags. Round the three hills lying on the rim and the shore. Like children round a fire on a winter night, near enough to feel it, not near enough to be burned.

They didn't fly away when he came out.

They looked at him too.

---

The Clan's world didn't fit them.

He saw it. Bit by bit. The Roosts: Kharrak tried the Roosts on the hundred-and-sixtieth, to see, because the Roosts were where dragons went, and put his weight on the third ledge, Teo's, and the ledge cracked. A long crack, through three hundred years of claws, and a piece of the ledge's lip fell into the gorge.

He'd come off it at once.

*It's small,* he said. *Everything's small.*

---

The setters' ledge. The pens. The caves. The hold. All built for dragons a tenth their size. The size they'd been when they came to him dying in a mill.

The riders didn't come near.

He understood that. A rider had a dragon the size of a barn and had thought that big all his life. He looked up at a dragon the size of a hill lying on Coldtarn's rim and didn't know what to do with his face.

The young dragons of the ledges were frightened.

---

Arn came on the hundred-and-eighty-fifth.

On Ildra. Up from Varrhold, where he'd been since he came back from the coast. Small and dark on her, coming over the moor at noon. He brought her down on the turf above the cave mouth, beside Kharrak's foreleg, which was taller than Ildra, and got off, and looked up.

At Kharrak. At Lisska on the crag. At Aurveth on the shore.

He looked a long time.

"Well," he said.

---

He saw it.

Calder watched him see it. Standing on the moor at Coldtarn's rim with his hand on his young dragon's neck, looking at three hills. The Clan's chief, in all but the chair.

"They'd eat the Scarfell in a year," said Arn.

"Yes."

"The Roosts won't hold them. Gorm says Teo's ledge cracked."

"Yes."

"The riders won't go near them." Arn looked at the freed on the crags. "Those will. Those sit round them all day. Leir says half the ledges are empty." He looked back at Kharrak. "They're not dragons any more."

"They're dragons," said Calder. "They're just big."

Arn didn't argue.

---

They talked on the hundred-and-eighty-sixth.

Properly. For the first time. Calder thought about that afterwards: two years, the ford, the ledger, the Tidehall, two pours, and they'd never once sat down and said anything that wasn't about the next thing.

They sat on Sigrun's rock.

At the tarn's rim. The flat rock under the rim where Sigrun Holm had sat in her cloak with her back to the wind and told him about Ragnald. Aurveth below them on the pouring-stone with her head on the water. Kharrak along the rim behind them like a red wall. Lisska on the crag above with her chin on her paws, listening, pretending not to.

Ildra between Arn's feet.

---

"You never wanted them," said Arn.

He said it to the tarn. Not as a question.

Calder looked at the black water.

"No," he said.

He let it be. Then he said the rest.

"They're not the Clan's," he said. "And they're not mine."

---

Arn nodded.

Slowly. As if he'd known, and wanted it said aloud, on this rock, at this water, where his father had gone down and his uncle after him.

"Gorm has them in the books," he said. "Still. *Aurveth. Kharrak. Lisska.* Bred, fed, bonded, mutton eleven years." He laughed, not much. "The riders asked for them under the Clan's thread. In the spring. At Bel's table."

"I said they weren't mine to give."

"I know. I was there." Arn looked at Ildra between his feet. "I didn't understand it."

"Do you now?"

"Yes," said Arn.

---

He talked about the pens.

Calder hadn't known all of it. Arn told it. The young red with its wing broken in the eleventh pen. Leir and the long knife. Holding its head. The third ledge, at dawn, and a voice like an unused hinge saying *no*.

"I never wanted one," said Arn. "I wanted one more than anything when I was fifteen and they walked past me. Then I stopped wanting it. I thought." He put his hand on Ildra's broken horn. "She chose me because I held a dying thing's head in the straw. That's all. Not because I was Haldor's son. Not because I'd stood on a ledge in a clean shirt."

He was quiet.

"Teo stood once," he said. "The next year. And nothing stopped for him either. And he laughed all the way down the stair."

"I know," said Calder. "Bel told me. Years ago."

---

They talked about the chair.

Arn told him about the coast. The riders round his fire on the Ashlings' edge. Bjarke. *Sit in it.*

"I said the chair's for whoever pours," said Arn.

Calder looked at him.

"Two of us poured," said Arn. "For my father, Ulric and me. For Ulric, you and me." He looked at the black water. "Gunna laughed for an hour."

Calder didn't laugh.

He thought about it a long time.

"I'm not Clan," he said at last.

"No," said Arn. "That's why it's funny."

---

And they talked about after.

"When it's done," said Arn. "If there's a *when*." He looked at Kharrak behind them, red, along the rim. "They'll not come back to the ledges."

"No."

"Good," said Arn.

He meant it. Calder could hear that he meant it.

"I'll not have them in the books," said Arn. "When it's done. Gorm can write *gone*. Gorm can write whatever he likes." He stood up off Sigrun's rock. "Any dragon that flies with the Clan flies by choice. It's in the ledger. Those three chose you." He looked down at Calder. "Not the Clan. Not even you, really. They chose."

---

He told him about the pour.

Before he went. Standing on the rim with Ildra. The two pairs lost off the Saltings' outer spit on the hundred-and-forty-fifth. Ivo Brack and his grey. Sunniva and her young red. Nine people in their baskets.

"We poured for them on the hundred-and-sixtieth," said Arn. "Here. Without ashes. The priest has words for it." He looked at the black water. "You weren't here. You were asleep in the cave."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be," said Arn. "You'd have poured with me, if I'd asked. I didn't ask. You couldn't stand."

He got up on Ildra and went.

---

Aurveth had been looking west every night.

He'd felt her do it. Since the caves. Lying on the pouring-stone with her head on the water and her new eyes open. Looking. Through the mountain, through the moor, through the Marches and the river and the downs, west and south, a long way.

At the middle of everything.

On the hundred-and-eighty-eighth he asked her to show him.

---

He lay against her.

On the pouring-stone. In the dark. With his back against her bronze side, warm, and his hand flat on her plates where the bond was. Wren asleep in the cave below. The freed on the crags round the bowl, dark shapes, still.

*Show me,* he said.

She didn't answer. She opened the place where they talked a little wider.

He looked through her.

---

Through the Scarfell.

Through the mountain he was lying on, as if it were glass. Down through the moor, the rock, the roots of the hills. Out, south and west. Over the Marches, which were nothing to her sight, a blur of stone and earth. Over the Wend, a thread of running water she saw as a cold line. Over the Candle Downs, black, dead, nothing in them.

To the Mere.

Sixty leagues.

---

The water first.

Wide. Shallow. Grey. He saw it through her as she saw everything now: as a weight, a shape, a temperature. The boiled Mere, gone cool again, lying in its valley below the broken terraces of Hallowmere.

And in the water, on its bottom, something lying.

---

The Last Titan.

He'd seen it. At the Ruin, going out of its chains, the night the world changed. He'd seen it in the cavern under Greyhold coming down toward Maren at the shut place. He'd never seen it like this. Through Aurveth. Through water and stone and sixty leagues.

Lying on the Mere's bottom on its side. Its great head on the mud. Its wings, stone and leather, folded. The chain-ends on its legs. The size of a hill, of three hills, of more.

And on its back, its plates.

---

He saw the eggs.

Five of them. Grown into its plates along its back, as Maren had told him in the cavern, as the old man had written. Five dark shapes, egg-shaped, each the size of a cart, sunk into the plates as if the plates had grown round them. Dark. Cold. Shut.

The sleep relics. From the five temples. The Ring, Vael's, the Scald, the Sun Stair, the Bell.

And among them, one more.

---

Burning.

Like a coal. In a grate, at night, when the fire's gone down and there's one coal left at the back still red, still alive, the only light in the room. Among the five dark ones. Not dark. Red. Gold. Breathing.

The control relic.

The one from Hearthmere's vault. The one the crown had kept under the oath three hundred years and never once been answered by. The one the old ones had made as a safeguard, to call the six into the light.

*It answers a breath that is unbound.*

---

He could see exactly where it was.

That was the thing. He lay on the pouring-stone with his eyes shut, looking through Aurveth's, and saw the place on the Last Titan's back as clearly as he could have seen a nail in a barn door.

Behind the left shoulder. Below the ridge of the spine by a hall's breadth. Between the second dark egg and the third.

Burning.

---

*Can anyone else see it?* he said.

*No,* said Aurveth.

*The lion?*

*He feels it,* said Aurveth. *Through the stone. He knows it's there. He can't see where.* She was quiet. *Only me. And you, through me.*

He opened his eyes.

The tarn. Black. The crags. The stars.

He thought of the Lamp's white-gold letters over the pool in the hall without a roof. *If one of us takes it out of the last one's back, the six will hear.*

Someone would have to know where to reach.

He shut his eyes again and looked until he'd fixed it. Behind the left shoulder. A hall's breadth below the spine. Between the second and the third.

Then he made her stop.

---

She'd seen the lion too.

She told him on the hundred-and-eighty-sixth, the night after Arn. He'd asked her what she looked at in the south, when she wasn't looking west.

*Him,* she said. *The lion. On the coast.*

She showed him.

---

The Coast Road. Along the Saltings' edge, south. A hundred leagues and more.

And on it, walking north, a grey hill.

Slowly. A furlong a stride. Along the road above the salt-flats, past empty stilt-huts and boats pulled up on the shingle, with the sea on one side and the Ashlings' scrub on the other. A grey warmth, the shape of a lion, the size of a hill.

And on its head, between its ears, very small, a woman.

*She's counting,* said Aurveth.

*How do you know?*

*Her mouth moves,* said Aurveth. *All the time. Like the old man's over his glasses.*

---

The rider came on the hundred-and-ninetieth.

From Rowan. Up from the camp behind the Wend, by the Way and the moor, a young grey-thread rider on a lathered horse, because no Clan rider would bring a dragon down near the three hills.

He brought a paper. He stood at the bottom of the slope and held it up and wouldn't come any closer.

Calder went down to him. Slowly. Stopping on the way.

---

*The Accord musters at the camp behind the Wend on the two-hundredth day.*

*The old ones are coming. The Lamp has been seen at sea, walking.*

*Come.*

*I. Rowan.*

---

He read it out. For Wren, who'd come down the slope behind him and was holding his coat.

"Say it again," said Wren.

"*The old ones are coming,*" said Calder. "*Come.*"

He looked up the slope. At the three hills lying round Coldtarn's rim in the afternoon light. Red. Bronze. Green. And the freed on the crags round them, a hundred dragons the size of barns, sitting, looking at the three as if waiting to be told.

"We'll go," he said.

Behind him, in the cave mouth, the old man had come out into the light in his blanket, with Corrie at his elbow. Lisska had brought them up from the coast two days before, and he'd hardly stopped talking since, with Corrie's pen going.

*Yes,* said Aurveth. From the shore. *Soon.*
