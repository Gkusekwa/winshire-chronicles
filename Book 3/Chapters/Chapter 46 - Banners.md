---

The lion came on the hundred-and-ninety-first.

Up the Wend's east bank, from its mouth. She saw it from the barn's roof at noon. A hill, walking. Grey. Along the river-meadows, a furlong a stride, with the Wend going past it brown and slow and the farms on the bank emptying in front of it like water out of a tipped bucket.

The camp panicked.

She'd expected that. She'd sent riders down the bank the day before when Brede's pairs came in with the word. It hadn't helped. Ninety thousand people saw a hill walk up the river toward them and nine thousand of them ran.

Then it lay down on the water-meadows below the camp's southern edge, and put its chin on its paws.

And a woman stood up on its head.

---

They stopped running after that.

Most of them.

---

The three hills came on the hundred-and-ninety-sixth.

Out of the north-east. Over the moor's edge, at dusk, down onto the long ridge above the camp's western side. Red. Bronze. Green. They lay down along the ridge's top like three new hills that had always been there, and the camp panicked again, worse, because these had wings.

A man got down off the red one's nose and walked down the ridge.

Slowly. Stopping twice.

---

The old man came the same day.

On the green one's neck, in a blanket, with sea-birds on his shoulders and a Blackwater girl behind him, holding on. Idony Vael. He was down before her wings were still.

He came to her barn before the camp had stopped running.

"Marshal," he said. "I need a table. And paper. And Corrie." He nodded at the girl behind him. "She writes for me. I've a great deal to write."

He wrote for three days.

---

The muster came in round them.

That was what the camp was now. Not only people behind a river. An army, or what passed for one. Every kingdom's beasts.

---

Hearthmere's knights. Fewer than three hundred now, on greyharts, with their hounds. What was left of every school in the kingdom. She'd lost four hundred and twelve on the Downs, and not all of them men.

The Wyldmarch. Tova's clans, ten thousand of them, and their stonehorns on the water-meadows. The Keepers in green with their wolves. Four roc-riders, Yeva at their head, their great rust-and-white birds on the eastern ridge, heads under their wings.

Sarovel. Mattias's unchosen, on the bond-steeds their knights had left behind when the Grace failed: tall, white, nervous horses that had been bonded and weren't now. And the olifants. Forty of them. Up the Sun Road from Solenne before it emptied, hauling Kessmoor's wagons now, in traces, grey and enormous and patient.

---

Isvaren. Thirty Wardens. Down the Iron Fells' road from a Vael that didn't answer, in their blue, with snow lynx at their heels, under a woman called Aud Hallin who'd lost an eye to frost and didn't talk about Vael at all. And six stormkite-riders, with their long thin grey birds that could feel weather coming a day off.

Saltreach. Rennick's sellswords. Nell Tallis's wyverns, and Black Ruth's, and the Crane brothers'.

The Clan. Arn's riders. A hundred pairs. And the freed, who'd come down after the three hills and sat round them on the western ridge.

Kessmoor. Pike's engines. Her engineers. Strake with her burned hands.

And on the water-meadows below the camp, a lion the size of a hill.

And on the western ridge, three more hills, with wings.

---

The Lady came on the night of the hundred-and-ninety-ninth.

Up the river.

She didn't see it begin. She was in the barn. Davin came up the loft ladder at midnight with his face strange and said, *Marshal, the river*, and she went down.

---

The Wend was pale.

All of it. The whole river, from the pontoon down past the camp and up past the barn, as far as she could see. Brown water, every day of her life. Pale now. Not cloudy. Pale. As if a light had come into it from underneath and was lying along its bed.

Cold. She could feel the cold off it on the bank.

People were coming down to the water in the dark. Hundreds. Thousands. Standing on the bank, in silence, looking at a river gone the colour of the moon on frost.

---

And at the pontoon, something came up.

Out of the water. Slowly. A neck. Long. Pale. Like a river itself, rising out of the river. And on it, a head. Narrow. Long-beaked. Like a heron's. With eyes the colour of deep water.

It lay on the river by the pontoon. Its head on the water, like a long pale boat.

The Lady.

Rowan had seen her painted on the prows of Saltreach ships all her life. On the doors of sailors' chapels. On the old man's grandmother's ferry, he'd told her once, at Liss's table.

She stood on the bank with Davin and looked at a god lying in the Wend.

---

The Lamp came at dawn on the two-hundredth.

Out of the west. Over the downs on the Hearthmere bank, with the sunrise full on it. It had come round in the night.

She saw it as you see the sun, by not looking at it. A brightness on the far downs, white-gold, coming down the slope in the sun's first light. Walking. Long legs. A long neck. A horn like a spear of noon.

It came down to the Wend.

And didn't stop.

---

It walked across.

On the light. On the sun's light on the water. The river didn't part. The water didn't move. It walked across the Wend on the road of light that lay on the river from the western bank to the eastern, as if the light were stone, and the pale water under it lay still, and the Lady's head on the water by the pontoon turned to watch it come.

It came up out of the river onto the eastern meadow.

Into the camp.

---

And every beast turned its head.

All of them. At once. She felt it more than saw it. As you feel a crowd go quiet behind you.

The hounds. Every knight's hound in the camp, turning from wherever they were, lying by fires, running the lines, at heel, and looking at the light. Ash and Sorrow at Wynn's knee. Her own horse under her, turning its head, its ears forward.

The greyharts. Every one. The horses on their pickets. The stonehorns on the water-meadows, ten thousand of them, lifting their great plated heads off the grass. The Keepers' wolves. The Wardens' lynx. Nell's wyverns on their chains, hooded, turning their hooded heads. The rocs on the ridge, out from under their wings.

The Clan's dragons. The freed on the western ridge. The three hills.

The lion.

Every beast in the camp, looking at the light.

---

The olifants knelt.

Sarovel's. Forty of them, in their traces, harnessed to Kessmoor's wagons on the camp road. She saw the first one do it. Slowly. Its great front legs folding. Going down onto its knees on the road, with its trunk curled, and the wagon behind it rocking. Then the next. Then all of them, down the line, kneeling in the road in their harness, facing the light, in the dawn.

The Sun on the Stair.

Three hundred years Sarovel had painted it on its banners and sung to it at dawn. Its olifants knelt to it now in the mud of a Marches road.

---

And out of the western hills, the unicorns.

She didn't know them at first. A herd of something coming down off the hills beyond the western ridge at a run, lean, grey and silver-black, deer-boned, with something on their heads that caught the sun. Eleven. Down past the three hills, which didn't move. Down through the camp's western lines, through the tents, people scattering. Across the camp.

To the eastern meadow.

They stood round the Lamp. In a ring. Their spiral horns of living bone lifted. Wild. From the Sunward Hills, Mattias said after, a hundred leagues and more. They'd followed the light.

---

Sarovel knelt first.

Mattias's unchosen. Off their white bond-steeds, onto their knees in the meadow's edge, all of them, at once. Some of them weeping. It was their god.

Then the clans. Tova, slowly, getting down off her bay mare. The Keepers. The herd-men.

Then the crown's men. The militia. The sellswords, last, looking at each other, and then at the light, and then down.

The whole camp. Ninety thousand people.

Kneeling.

---

Rowan didn't.

She sat her horse at the eastern meadow's edge with the white blaze turning toward the light under her, and didn't get down.

She'd never prayed. Knights of Greyhold didn't, much. She'd sat in the crown's chapel at Hallowmere with a candle burning down beside twelve dead lamps and not prayed. She didn't kneel to things.

She looked round.

---

Her knights were looking at her.

Two hundred and ninety of them. On their greyharts and off them. The ones who'd held the Downs. They were looking at her. Not at the light. At their Marshal. Waiting.

And the crown's men behind them. And the militia. Agnes Wick's porters from the corn exchange, half of them on their knees and half of them not, looking from the light to her and back. Unsure.

Not sure if they should.

Not wanting to be the only ones.

---

She got down off her horse.

She knelt. In the wet grass at the meadow's edge, in her mended surcoat, on both knees.

Not for the light.

For them. So that none of them knelt alone. So that every knight and crown man and porter who'd been looking at her could see their Marshal on her knees with them, and get down, and not be ashamed.

They got down.

---

Davin didn't kneel.

She saw him at the corner of her eye. Standing at the back, by the barn, with his writing-case open on his arm and his pen going.

Counting.

She almost laughed.

---

The council sat on the two-hundred-and-first.

In the barn. Round the door on trestles. Everyone. Thale. Pike. Tova. Arn. Mattias. Rennick. Aud Hallin with her one eye. And at the end of the table, apart, at either corner, a knight with a grey neck and a dead arm in her coat, and a farmer with scarred hands and no voice.

And in the middle, on a stool, in his blanket, the old man.

With Corrie beside him. And three days' writing.

---

He told them what they were.

---

He didn't make a speech. He wasn't the kind. He read it. From the pages. Maren's square hand from the far shore, and Corrie's round one from the last three days, in order, slowly, in his thin voice, with his hands shaking on the paper, as they always did now.

The light on the hills. The script in the air. The lines.

*We were as they are.*

*We woke, and drank.*

*We stopped.*

---

The barn went very quiet.

He read on. All of it. *We put down what we were, and walked without it. The small ones saw us walk, and gave us names. They made us houses. They lit lamps to us. They said we walked unbound.*

*We went into the light.*

*They kept the names.*

He put the page down.

---

"The gods," said Idony. Not reading now. Looking round the table. "In every chapel. The Grey Mother in your hearthstones, Lord Thale. The Lamp on Sarovel's Stair. The Lady on every Saltreach prow. The three-faced god at every crossroads in the Marches." He looked at the farmer. "They were Titans. Like the six. They woke, and drank, as the six are drinking. And they stopped."

He looked at the paper in his hand.

"*The gods are what is left of Titans who stopped,*" he read.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

---

Then he read them the other thing.

The collapse. What the Grey Mother had shown the knight on the hill. The one realm. People among beasts. The old ones teaching the hooked script, so the small ones could talk. And the small ones taking the script and striking it into iron. Into a collar. Onto a beast. Then many beasts. Then a chain, with *hold* on the inside of every link.

Then a Titan.

Under a hill, chained, its breath going up into a city that lit its lamps on it.

And the six rising. And the realm breaking. And the sea coming in.

"The one realm fell," said Idony, "for doing exactly that."

---

Thale had gone white.

She saw it. Across the table. The old man's chain of office on his chest. The crown's. The lamps that had been the gods' gift to the crown, in a chapel with twelve glass lamps on its altar step, that she'd sat in.

Fed on what bled out of a chained Titan under Greyhold. For three hundred years.

Thale didn't say anything. He put his thin hand flat on the table, very still.

---

Tova said it.

Slowly. Weighing each word. Her root-knotted hands flat on the door.

"*One again,*" she said.

The table looked at her.

"The Oneward's oath," said Tova. "The Restoration. That burned the Thornwood's edge and chained beasts in my country for fifty years. *One again.* Back to the one realm. To the old days, when the old ones walked." She looked at the old man. "That."

"Yes," said Idony.

"They wanted *that* again."

"Yes," said Idony. "They didn't know what it was."

---

Mattias put his face in his hands.

She saw him do it. The young tribune with ink on his fingers. Sarovel's. Whose Bond-King's line had held a unicorn bond nine generations, the whole kingdom said, and it had been a binding. Collars. Smoke. A beast standing empty in a palace while the kingdom bowed.

*The one realm fell for doing exactly that.*

He didn't say anything either.

---

By dusk the camp had it.

All of it. From the barn's door, from Davin, from Corrie, from the knights who'd stood guard and the clerks who'd fetched water. Round every fire, along three leagues of the Wend's bank. The gods are Titans who stopped. The old realm fell for chaining one.

*One again.*

Nobody said it after that.

Not one fire. She walked the lines that night and listened. Every Restoration pamphlet the camp had ever seen had six hands joined in a ring over those two words. Half the unchosen in it had said them once. Some had worn the ring.

Nobody said them now.

---

The old man told them the plan.

After that. With the knight and the farmer at the table's ends, saying nothing, and the Lamp's light coming in white-gold through the barn's door from the eastern meadow where it stood with its ring of unicorns.

The relic. The stone in the Last Titan's back. The old ones had made it, when they laid the six down. A safeguard. *It answers a breath that is unbound.* No person could hold it. No bound beast. No chained Titan, even with it grown into its back.

Only one of them.

"The Lamp will carry it," said Idony. "If the Lamp takes it out of the last one's back, the six will hear. They'll come into the light with the one who holds it." He looked round the table. "And stop. As the old ones stopped."

---

"How does it get to it?" said Arn.

"We take it there," said the farmer.

His first words. Cracked. From the table's end. Everyone turned.

"The Accord holds the six," said the farmer. "Together. In one place. Long enough. And the Lamp goes in and takes it."

Rowan looked at him. Then at the knight at the other end.

The knight nodded.

---

Pike asked her question at the end.

When the plan had been said, and the table was quiet, and Thale had his hand still flat on the door and Mattias still had his face in his hands. Pike put her spectacles up on her forehead.

"Master Vael," she said. Very politely. "One thing. For the record."

The old man looked at her.

---

"And afterwards?" said Pike. "When the Lamp has carried the stone into the light. Does the stone go too?" She tilted her head. "Or does it remain? For keeping?"

The old man thought about it.

"I'd think it goes," he said. "It's theirs. They made it."

"Of course," said Pike.

She took her spectacles down and put them on her nose. "I ask only for the record. The clause, you'll recall. Titan-matter and relics, after the war. Into the Assembly's keeping." She wrote something in a small book. "If it goes, it goes. Of course. Thank you, master."

---

Rowan noted it.

She noted everything Pike said. She'd learned to. She sat at the end of the door on its trestles with the Lamp's light coming in white through the barn's door and thought: that's Kessmoor. That's Kessmoor being Kessmoor. It measures everything. It asks what happens to everything afterwards. It writes it in a little book.

She thought nothing else of it.

She'd think of it later.

---

Kit's barge came later, on the two-hundred-and-twelfth.

The second Merrin barge. Up the Wend from the Staithe. With a lean weathered woman with a crooked nose at the tiller, and its hold full, and its deck full of green.

Keepers.

Thirty of them. The last of the Wyldmarch's Keepers, from the dead Thornwood's western edge, who'd walked to the Wend and waited at the Staithe for anything that would take them on, and been taken by an unsalted reef-pilot at the Accord's cost.

And their wolves.

---

The barge tied up at the camp jetty.

The wolves came off first. Before the Keepers. One at a time, down the plank, grey and lean and silent, onto the jetty. Thirty. Forty.

They didn't stop at the jetty.

They went up the bank, through the camp, between the tents and the fires, and the people stood aside to let them pass. Up to the eastern meadow. To the Lamp, in its ring of unicorns.

And lay down. In the grass. Facing it.

The woman at the barge's tiller watched them go. Then she got down and tied off her own lines and went to find the old man.

---

They paraded the banners on the two-hundred-and-fifth.

On the water-meadows. The Accord's first muster. Every kingdom in a line along the river, with its beasts, under its colours.

Hearthmere's grey and gold, the crown's. Sarovel's white and gold, the Sun on the Stair, carried by an unchosen tribune with ink on his fingers. Isvaren's blue. Kessmoor's black and grey. Saltreach's rags: a dozen captains' pennants on one pole. The Wyldmarch's green, which they'd never carried as a banner before and had made out of a Keeper's cloak on a stonehorn's pole. The Clan's thread, red and grey, plaited together on one staff.

And the Marshal's.

Plain grey. No device. She'd had it made out of a crown-man's cloak.

---

The knight and the farmer were at the line's two ends.

Apart. Maren at the southern end, standing in front of the lion lying on the water-meadow behind her. The farmer at the northern end, below the ridge where the three hills lay with their heads on the turf.

They didn't look at each other.

They didn't need to. Everyone looked at them.

---

And in the eastern meadow, behind it all, the Lamp stood in its ring of unicorns and wolves, too bright to look at.

And in the river, the Lady lay with her head on the water by the pontoon.

Rowan rode down the line on her grey horse with the white blaze, slowly, past every banner, and didn't kneel to anything.

She'd done that once. For them.

Once was enough.
