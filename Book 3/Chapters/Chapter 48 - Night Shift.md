---

They finished it on the two-hundredth.

Nobody said so. There wasn't a bell. There wasn't a word from the clock-hill. Strake came down the iron stair at the evening's six with a board under her burned arm and walked round the ring once, slowly, looking at every shackle, and wrote something on the board, and went back up.

That was all.

In the morning the orders said: *Break down for the trains.*

---

He'd been on nights since then.

Three hundred of his hands. The rest on days. The work didn't stop for dark at Ironhythe. Nothing did. Under the green lamps, in the Ring Yard, with the canal black on one side and the cinders red with firelight on the other, they took the Bridle apart.

---

It was harder than putting it together.

Putting it together you laid a thing on the ground and joined it. Taking it apart, everything was already joined, and heavy, and warm.

The engines were warm. All twelve. They'd fired them once each, in the last ten days, one a night, Strake and Pike standing by with their boards while the chimneys went up and the brass in the guts turned and the whole yard shook. Then they'd banked them. The fires low in the boxes. Kept warm.

You didn't let a heartstone engine go cold, Strake said. It didn't like it.

So they were warm when the rail-cranes came hissing down the yard on their wheels and lowered their great hooks, and the hands climbed up and made them fast, and the cranes lifted the engines off their sledges a hand's breadth at a time and swung them out over the cinders to the sidings.

---

The sidings ran along the Coldrun.

Twenty lines of rail side by side, beside the canal. And on them, iron flats. Low wagons, no sides, long as a barn. A flat for each engine. Two rail-engines to pull each flat when the word came. Ninety-six more for the chain.

The chain they cut at its shackles. Ninety-six lengths. Each length a flat-load. Each link as long as a man, and the hands carried them, as they'd carried them in, sixty to a length, in a line, with poles under the links and their shoulders under the poles, singing.

The ring had a double flat of its own.

---

He watched them lift the ring on the two-hundred-and-sixth.

In its timber cradle. Two men tall, waist-thick, black. Three cranes together, hissing. Up off the cinders. Out. Slowly. Swinging a little, so that the hands on the guide-ropes leaned their whole weight back and dug their heels into the cinders, and the lamplight went round inside it.

Inside it, the six hollows. In a circle. Egg-shaped. A hand's breadth deep.

He looked at them as it went over.

Then he went back for the next length.

---

The hands slept in the harvest sheds now.

That was new. They'd had the barracks across the canal, since the forty-fifth day. Long wooden sheds with bunks. Cold, but theirs. On the hundred-and-ninety-eighth a clerk came with a paper and said the barracks were wanted for the Hearthmere smiths. Two hundred more of them, up the Iron Road, with families. Hearthmere had smiths and nothing to feed them on. Kessmoor had bread.

The hands could go to the harvest sheds.

---

He hadn't known what they were until he walked his hands down to them.

Half a league down the Coldrun, on the east bank. Brick. Long. Six of them in a row, with chimneys, and a gate onto the road, and a yard. He'd smelled them from the works for half a year and not known what the smell was.

He knew at the gate.

---

Kessmoor bought beasts.

Dead ones. Dying ones. From the kingdoms that had been drained. They came up the Iron Road and in off the Coldrun by the cartload, from wherever there were beasts that couldn't be fed or had died in the cold or been bonded and weren't now. Cattle. Horses. Hounds. Once a stonehorn, on a flat of its own, with its plated head hanging off the end. Sometimes a bond-beast.

Kessmoor paid by the weight at the gate.

And in the sheds they cut the hearts out.

---

He saw it the first night. He couldn't not. The sheds were one long room each, open to the roof, and at the far end of each there were iron vats, six to a shed, with fires under them, and men in leather aprons and leather masks feeding them.

Hearts. Into the vats. Boiled. Down. For days. Until what was left at the bottom of the vat was a grey grit, like ash, like sand, that men raked out with iron rakes into iron trays.

Kessmoor pressed that into heartstone.

*Old stock.*

---

The hands slept at the other end.

On straw. In the empty half of the sheds, where the racks had been cleared. With the vats going at the far end all night, and the fires, and the men in masks, and the smell.

The clerk said it was warm. It was. The vats made it warm.

---

About a third of them wouldn't.

He didn't blame them. He didn't make them. The first night a hundred of his hands walked in through the gate and stood in the doorway of the first shed, and looked down it to the vats, and turned round and walked out again and lay down on the towpath under sacking, by the canal, in the cold.

By the tenth night it was a thousand.

He slept in the sheds. Somebody had to. Somebody had to be where the clerks could find him, and the bread came, and the names were called.

He didn't sleep much.

---

The bread came on time.

Every day. Every hand. A loaf each, warm, out of Moss's bakeries, on carts, at the sheds' gate at the morning's six and at the works' gate at the evening's six for the nights. Not once late. Not once short. Iron coin on the seventh day, counted into his hand by a clerk with a list, and he counted it out to them.

Moss had said bread. There was bread.

He was grateful for it. He was. Three thousand people alive who'd have been dead in Solenne's ruins by now, if he hadn't walked them here.

He hated it.

He'd have killed for it a year ago. He'd have killed for one loaf. He stood at the sheds' gate every morning and took the bread off the carts and gave it out and said *thank you* to the carter, and hated it so much he couldn't eat his own till noon.

---

Ines found the hide on the two-hundred-and-fourth.

She was one of his. Thirty, strong, quiet, from the low city's dye-works, where the water ran blue. She'd carried at the front of the chain-lengths since the first week, because she was strong, and never sang, because she didn't.

She'd gone to the far end of the shed for water. The pump was by the drying racks.

The hides hung on the racks. Every beast that came through the gate came out of it two ways: a heart in a vat and a hide on a rack. Horse. Cow. Hound.

She came back without the water.

He saw her face and went down.

---

A white hide.

Long. A horse's. Fine. The finest on the rack, white as milk, with a mane still on its neck-edge, and on the shoulder, burned into it, small, the size of a hand, a sun over a stair.

And under the sun, three letters.

"Corvaille," said Ines.

He didn't know it.

"A house," said Ines. "A little one. Knights. My brother was groom to the son." She looked at the hide. "That was his. The son's. When the Grace went the son went into the river and my brother went after him and they both drowned and the horse was left."

She looked at it a long time.

"They sold it," said Ines. "Somebody. Here. For the weight."

---

She didn't say anything else.

She went and got the water. She brought it back. She did her shift.

That night she lay down on the towpath under sacking with the others. And didn't come back into the sheds.

---

He watched Callis.

As the Marshal had said. *Watch him.* At the end of three lines on plain wax, from the Marches, two months ago. He'd kept the note. He'd watched.

There was nothing to see.

---

When they broke the Bridle down Callis went with the seventh engine. Onto its flat, in the sidings, as its fire-hand. Somebody had to keep the boxes fed while the engines waited. So he sat on the flat at night by the seventh's firebox with a shovel and a heap of the grey stone in a crate, and fed it. A little. Every hour. To keep it warm.

He slept under the flat in the day. Between the wheels. On sacking.

He ate his bread. He fed the box.

He didn't talk to anyone. Nobody talked to him.

---

On the two-hundred-and-eighth Tobin went past with a length.

Sixty of them under the poles, singing, in the dark, along the sidings toward Crane Three. He was at the back, where he always was. And as they went by the seventh flat, Callis looked down at him from beside the firebox, with the shovel across his crooked arms.

"You wrote to someone," said Callis.

Tobin didn't stop. He didn't look up. He said:

"Yes."

They were past. He thought that was the end of it.

"Good," said Callis. Behind him. Quietly.

That was all.

---

The order came on the two-hundred-and-ninth.

He nearly didn't see it. Nobody read the work-sheets. They came down from the office at the evening's six on a board, and the foremen read them out, and the hands did what was read, and the board hung on a nail by Crane Three all night, and nobody looked at it.

He looked at it because he was waiting for the bread.

---

*Lengths 61–64 to Crane 3.*

*Flat 12: axle-grease, all eight.*

*Central ring: inner face to be cut, per Drg. 41-c. College hand only.*

*Lengths 65–68 to Crane 4.*

---

He read it twice.

Then the bread came, and he gave it out, and went back to the lengths.

---

The College hand came the next night.

An old man. Small, bent, with a white beard cut square, in a Kessmoor coat that didn't fit him. Isvaren-trained, the hands said. From Vael. He'd come down the Iron Fells before Vael went quiet. Kessmoor had bought him too.

They'd put a screen round the ring on its double flat. Canvas, on poles. A lamp inside. And all night, behind the canvas, a small sound. *Tick. Tick. Tick.* A graver's hammer on iron.

---

He went past it at the third hour with a length.

The canvas had a gap at the corner where the wind had it. He looked through. He didn't mean to.

The old man was inside the ring. Kneeling in it. In the curve of it, with his lamp beside him and his graver in his hand, between two of the hollows. Cutting.

A line. Round the inner face. From hollow to hollow.

Hooks. Little bent strokes, each like a fish-hook, in a row, small and exact.

He'd seen that writing. He'd seen it at Greyhold, when he was a student, cut in the arch. He'd seen it on the crown's chain, in the songs. He'd never been able to read it.

He didn't stop. The length went on, and he went on with it.

---

Nobody read the work-sheet.

Nobody asked.

He didn't either.

---

The stage went up on the two-hundred-and-tenth.

Iron. A platform, bolted on top of the ring where it lay on its double flat. Railed round, waist-high. A step up to it. And at its front, facing in, over the ring's middle, an iron stand. Like a lectern in a chapel. Like something you'd put a book on, or a hand.

He looked at it a long time, from the cinders.

---

Wardle was night foreman on the Ring Yard.

A Kessmoor man. Fifty. Wide, mild, bald, who whistled. All night. Through his teeth. The same four songs. He never shouted. He never hit anyone. He read the work-sheet out at the evening's six in a pleasant flat voice as if it were the price of fish, and went round the yard all night with his hands behind his back, whistling, and if a length went down he helped lift it.

Tobin liked him. That was the trouble.

---

"Foreman," he said, at the fourth hour, by Crane Three, with the stage black against the lamps behind them.

Wardle stopped whistling.

"What's the stage for?"

Wardle looked at it. Over his shoulder. As if he'd only just noticed it was there.

He shrugged.

"Fallback," said Wardle.

And started whistling again.

---

He thought of Tam.

At the fourth forge. Ten weeks gone. Standing in front of a brass half-egg template with his hammer down, while the strikers looked at their own anvils.

*I'll not make a thing I can't see the use of. I did that once.*

He thought of that.

He thought of three thousand loaves at the sheds' gate at the morning's six. A thousand people on the towpath under sacking. Moss's list, with every name on it. The bread stopping.

He let it go.

He went back for the next length.

---

The news-wagons came on the two-hundred-and-thirteenth.

Up the Iron Road from the Marches. Three of them, with the Accord's seal on the tail-boards, and printed sheets in bundles, and a clerk on each who stood on the box at the works' gate in the morning and read it out.

Tobin heard it on the towpath. He'd come off the night and gone to give Ines her bread, because she wouldn't come to the sheds' gate for it any more.

He heard the clerk across the canal. Loud. Flat.

*At the Accord's council, in the Marches, on the two-hundred-and-first day.*

*The gods are Titans who stopped.*

---

He stood on the towpath with the loaf in his hand.

The clerk read on. All of it. The Lamp, walking across the Wend on the light. The Lady in the river. The beasts turning. The script on the far shore. *We woke, and drank. We stopped.* The one realm. A Titan chained under a city. The fall.

*The one realm fell for chaining one.*

And then, near the end, read out like the rest:

*The olifants of Sarovel knelt in their traces.*

---

Half of Ironhythe laughed.

He heard that too. From across the canal. From the works' gate, from the canteens, from the clerks on the iron stair. Kessmoor laughed. Not unkindly. As you'd laugh at a story about a talking goat. Kessmoor didn't pray. Kessmoor had never prayed. *Kessmoor measures*, the clerks said, and they said it now, laughing, to each other, and somebody said *Titans, is it*, and somebody said *well, we've got an engine for that*, and they laughed more.

The other half went quiet.

---

The Hearthmere smiths' wives, at the barracks' doors, with their children. The old women of Ironhythe's low town, who still kept a lamp burning in their windows to the Sun on the Stair because their grandmothers had come up from Sarovel. Odo, he heard later, in his chair on the barracks' step, who didn't laugh, for once.

And his.

On the towpath. A thousand of them, under sacking, sitting up. Listening to the clerk across the black water.

*The olifants of Sarovel knelt.*

Not one of them said anything.

---

Ines took the loaf out of his hand.

She looked at it. Then across the canal at the works, and the sidings, and the long black line of flats under the green lamps with the Bridle on them. Then down the bank at the harvest sheds, with their chimneys going.

She didn't eat the bread.

She held it in both hands, in her lap, and looked at the water.

---

Wardle read the sheet that evening, at the six, before the work-sheet.

He read it in his pleasant flat voice, all of it, as if it were the price of fish. Then he folded it and put it in his coat.

He didn't whistle for an hour.

Then he started again.

---

The last length went onto its flat on the two-hundred-and-fifteenth.

At the fourth hour of the night. Length ninety-six. Sixty of them under the poles, and the crane's hook coming down, and the length going up off their shoulders into the dark, and swinging out, and down, onto the last flat on the last line of the sidings, with a sound like a door shutting.

Then there was nothing left on the cinders.

---

He stood on the Ring Yard with his hands.

Empty. A quarter-mile of cinders and iron plates, flat, and the marks in the cinders where the sledges had been, in a circle, and the spokes.

And along the Coldrun, in the sidings, the Bridle.

In pieces. On flats. Twelve warm engines and ninety-six lengths of chain and a ring with six hollows and a line of hooked script round its inside and an iron stage on its top. In a line half a league long under the green lamps, with the fire-hands on the flats feeding the boxes. Waiting for the word to go down the Iron Road.

*Fallback.*

---

He walked down to the towpath at the morning's six.

Past the harvest sheds. Past the gate, where the first carts of the day were coming in with their loads under sacking and their wheels creaking with the weight. Past the chimneys.

To the canal bank. Where a thousand of his people were lying under sacking in the cold, rather than sleep beside the vats.

He found Ines. He lay down next to her, on the towpath, on the cold mud, and pulled a sack over himself.

It was cold.

He slept.
