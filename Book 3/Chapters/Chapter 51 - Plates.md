---

The wolves found them on the second day.

Rhun's. Grey, low, going in among the fallen trees on the south side at first light with their noses down, where nobody else could go, because the walking wood had lain down all at once and there was a league of it lying every way on the black ground like straw after a storm.

At noon one of them stopped and lifted its head and howled.

---

He went in on foot.

He couldn't fly yet. Not low, not among trees. He could breathe, a little at a time, as long as he didn't hurry. He went in behind the Keepers, over the trunks, under them, his palms wrapped in wet cloth, Hobb behind him with the water-skin.

It took an hour.

---

Tamsin lay on her side.

Under four oaks, grey, fallen across her. Her grey-green wings folded under the weight. Her old scarred head along the ground.

And on her neck, Gunna.

Still there. Her hands flat on Tamsin's neck, as he'd seen her put them, and her head down on her hands. Grey hair over her face. As if she'd lain down to listen to something under the scales.

The Keepers took the oaks off them. It took twenty men and two bears.

He sat on a trunk while they did it, and didn't help, because he couldn't, and watched.

---

They found the green a league off. Bryn Tolle's. Small as a horse, under an ash, with its neck broken. They didn't find Bryn.

The brown was on the western edge. The woman who'd lived sat with it all afternoon.

---

The Clan poured at Coldtarn.

Always had. You burned your dead for seven days, or eleven for a chief, and raked them into a grey urn, and carried the urn up to the tarn, and poured. The ash went down into the black water that never froze. That was where the Clan's dead were. All of them. Sigrun Holm. Haldor. Ulric.

Coldtarn was a long way off.

---

Arn asked Tova.

Calder heard him do it. At the clans' fire that night, on the downs above the fallen wood. Arn standing, in his red thread, thinner than he'd been, with Ildra a red-gold shape behind him in the dark. Tova sitting, her stick across her knees.

"They died in your wood, Elder," said Arn. "Carrying your people. I'd not take them out of it without asking."

Tova looked at him a long time.

"Pour them here," she said.

---

The riders didn't like it.

He heard that too, after, in the dark, among the dragons. Not Coldtarn. Not the tarn. In a dead wood, in the Wyldmarch, where nobody'd ever poured. A grey-thread man said it to Arn's back.

Arn turned.

"Ulric poured with a farmer," said Arn. "Gunna laughed for an hour." He looked at the fallen wood. "She'll not mind a wood."

Nobody said anything after that.

---

"It will need something to grow from," said Tova, to nobody, at the fire.

---

They burned them on the two-hundred-and-thirtieth.

No seven days. There wasn't wood. There was a league of it lying on the ground, and nobody would touch it to burn. And there wasn't time. And there was Kharrak.

He stood on the downs with the Clan in a ring and Kharrak's head lying beside him on the grass.

*Small*, he said.

*I know.*

---

It couldn't be small. White, out of Kharrak's mouth, onto the old dragon and the woman on her neck where the Keepers had laid them together on the burned ground. The heat came back across the downs so hard that the ring stepped back three paces as one.

It was enough. That was what he'd meant.

An hour. Not seven days. An hour, and the old grey-green dragon three hundred and twelve years old, and Gunna Tarne of the ledges, who'd bowed to a lion the old way and laughed down a stair, were white ash on black ground.

Then the green. Then the brown, with the woman standing close, closer than she should, with her face red from the heat and not stepping back.

---

The riders raked them up.

No urns. Not this far from home. The Keepers brought four clay jars, round and brown, that they kept their seed in. Rhun gave them. She didn't say anything.

---

They poured at dusk.

On the fallen wood's western edge. Where the grey trunks lay every way on the black ground, a league of them, and the first stars were coming out over them, and it was very quiet.

No priest. The old priest was at the Wend, too old to fly.

Arn said it.

---

"Gunna," said Arn. "Daughter of Tarne. Rider of the ledges."

He stopped. He looked at the jar in his hands.

"And Tamsin," said Arn. "Who carried eleven. Twice."

---

Then he said the other thing. The thing they always said.

"Who pours?"

---

Nobody answered.

Gunna had no one. No child. No husband living. The ledges' riders were her people, and most of them were here, and none of them moved, because it wasn't theirs to.

Arn stood with the jar.

Then he looked across the ring, in the dusk, at Calder.

"Pour with me," said Arn.

---

He went out.

His breath short. His palms in their cloth. Across the ring, over the black ground, and nobody said *he's not Clan*. Not this time. He put his wrapped hand on the jar beside Arn's two.

Ildra came behind Arn's shoulder. Small. Red-gold. Her head low, against him.

They tipped it.

---

The ash came out pale. Onto the black ground, among the fallen grey trees. It lay there. It didn't go down, as it went down in Coldtarn. There wasn't any water. It lay on the ground in a pale heap in the dusk, and the wind didn't take it.

And the dragons made their sound.

All of them. Arn's forty. The freed ones on the downs. The three. Low. Long. The sound they made at the tarn, that wasn't a roar and wasn't crying, that went down into the ground.

---

Then Lisska put her head down.

He didn't tell her to. Her great green head, the size of a barn, came down past him in the dusk over the heap of ash on the ground, and she put her nose to it, very close.

And breathed on it.

Small. The smallest wind he'd ever felt off her. A breath.

The ash went into the ground. Between the black crumbs of the burned earth, down, as it went down into the tarn, and was gone.

*There*, said Lisska. *Now it's like the water.*

---

He looked for Maren.

He didn't mean to. She wasn't at the pour. She was at the edge of the downs, with the Keepers, by the lion, who lay with his left foreleg held out in front of him. Standing. A long way off. In her mended coat.

She didn't come closer. He didn't go.

---

The carts came on the two-hundred-and-thirty-first.

He heard them before he saw them. Wheels. A lot of wheels. And a sound under the wheels that he knew from Ironhythe and from nowhere else: a hissing, and a thump, and a hissing.

Up the east road out of the west. Onto the downs. Forty wagons. Kessmoor's, black, with iron tyres and the Assembly's mark painted on their tail-boards. Two hundred men walking beside them in leather aprons. Saws on the wagons, long ones, two-man and four-man. Picks. Jacks. Chains.

And two things at the back of the line, on wheels, with chimneys, hissing.

---

The man at the front got down off the first wagon and walked across the downs to the Clan's fires.

Square. Red-bearded. A leather case under his arm. He'd seen him at the ferry house. Pike's man.

"Master Fenn," said Engineer Garth Tolley. "Under the clause. We've come for the recovery."

---

"You were quick," said Calder.

His voice. Cracked. It still hurt.

"We left the Wend on the two-hundred-and-fourteenth," said Tolley. "A week behind the clans. It was always the plan, Master Fenn. If the kill was made." He nodded at the fallen wood, and beyond it, the great still shape lying under its crown. "It was made."

He looked pleased. Not unkindly. As a man looks at a field when the harvest's in.

---

"What are those?" said Calder.

He nodded at the two hissing things at the back of the line.

"Winches," said Tolley.

"The Marshal said no engines in the Thornwood."

"No engines in the battle, Master Fenn. They're winch-carts. For lifting." Tolley opened his case. "I have the minute." He found it. He read it out. "*No engines in the Thornwood.* Yes." He closed the case. "They're winches."

---

They started on the plates at noon.

He watched from the downs. He couldn't stop watching.

The Antlered lay where it had died. On its side, a hill, in the fallen wood, with its head in the ground to the antlers' roots and the crown lying across the stone like a second fallen forest. Mossed, grey-brown. He'd thought the moss was all there was.

It wasn't. Under the moss, it was plated. Like bark on an old oak. Each plate the size of a door, grey-brown, ridged, overlapping the next.

Tolley's men climbed up onto it with their saws.

---

Four-man saws. Two men on each end, on ropes, on the great curved flank. Sawing.

The sound came across the downs all afternoon. A long dry rasp, and a pause, and a rasp. Like men cutting a tree that wouldn't fall. And now and then a crack, and a plate coming loose, and the winch-carts hissing, and the chains going taut, and the plate lifting off the flank and swinging out and down onto a wagon.

Grey. Door-sized. Ridged.

By dusk there were nine on the wagons.

---

He sent Hobb to ask.

"What do they want the plates for?"

Hobb came back. "He says study, Master Fenn. And safe custody. Under the clause."

"Ask him what they'll make."

Hobb went. Came back. "He says that's for the Assembly."

---

The heart was under the stone.

Where Maren had closed it. Between the antlers' roots, in the bone, in the ground. Tolley walked round the place where the skull went down into the earth, slowly, with his case, measuring it with his eye.

Then he sent Hobb to her.

---

Hobb went across the downs in the dusk to the edge where the lion lay. He came back slower.

"He asked her to open the ground," said Hobb. "So they can get at it."

"And?"

Hobb looked at his feet. "She said: *Tell him the ground's closed.*"

---

They started digging in the morning. With picks. Two hundred men, round the skull, in shifts, through the burned earth and into the stone, all day, and all the next.

Tolley never said what they were digging for.

He said *the find*. He said *the matter*. He said *the recovery, under the clause*. He said it all day, to his foremen, in a pleasant voice, with his case under his arm.

He never said *heartstone*.

Nobody did.

---

*Let me*, said Kharrak.

On the second night. Lying on the downs with his chin on the grass and his gold eyes on the carts, forty of them, lit by Kessmoor's green lamps, with the plates stacked on them. Nineteen now.

*Let me. Small. Just the carts. Not the men.*

---

*No*, said Calder.

*Why?*

He thought about it. He wanted to. He wanted to so much that his palms hurt, under the cloth, as if they could feel the fire coming.

*Because then it's us*, he said. *Not them. Us. Burning Kessmoor's carts in the night. And Rowan's word's nothing. And the next time it's Kessmoor burning something of ours, and saying the dragons started it.*

Kharrak was quiet.

*I don't care about Rowan's word*, said Kharrak.

*I know. I do.*

---

*He's right*, said Lisska. From the crag of herself, behind them, in the dark. *I hate it. He's right.*

Aurveth said nothing. She was looking at the skull in the ground, through it, with the deep sight, at something he didn't want to see.

---

Tova read the clause herself on the second day.

She walked down to the carts on her stick with Rhun beside her and her grandsons behind, and stood in front of Tolley among the plates, and asked to see it. Tolley gave her the book. She read it slowly, with her old finger under the line.

Then she turned back to the other page. The one with the little horse on it.

*No more heartstones. From living beasts. Not one. Not a bird.*

---

"I said no heartstones," said Tova.

Tolley looked at the page over her shoulder. He read it out. Pleasantly.

"*From living beasts*, Elder," he said. "Yes."

He took the book back. He looked at the great dead shape in the fallen wood.

"It's quite dead," said Tolley.

---

They flew to the Wend on the two-hundred-and-thirty-second.

He and Arn and Tova. The three at the roc's pace, because of Tova; she sat Yeva's roc beside them, wrapped in felt, her face set, not looking down. Two days.

The clans would walk it in a fortnight, going home. The carts would follow them. Slower. Loaded.

---

The council sat on the two-hundred-and-thirty-fourth.

In the open, on the water-meadow, as it had when Maren made them. The barn door on its trestles in the grass. The place where the lion had lain was empty, a long flattened shape in the meadow, the grass not yet up.

Rowan. Thale. Pike, with her spectacles. Mattias. Rennick. Aud Hallin with her one eye. Arn. The old man in his chair with Davin. Tova.

And Calder. Standing. He didn't sit at Rowan's tables. He stood at the end.

---

He said it badly.

He knew he would. He wasn't a speaker. His voice was a crack in a pot. He stood at the end of the door and said it anyway, all of it, in the cracked voice, and they leaned in to hear him.

"You're cutting it up," he said. "A god's kin. The Lamp's kin. The Mother's. For lamp-oil."

---

Pike put her spectacles up on her forehead.

"For study and safe custody, Master Fenn," she said. Mildly. "At the war's end. As signed." She opened her book. She turned it round on the door so that he could see it. "By you."

His own name. Big. Square. CALDER FENN, under his card, *the dragons' farmer*, in the Hall of Measures, under the clock.

He'd signed it. He'd signed it without reading it, because Arn had, and he'd wanted to be back at Corsa before the tide.

---

"The war's not ended," he said.

"The recovery is begun now," said Pike, "so that the matter's safe. Things go missing in a war, Master Fenn. You'll have heard of a Halloran house." She closed the book. "The clause covers custody from recovery. Clause four. You'll find it."

He wouldn't find it. He couldn't read the clauses. He could read his name.

---

Tova spoke.

"I said no heartstones," she said.

Not loud. She never was. She sat at the door with her hands flat on it.

"I put my mark on it. In the Hall. A little horse. *No more heartstones. From living beasts.* Your guildmaster accepted it. You accepted it. *We'll find other fuel*, you said. Very fast."

"I measure fast," said Pike.

"Yes," said Tova.

---

"From living beasts, Elder," said Pike. Gently. As if to a child. "The minute is exact. Kessmoor keeps to the minute. It is not a living beast. It is dead. Master Fenn and the knight killed it, at the Accord's word." She put her hands together on the door. "We take nothing from any living beast. We never shall."

Calder thought of a smell. He'd heard Sarovel's hands in the gallery at Ironhythe talk of it once, a smell down the canal.

He didn't say it. He didn't know it.

---

Tova stood up.

Slowly. On her stick. She looked down the table, at all of them. At Pike. At Thale. At Rowan.

"Then the Wyldmarch has nothing to say to this table," said Tova.

She turned and walked away. Across the water-meadow. Up the bank.

And Yeva got up and went after her. And on the bank, the Keepers in their green, and their wolves, got up off the grass, and went after Yeva.

---

They didn't leave the camp.

He watched them go to their own fires, on the camp's eastern side, by the stonehorns' meadow. They sat down there. They didn't pack. The clans were still on the road from the Thornwood; the Wyldmarch had nowhere to go that wasn't dead.

They didn't come back to the table.

---

Rowan didn't move.

She sat at the door's head with her hands flat on it and watched them go, all the way up the bank, and her face didn't change.

Then she looked at Calder.

"The clause stands," said Rowan.

---

"Why?" he said.

"Because the Accord's word is all we have." She didn't raise her voice. "I've no army, Master Fenn. I've no crown. I've a door on trestles and seven kingdoms' marks on a page. If I break the clause the day it costs us, every kingdom at this table breaks the next one the day it costs them." She looked down the table at Pike, and Thale, and Rennick. "I'll not be the first."

"It's wrong."

"Yes," said Rowan. "Probably."

She looked at him.

"I'll not be the first," she said again.

---

He found her after. In the barn's yard, in the dark, alone, by the water-trough, with her greyhart drinking.

"I know," she said, before he'd said anything.

"Then why?"

She was quiet a long time. The greyhart lifted its head out of the trough and looked at him with water running off its muzzle.

"Because I signed it," said Rowan. "And so did you."

---

He slept in the barn. On straw, in the loft, as he had at the muster. The three on the ridge. Wren and Ada half a day's flight north-east, at the caves.

He didn't sleep.

On the two-hundred-and-thirty-sixth, in the night, he heard the old man.

---

Through the wall. In the room next to the loft, where Idony slept and Davin wrote and a brass lamp burned on a shelf among the glasses. The reading glasses. Tide-glasses. Eight of them, now, in a row: sealed glass, a drop of quicksilver in each under a rune, that rose and fell with the Deep, and that only he could read.

He heard the old man's voice. Thin. High.

Shouting.

---

"*Again.* Read it again."

Davin's voice, low. Numbers.

"No. *No.* That's not right." A cup going over. "That's not the right way. It's going the wrong way, Davin. Read me the fourth again. Read me the *fourth*."

Numbers.

"West," said the old man. "Oh, no. No. *West.*"

---

He lay in the straw and listened to the old man shout numbers at a glass in the dark, and didn't understand any of it, and felt Aurveth on the ridge outside lift her head, and turn it, slowly, to the west.
