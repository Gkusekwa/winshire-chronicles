---

On the two-hundred-and-thirty-sixth Idony sat in his room at the camp with seven of the eight logs in front of him, and waited for the last.

He'd asked for the riders on the two-hundred-and-seventh.

The day before the clans marched. He'd gone to the barn in his blanket, with Davin, and asked the Marshal for eight of her knights for twenty days, and eight horses, and one thing each to carry.

"What thing?"

"A glass," he'd said.

---

She'd looked at him as soldiers looked at him. Patiently. As at a man asking for eight horses to carry eight cups.

"Master Vael."

"When it dies," he'd said, "something will happen. I don't know what. I've never seen one die. Nobody has." He'd put his hands under the blanket so she wouldn't see them shake. "I want to see it move. Whatever it is. I want glasses on the road, a day apart, from the Thornwood to here and on past here. And a knight at each one writing down how high it stands. Every quarter of an hour. By the sun."

"For how long?"

"Until it's dead. And a week after."

Rowan had looked at him a while longer.

Then she'd given him six knights, and Hobb to make seven.

---

So there'd been eight glasses on the road.

Sealed glass, the length of a finger, a drop of quicksilver in each under the rune that rises with the Deep. Packed in wool. Carried out along the road by six of Rowan's knights and a clerk's boy.

The first at the Thornwood's western edge, with Hobb. The second at the Wyldmarch border, where the dead country ended. The third at Skarrow, on the Scarfell's east side. The fourth at Varrhold's gate. The fifth here, on the shelf in his room, with Davin. The sixth on Greyhold's fallen hill. The seventh at the Mere's eastern shore, at a drowned farm called Sallow End, where a knight sat in a hayloft above the water for twenty days with the Last Titan lying a league out under it.

And the eighth at the Staithe. Off the line. To be sure.

---

They'd brought the logs back one by one.

The Staithe's first, on the two-hundred-and-thirty-third. Sallow End's on the thirty-fourth, the knight grey-faced from sitting above the Mere. Greyhold's. Skarrow's. The border's. Hobb's from the Thornwood, on the thirty-fifth, carried by a Clan rider.

The fourth came last.

On the two-hundred-and-thirty-sixth, at night. A young knight with a cough, off a horse that had gone lame on the moor. And Davin had read it out, and he'd shouted.

He wasn't proud of the shouting.

---

He'd had it wrong. That was the shame of it.

He'd thought it would go free. He'd said so, to himself, to Davin, for twenty days. When a Titan died, its breath would go back into the ground it came from. Into the Deep. Into the Wyldmarch. And the dead wood would get something back. A little. As a field gets something back from a dead beast in a ditch.

He'd thought he would see one glass leap, at the Thornwood, and fall, and nothing else.

---

He pinned them to the barn wall in the morning.

The eight logs. Davin's columns and six knights' columns and Hobb's, in a line, east to west. Quarter-hours down the side. Heights across. He couldn't hold a pin. Davin held them, and he said where.

Then he stood back from the wall in his blanket and looked.

---

Noon on the two-hundred-and-twenty-eighth.

The Thornwood's glass leapt.

Hobb had written it in letters a finger high. *UP. TO THE TOP. THE GLASS HURT MY HAND.* And a quarter-hour after, in smaller letters, shaking: *down again.*

---

He moved his eye one log west.

The border. A quarter-hour after the Thornwood's noon. A leap. And down again.

Skarrow. A quarter-hour after that. A leap. Down.

Varrhold's gate. The fourth. The one he'd waited for. A quarter-hour after. *Leapt. Fell.*

---

The camp.

His own glass. Davin's columns. On the two-hundred-and-twenty-eighth, an hour after noon, Davin had written a figure that was nearly the top of the glass. And a quarter-hour later a figure near the bottom.

Davin had logged it. Neatly. Every quarter-hour.

Nobody had looked. He'd been in the barn with the Marshal that noon, arguing about carts.

---

Greyhold's hill. A quarter-hour later. Up. Down.

And Sallow End.

---

The knight in the hayloft at Sallow End had written: *Up.*

And on the next line, a quarter-hour later: *Up.*

And on the next: *Up.*

And on every line after it, every quarter-hour, for four days, until the knight had packed the glass in its wool and ridden east: *Up. Up. Up. Still up.*

---

He looked at the eighth. The Staithe. Off the line.

Flat. All twenty days. It hadn't moved once.

---

"It went down the road," said Davin.

Quietly. Beside him. He'd seen it too. He was cleverer than people knew; he'd been a clerk's boy in a corn exchange and he could read a column.

"It went down the road," said Davin. "Like a cart. A quarter-hour a station. From the Thornwood. West." He put his finger on Sallow End. "To there. And it stopped there."

"Yes," said Idony.

"What's at Sallow End?"

He didn't answer. Davin knew.

---

It was like a wave going down a rope.

He'd seen it done, as a boy, on the ferry at Glasswater. You tied one end of a rope to a post and held the other and gave it a flick, and the wave went down the rope to the post. And stopped there. Because the post was holding it.

The Antlered's breath had gone down the road, west, a quarter-hour a station, through the Deep. Under the border country. Under the Scarfell. Under the camp, under his feet, while he argued about carts.

To the one holding the end.

---

He sent Davin up the ridge.

It was the two-hundred-and-thirty-seventh. Cold. The first frost on the water-meadow, white, and the Wend running grey past it. He couldn't climb the ridge. Davin went, with a board, and asked the farmer to look.

The farmer looked.

Davin came back at noon and read it off the board, in his clerk's voice, exactly as the farmer had said it, because Idony had told him to write every word.

---

"*It's brighter.*"

Davin read on.

"*The coal. In its back. Among the five. The five are the same. Dark. The coal's brighter. Like a coal somebody's blown on.*"

He read on.

"*And it's turned. On the bottom of the Mere. It was lying with its head west. It's lying with its head this way now. Toward us.*"

---

He sat down.

On the bench by the barn wall, under the logs. In his blanket. With his hands in his lap.

---

*It answers a breath that is unbound.*

He'd thought that was all it did. Answer. Hold. A safeguard, the Lamp had said. A stone the old ones made, so that one of them could hold it and call the six into the light.

He hadn't thought about what else a stone like that might do, in a back that wasn't theirs.

A dead Titan's breath went into the Deep. It had to go somewhere. Nothing was let go, in the Deep. Nothing came free. It went to the nearest of its kind. And the nearest of its kind, the one at the end of the rope, had the stone in its back that the six were tied to.

It went to him.

---

He'd been right about one thing.

He'd been looking. All twenty days, through the glasses and without them. In the Thornwood logs, in Hobb's careful letters. For anything new. In the ground. Anything small, stirring, where the great thing had been.

There was nothing.

There'd been nothing, he thought, in all the College's records. Nothing new in the ground anywhere. He'd never thought about that either.

Nothing comes free.

---

He went to Tova first.

Before the council. Before the Marshal. Because it was her wood, and she'd poured a rider's ash on it so that it would have something to grow from.

Davin carried his chair. Across the camp, in the frost, to the eastern side, where the Wyldmarch sat at its own fires by the stonehorns' meadow and didn't come to the table any more.

---

She was by the fire. On a stool. With her stick across her knees and a bowl of something in her root-knotted hands.

He told her.

Not the logs. Not the rope. He told her plainly, in as few words as he had.

"It didn't go into your ground, Elder," he said. "The breath. When it died. I thought it would. I was wrong. It went west. Under everything. To the one in the Mere." He looked at the fire. "I'm sorry. Nothing will grow from it."

---

Tova was quiet.

"Then what grows from the ash?" she said.

"Something," he said. "Ash is ours. It isn't theirs." He looked at his hands. "That was a rider and a dragon. That's ours. Something will."

She nodded.

She put her bowl down by the fire.

"I'll come to your council, master," she said. "Once. For this. Not for the table."

---

The council sat on the two-hundred-and-thirty-eighth.

In the barn, with the door shut against the frost. Rowan. Thale. Pike. Mattias. Rennick. Aud Hallin. Arn. The farmer standing at the back by the ladder, as he always did. Tova standing too, at the barn door, with her back to it, not at the table.

The knight wasn't there. She was on the road from the Thornwood, walking with the clans and the lame lion, a week out.

He didn't sit either. He stood by the wall, by the logs, and Davin held the lamp.

---

He told them.

All of it. The glasses on the road. The quarter-hours. Noon at the Thornwood and a wave going west down a rope, station by station. Sallow End. *Up. Up. Still up.* The Staithe, flat. The farmer's coal, brighter, like a coal somebody's blown on. The great shape on the Mere's bottom, turned round to face them.

They looked at the logs on the wall. Pike got up and went close to them, with her spectacles on her nose, and read every column, slowly, with her finger, and he didn't stop her.

---

"Every one we kill," he said, "he eats."

---

Nobody said anything.

Then Pike, from the wall, without turning round.

"Then we kill them all at once," said Pike. "Five. On the same day. At the same hour. Before he can take any."

"Then he eats all five at once," said Idony.

Pike turned round.

"It doesn't go to him over the day, Master Engineer," he said. "It goes to him in a quarter-hour a station. Look at the column. If five die at the same hour, he has five in his back by nightfall. And the coal burns like the sun."

---

Pike looked at him a long moment over her spectacles.

Then she nodded, once, as she might at a figure she'd been given and couldn't argue with, and sat down, and wrote something in her small book.

---

Rowan put her hands flat on the door.

"Then killing them is no use," she said.

"Worse than no use," said Idony. "It feeds him."

"Then the stone has to be taken," said Rowan. Slowly. "Out of his back. Not won by killing what he holds. Taken."

"Yes."

"By the Lamp."

"By the Lamp," said Idony. "Only by the Lamp. It was always the plan, Marshal." He looked at the farmer, standing by the ladder in the dark with his scarred hands at his sides. "Now it's the only one."

---

The farmer said nothing.

Arn said, after a while, very quietly: "Then Gunna died for nothing."

"No," said Idony.

He was surprised by how hard he said it. Everyone looked at him.

"No," he said. "Eleven Keepers walked out of that wood. And four wolves didn't, and an old dragon went back for them. That's not nothing." He found that his hands were shaking, under the blanket, and didn't care. "The Antlered was nothing. It was a hill that hurt. Gunna wasn't."

Arn looked at him.

Then he looked down at the table and nodded.

---

At the door, Tova had gone.

He didn't see her go. He looked up and the door was open a hand's breadth, and the cold coming in, and she wasn't there.

---

The turn came on the two-hundred-and-thirty-ninth.

All at once. As news did now. From five directions, by every way news could come.

---

A snow-hawk first. Out of the north, at dawn, to Aud Hallin's wrist. From a Warden on the Iron Fells' north side, in pencil, on a strip of birch bark. *The coil has left Glasswater. Going south. The lake is moving again. Ice breaking.*

Then a pigeon. Lady Ismay's. The last she had, she wrote, from Sorrel Isle, in her schoolroom hand. *The Sun has come down off the Stair. In the night. It's going north-east. Up the Sun Road. The whole road is light.*

Then the semaphore. Down the Iron Road from Ironhythe, arm to arm across the hills, read off at the camp's last tower by a Kessmoor clerk, and brought to the barn by Pike herself. *The Furnace out of the Scald. Going east. Not toward us. East.*

Then a gull-hawk from the Wend's mouth, where the Merrin barges lay, in Kit Merrin's quick bad hand. *The Bell's turned. Up the coast. North. Making for the Wend's mouth, I think. Whole sea's white.*

---

And his own glasses.

The camp's, on the shelf. Sallow End's, which he'd had a knight carry back to the Mere's shore and set again. The Last Titan was moving. Under the Mere. East.

And the others, one by one, as the riders brought them in.

Five breaths.

From five horizons.

All turning.

---

He sat up all night with them.

With Davin. With the lamp. With seven glasses in a row on the shelf and the strips of birch bark and pigeon-paper and semaphore copy laid out on the floor round his chair.

He drew it. Or Davin did, while he said where. Five lines on the old map. From Glasswater, south. From the Sun Stair, north-east. From the Scald, east. From the sea, north. From the Mere, east.

Five lines.

Going to one place.

---

He knew the place before Davin had finished drawing.

He'd known it all his life. He'd copied it himself, at the College of Tides, as a young man, on the founding chart. He'd carried that chart in oilcloth from Vael to Liss's ferry house and from Liss's ferry house to here.

He had Davin unroll it on the floor.

---

The tide-lines.

Every kingdom's. Drawn by the College three hundred years ago, in faded brown ink, the lines the Deep runs on under the ground, like the lines on the palm of a hand. Isvaren's, down from the north. Kessmoor's, from the west. Sarovel's, from the south. Saltreach's, from the coast. The Wyldmarch's, from the east. Hearthmere's.

All of them running to one place. Meeting. Crossing. Knotted.

In the middle of the Marches.

Under the Wend.

At the eddy where the island was.

---

The middle of the world.

The Oneroad's six ends met there. The old road, the road of the one realm, that went out to every kingdom. They met there because the tide-lines did. The one realm had built its road on the Deep's lines, because that was where the power ran.

And the Last Titan had been chained there. Under the island. For three hundred years. On the knot.

---

He sent for the Marshal in the last hour of the night.

She came. In her surcoat over her nightshirt, with her sword. She'd thought it was an attack. She stood in the door of his little room and looked at the old man in his chair and the clerk on the floor and the chart, and put the sword down.

"Master Vael."

He showed her.

---

"He doesn't need to break six kingdoms," he said. "He's learned that. He split the six and sent one home to each, and we held. Some of us. Badly. But we held." He put his shaking finger on the knot. "He doesn't need six. He needs one place."

He looked up at her.

"It's the middle of the world, Marshal. Every kingdom's Deep runs through it. All six. Here. Under the eddy." He breathed. "If the middle breaks, every kingdom's Deep goes at once."

---

Rowan looked at the chart for a long time.

"All of it," she said.

"All of it. At once. Everywhere." He sat back. "Every temple's waking, everywhere, on the same day. Every surge Vael had, and the Ring, and the Scald, in every kingdom, at the same hour."

"And after?"

"After the surge, the drain," said Idony. "For ever."

---

She didn't say anything.

She went to the window. A shutter on a wooden peg, in a barn wall. She opened it. The cold came in.

He looked past her, out of the little window. He'd lost the night. It was dawn. Grey. Frost on everything.

---

On the ridge above the camp, the three were lying as they always lay. Red. Bronze. Green. Their heads on the turf.

He watched them lift their heads.

All three. At once. As one. As three hounds lift their heads at the same sound. Up off the turf, on their great necks, and round.

West.

They stayed like that. Looking west. Not moving.

And between Kharrak's forelegs, small, in the grey light, a man standing, looking west too.
