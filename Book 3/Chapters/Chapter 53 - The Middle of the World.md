---

The lion walked on four legs again from the two-hundred-and-thirty-sixth.

Stiffly. The left foreleg put down with care, as an old man puts down a bad foot on a stair. But four. She sat on his head between his ears and felt each step come up through his skull into her, and counted them. Nine days out of the Thornwood. The clans strung out behind on the road west, three leagues of them, the stonehorns, the carts, the old people walking.

On the two-hundred-and-fortieth they were at the Candle Downs' east end. Burned country, still. Black stubble to the edge of the sky, and the first frost on it white in the mornings.

She counted that too.

---

Brede came on the two-hundred-and-forty-first.

Out of the west, low, on his old blue, with the red-thread girl with the healed lip on her brown behind him. He came down on the road ahead of the clans and walked back to the lion with his white beard in the wind.

"Knight," said Brede.

"Brede."

"Marshal's word. Two things."

---

The first she half knew. She'd felt it through Orrun on the road: a pull in the ground under everything, all turning one way. The five were moving. Toward the middle. The old man had found it on his glasses and told the Marshal in the night.

"And the second?"

Brede looked south.

"The Sun's come down off the Stair," said Brede. "It's coming up the Sun Road. And there's a column on the Sun Road in front of it."

---

Eight thousand.

Solenne's low city, what was left. And the people off Sorrel Isle, who'd taken boats back to the mainland when the Sun began to stir. They'd left on the two-hundred-and-thirtieth, walking north up the pilgrim road for the Wend, because that was where the Accord was, and the Accord had bread.

Perrin Sade had them. The low city's voice, from Liss's table, a lifetime ago.

Olifants. Twenty. Bond-steeds. Carts. The old people and the children on the carts.

"Where?"

"Two days off the Sun Road's mouth," said Brede. "When we saw them. That was yesterday." He looked at her. "The Sun's a day behind them. Less."

---

She left the clans with Tova.

She didn't need to ask. Tova was on her bay mare at the column's head, and looked up at her on the lion's head, and nodded before Maren had said anything.

"Go, knight," said Tova.

---

They went south.

At his own pace. Not the clans'. His great stiff stride eating the burned downs half a league at a time, the wind of it in her face, the frost going by under her in a grey blur.

*It hurts*, she said.

*A little.*

*Can you?*

*Yes.*

She counted his steps. She lost them. She started again.

---

The Sun Road came out of the Sunward Hills through a gate.

That was what the pilgrims called it. The Vintner's Gate. She'd never seen it; she'd only seen it on the map at Greyhold, in the schoolroom, a mark where the road went into the hills. A cut. A league long. Two steep hillsides, chalk and old stone, terraced with vines, coming down close on both sides, and the road at the bottom, and at the north end, where it opened onto the Downs, the long low beast-stables where the pilgrims had changed their mounts.

She came to its western lip at dawn on the two-hundred-and-forty-third.

---

And looked down.

---

The column was in the Gate.

All of it. Strung out a league along the road at the bottom of the cut, between the hillsides. The front of it coming out at the north end, past the stables, onto the Downs. The back of it still at the south end, where the road came in out of the hills.

People. Walking. Thousands. Carrying children. Pushing barrows. The carts, in a long line, with the old people sitting up on them in blankets. Bond-steeds, tall and white, led, shying. And at the back, the olifants. Sixty of them. Grey. Hauling the heaviest carts in their traces, slowly, at an olifant's pace, which is a man's.

And behind the olifants, coming up the road out of the hills, the day.

---

That was what she saw first. Day.

Not a shape. Light. A white-gold brightness on the road at the south end of the Gate, half a league across, as if a piece of noon had come loose from the sky and was walking up the road on its own. The vines on the terraces either side of it going black as it passed. Smoke coming up off them.

And in the middle of the light, a shape she could only see by not looking at it.

A horn. Long. Straight. Like a spear.

The Horned Sun.

---

It was coming up behind the olifants.

At a walk. Not fast. It didn't need to be fast. The column couldn't be fast. It walked up the road with its half-league of day round it, and the back of the column was in the light already, the last carts, the last olifants, white in it, and she heard the screaming start.

---

*Down*, she said. *Behind them. Now.*

He went.

---

Down the western hillside. Through the vines. A hill going down a hill, the terraces breaking under his feet, stone and earth sliding. Onto the road at the south end of the Gate, behind the last olifant, between the column and the light.

And out of him, beside him, the other.

One. As big as he. Grey. Stone. Rising out of the road beside him as if it had always been there under the paving, waiting.

Two lions across the Sun Road. Shoulder to shoulder. Facing south.

---

The light came on.

She felt it through both of them. Heat. Not fire. Heat like an oven's door opened, like the inside of Tam's forge with your face in it. On the lions' faces. On their manes. On their shoulders as it came up to them.

*Hold*, she said.

*Holding.*

---

It walked into them.

The Horned Sun. A hill of light with a horn in it. It walked up the road and put its head down and its horn came at Orrun's chest, and he turned it on his shoulder, and the horn went past him along the stone of his neck with a sound like a grindstone.

And it pushed.

Both lions slid. Back. Up the road. Toward the column. A pace. Two. Their claws in the paving, the paving cracking, coming up in slabs.

They held.

---

She was on the western lip.

Kneeling. Her one good hand flat on the ground. On the vines and the chalk and the stone under the chalk.

And she felt the hillside.

---

All of it. The whole western side of the Gate. A league of it. Chalk and stone and the old bones of the hills, hanging above the road, steep, held up by nothing but its own weight leaning on itself. She could feel exactly where it would go if she asked it.

Down. All of it. Onto the road. Onto the light.

She could bury the Sun. A league of hillside on top of it. It would take a breath.

---

And she could feel the column.

Under the hillside. All of it. The whole league of the road. Eight thousand people walking along the bottom of the cut under the western slope, from the stables at the north end to the olifants at the south. Under her hand.

If the hillside went, it went on them.

---

*Subdue*, said Pell.

---

She heard him. Not through Orrun. Through nothing. In her own head, in his own voice, from the front of the yard at Greyhold, with two fingers gone from his spear hand.

*Subdue. Don't kill. You take the wound. You take the risk. You take the long way round.*

---

She took her hand off the ground.

---

The olifants knelt.

She saw it from the lip. The last ones, at the back, at the edge of the light. As Sarovel's forty had knelt on the camp road at the muster. One. Then another. Their great front legs folding. Going down in their traces on the road, with the carts behind them, facing south. Facing the light.

Their god. Come up the road behind them.

They wouldn't move.

---

The carters couldn't shift them. She saw them try. Small men with goads, pulling at the harness, shouting. The olifants knelt with their trunks curled and their eyes shut and didn't move.

And the bond-steeds went mad. The tall white horses, all down the column, that had been bonded and weren't. Rearing. Screaming. Breaking their leads. Running north through the column, through the people, knocking them down.

---

Then she saw a man running back down the column.

Small, from up here. Dark. Running against the crowd, south, toward the light. Shouting. And people turning to follow him.

Perrin Sade.

He reached the kneeling olifants, and got a knife out, and cut the first one's traces. And the people behind him took the cart's shafts in their own hands. Twenty of them. And pulled.

The cart moved.

---

They did it all down the line.

She watched them. Cutting the traces. Leaving the olifants kneeling in the road. Taking the shafts. Twenty people to a cart. Dragging the carts north up the road, by hand, at a walk, away from the light, with the old people and the children on them.

And the two lions holding the Horned Sun at the south end of the Gate, sliding back a pace at a time.

---

It went on all morning.

She knelt on the lip with her hand off the ground and didn't help. There wasn't anything to help with. She could drop a hillside. She couldn't shift an olifant.

She watched Orrun's mane begin to smoke.

---

*It's hot*, he said. Very quietly.

*I know.*

*It doesn't want to fight me.*

*What does it want?*

*To go where it's called*, said Orrun. *It's called. Like the others. We're in the way.*

---

The Lamp came at noon.

Out of the north. Over the Downs. She saw it as she saw the Sun: by not looking. A second light, white-gold, coming down over the edge of the Downs above the stables at the Gate's north end. Walking down the eastern hillside on the light the sun laid on it. Along the terraces. Down. Past the column.

The people stopped dragging the carts. They looked up. They went down on their knees in the road, all of them, as their olifants had.

The Lamp walked down the length of the Gate on the eastern hillside, slowly, a league, and came down onto the road at the south end between the two lions.

---

And the Horned Sun stopped.

---

They stood face to face. Across the lions' shoulders. Two lights. Two horns.

Kin.

She'd known it, from the old man, from the far shore. *Kin to the Horned Sun.* She hadn't understood it. Now she did. She couldn't tell them apart, except that one was still and one was burning.

They didn't do anything. She couldn't see that they did. They stood face to face in the road, with the vines black on the hills either side, for a long time.

And the Horned Sun lowered its horn.

---

Then it turned.

Slowly. Its half-league of day going round with it. And walked back south. Down the road. Out of the Gate. Into the hills.

She felt it go through Orrun. Two days south. Into the Sunward Hills. And lie down there, among its own blackened vines, burning, and wait.

Not gone. Waiting.

---

The olifants got up.

---

The shadow went back into him at dusk.

On the road. When the column was out of the Gate and onto the Downs, and the light was two days south, and there was nothing left in the cut but kneeling marks in the dust and twenty olifants walking north on their own, without traces, after their people.

It went into him as breath goes in. And its burns came with it.

She felt him take them. All of them. The shadow's scorched mane and blistered shoulders. Into his own. He lay down in the road and shut his eyes.

*Two of me*, he said. *Both burned.*

*I know.*

*I'm glad it was only two.*

---

Perrin Sade found her on the lip in the dark.

He'd climbed up. He was out of breath. A thin dark man, younger than she'd thought, with dust to his knees.

He looked at her a long time.

"Your face is burned," he said.

She put her hand up to it. Her right hand. She couldn't feel her face; she couldn't feel the burn. She felt her palm touch something.

"On the right," said Perrin Sade. "Where it faced the road."

"I didn't feel it."

He nodded slowly, as if that were an answer.

"You could have brought the hill down," he said. "On it. You were going to. I saw you."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

"You were under it," said Maren.

---

They came in to the Wend on the two-hundred-and-forty-eighth.

The column behind the lion, five days on the burned Downs. Eight thousand. Twenty olifants walking loose at the back, following, and nobody tried to harness them again. At the Wend they went to the forty that had hauled Kessmoor's wagons since the muster and stood with them, and Kessmoor didn't get any of them back.

The clans had come in three days before.

---

Home.

She didn't know when she'd started calling it that. It wasn't. It was the place where the island had been.

---

The island was gone.

It had been gone since the night the Last Titan came up out of it, when the Wend's bed opened and swallowed it and closed again. She'd seen that. She'd stood on the bank and watched.

There was a pool there now. Wide. Slow. Where the river bent below Greyhold's fallen hill, the Wend spread out and went round and round on itself in a great grey eddy, a quarter-league across, turning. Nothing grew in it. Birds didn't sit on it.

The middle of the world.

---

Liss's ferry house stood on the Hearthmere bank above the eddy, as it always had.

And across the eddy, a rope.

---

New. She could see it was new. Pale hemp, three-strand, as thick as her wrist, running out from Liss's post on the Hearthmere bank across the turning water to a post on the Saltreach side. And the ferry on it. Liss's flat scow. Halfway across. With Liss at the windlass, in her shawl, hauling.

The old rope was gone. The Kessmoor hawser, black, that had run there since the year before.

---

"Chafed through," said Liss.

She'd tied up. She stood on the landing with her hands on her hips and looked up at the lion, and then at Maren, and didn't step back. Liss never had.

"On the post. On the fortieth. It's turning the wrong way, Maren. The eddy. Twice a day now. It never did that." She looked at the water. "Like a tide. Only there's no tide this far up."

"The two-hundred-and-fortieth?"

"Whenever it was. I'm not Davin." Liss sniffed. "The camp's rope-walk laid me a new one. Three-strand. It'll hold." She looked at Maren's face. "What happened to your face?"

"I don't know," said Maren.

---

The eddy turned the wrong way that afternoon. She watched it.

The whole quarter-league of grey water slowing. Stopping. Lying still for a long breath, flat as a plate.

Then turning the other way.

Liss's new rope went taut across it, and creaked, and held.

*He's coming*, said Orrun.

*I know.*

*Home*, said Orrun. *This is where his chain was. He's coming back to where his chain was.*

---

The council sat that night. In the barn. The camp was moving: tomorrow, three leagues up the Wend, to the eddy, and every tent and cart and fire with it.

The knight was at it. The farmer wasn't. He'd gone north to Coldtarn three days before, Davin said, to his family. Nobody said why.

---

Rowan said it plainly.

"We'll make the stand here." She put her finger on the old man's chart, on the knot of brown lines. "At the hub. Where the Oneroad's six ends meet. If he wants to break the middle of the world, he'll have to come to it. So will they all."

"And we'll be in the way," said Thale.

"We'll be in the way," said Rowan. "On both banks. A line across the Vale, west to east, river in the middle. The Lamp and the Lady and the lion and the three with us." She looked at Maren. "I need walls, knight."

---

"How long?" said Maren.

"From the river to the downs' foot. Each bank. A league each, near enough."

"How high?"

Rowan looked at her. "As high as you can," she said, "without dropping a hill on anyone."

---

She raised the first on the two-hundred-and-forty-ninth.

On the Hearthmere bank. At dawn. Kneeling on the frost at the Wend's edge, below the ferry house, with her right hand flat on the ground and her dead left in her coat, and Orrun lying behind her with his chin on his paws.

The Vale was dead ground. The Last Titan's. He'd drunk it for three hundred years in his chains. No words worked here. Nothing a college could teach. Only what came through the bond: the stone, moving through her, because it was Orrun's.

She didn't need words.

---

She pulled.

Not everything. Not a hillside. She'd learned that much. A line. From her hand, west. Along the ground. Under the frost, under the frozen grass, under the soil, to the stone. A line of stone a league long, from the river's edge to the foot of the downs.

Up.

---

It came up out of the Vale's floor like a grey back rising out of water.

Slowly. The frozen earth splitting along a league, in a long dark seam. Stone coming up through it. Grey. Rough. Waist-high. Knee-high first, then waist. Men in the camp stopping, all along it, with their arms full. Then man-high. Then higher. Twice a man's height. A wall. A rampart, broad as a road on top, going away west across the Vale from her hand, straight as a ruled line, to the downs.

She held it there.

Then let go.

It stayed.

---

She looked at her hand.

She'd had to look. She couldn't feel it. She'd felt the frost under it when she put it down, cold. She hadn't felt the cold go. She'd felt the stone come up through it, rough. She hadn't felt the rough go. And now, at the end, with her hand flat on the ground in front of her, she couldn't feel the ground.

She looked at her hand to know it was there.

It was there. Grey at the fingertips. Lying on the frost.

She couldn't feel the frost.

---

*Maren*, said Orrun.

*It's all right.*

*It isn't.*

*No*, she said. *But it's done.*

---

She did the second on the two-hundred-and-fiftieth.

On the Saltreach bank. From the river east, to the scrub. Liss took her over on the ferry, on the new rope, with the eddy turning under them the right way for once. Orrun swam it. He didn't like swimming. He did it.

She knelt on the far bank and put her hand down and couldn't feel anything at all, and pulled anyway, and the stone came up. A league. East. Twice a man's height. Broad as a road on top.

Two grey lines across the Vale. One on each bank. The river between.

---

She climbed up onto the eastern one at dusk.

She had to use her hand on the stone to climb and couldn't tell if it was holding. She watched it. She made herself watch it, every handhold, because she couldn't feel the grip.

She stood on top in the wind.

---

The Vale lay under her. Both walls. The camp coming up the Wend to the eddy, three leagues of it, on both banks, fires lighting. The eddy turning, grey, in the middle. Liss's lamp on the ferry. The lion lying at the foot of the wall below her with his scorched mane.

And out of the south, over the Saltreach bank, in the last of the light, wings.

---

Dragons. The Clan's. Red and grey and brown, a dozen, flying low and slow out of the south from the Saltings, from the coast, with baskets slung under them, and people on their backs.

And beside them, wyverns.

Nell Tallis's. Black and grey. Hooded, their riders bent low. With baskets too. And people. Flying in the same flight. Wing to wing. Dragon and wyvern and dragon. Over the Saltreach bank toward the camp, in a long uneven line, in the dusk, carrying the coast's last people in.

---

Brede came up the wall behind her. Slowly. Old knees.

He stood beside her and watched them come.

"Nobody alive's seen that," said Brede.

"Seen what?"

"That." He nodded at the sky. "Wyverns and dragons in the one sky. Not fighting." He was quiet. "My grandfather fought Saltreach's wyverns over the Saltings. His grandfather before him. Two hundred years." He watched them come down over the camp's southern edge, together, dragon and wyvern, landing side by side in the frost. "Nobody alive."

---

She watched them land.

Then she looked down at her hand on the top of the wall, the grey right hand, lying on the stone where she'd put it to steady herself.

She couldn't feel the stone. She couldn't feel the wind on it.

She counted the fingers, to be sure they were all there.

Five.
