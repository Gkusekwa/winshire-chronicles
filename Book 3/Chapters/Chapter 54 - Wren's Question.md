---

He flew north on the two-hundred-and-forty-fifth.

He didn't tell anyone why. He didn't know why, at first. There was talk of moving the camp up the Wend to the eddy. The knight was coming in from the west, with eight thousand people on the road behind her. Rowan wanted him at her council, standing at the back as he did, and he went to her in the barn's yard in the dark and said he was going home for three days, and she looked at him and didn't ask.

"Three days," said Rowan.

"Five."

"Five," said Rowan.

---

The three took him in two hours.

Up off the ridge at dawn, over the Marches going grey with frost, over Harrow's End, where the Fenn top field lay above the empty village with snow on its stone walls, over the Scarfell's edge. The first snow was on the tops. White on the moor's high places, the black of the heather under it.

Coldtarn by mid-morning. Black. In its bowl of crags. And round it, on the crags, the freed, who looked up as the three came down, and didn't fly.

---

Ada was at the cave mouth.

She always was. He didn't know how she knew. She stood in the doorway with her shawl over her head and her hands under it and watched the three come down on the rim, and then watched him walk down Kharrak's face to the turf, slowly, stopping once for his breath.

"You're thinner," said Ada.

"So are you."

"I'm old. I'm allowed."

---

Wren was in the cave.

At the warm spring, at the back, where it came up out of the rock steaming into its stone basin and ran away under the floor. She was sitting on its edge with her feet in the warm water and her milestone stone in her lap, the one he'd brought her from the far shore with the old words melted off it.

Eleven. She'd been nine, once, when it started. He didn't know where the two years had gone. Into the sea, some of them.

She looked up.

"Papa."

"Wren."

"Your voice is still wrong."

"Yes."

"Say something else."

"Wren," he said again, and she laughed.

---

Old Grey was in the back stall.

Joss's old dray-beast, grey, huge, quiet, who'd been bound once and made to kill and had come back. He lifted his head when Calder came past and looked at him with his ordinary brown horse's eyes, and put it down again into his hay.

---

Ada didn't sing that night.

He noticed it. He'd noticed it before, in the summer, when he lay on the pallet after the Lit Shore. She'd always sung. At the hearth, in the evenings, the old songs. *The old ones went up into the light, and the one fell into six.* He'd heard it all his life, at Harrow's End, every winter.

She didn't sing it now. She'd sung a little, after the summer. She hadn't at all since the news came up from the muster, the printed sheet with the Accord's seal on it, that a Clan rider had brought to the caves and Bel had read out at the fire. *The gods are Titans who stopped. The one realm fell for chaining one.*

*Till the six are one again*, the song ended.

Ada didn't sing it any more. She sat by the fire and mended, and the fire talked instead.

---

Wren asked at the spring.

The second night. The two-hundred-and-forty-sixth. Late. Ada asleep in her blankets by the fire, and the three outside on the rim, and the cave dark except for the coals and the steam coming up off the warm water, white.

He was sitting with his back against the rock and his feet in the spring, because it helped his breath. Wren was beside him. She'd been quiet a long time.

"Papa," she said.

"Mm."

"What happens if we lose?"

---

He didn't answer for a moment.

"We won't," he said.

Wren looked at him. In the dark. He could feel her looking.

"Say it twice," said Wren.

---

He opened his mouth.

He'd said things twice for her all her life. Lisska's words. *She says hello. She says hello.* Yes, he'd come back. Yes, he'd come back. Everything. She'd made him, since she was four, since Senna, because if you said a thing twice it was true.

He opened his mouth to say *we won't* again.

It didn't come.

---

She waited.

He sat in the warm water in the dark and couldn't say it. Not because it wasn't true. He didn't know if it was true. Because he couldn't say it twice to her and not know.

"I don't know," he said instead.

Wren nodded. As if that was what she'd expected. As if that was better.

"Nobody's said, have they," she said. "What happens. If."

"No."

"Somebody should," said Wren.

---

He lay awake after that.

On the pallet by the spring. With the steam going up white in the dark and Wren asleep against his shoulder, and her question.

*Somebody should.*

---

Nobody had.

He went through it in the dark. Every council he'd stood at the back of. Rowan's. The Accord's. At Ironhythe in the Hall of Measures under the clock, and in the barn on its trestles, and on the water-meadow in front of a lion's eye.

Every plan began the same way.

*When the Lamp takes the stone.*

The line across the Vale. The wards at noon. The sectors. The beasts in their places. The lion on the river. The three in the air. All of it to hold the five in one place long enough for the Lamp to reach the coal.

And then: *when the Lamp takes it*.

Nobody had ever said: *and if it doesn't*.

---

He knew why.

You couldn't. Not at a table. Not with ninety thousand people behind a river listening at the door. You couldn't stand up at Rowan's council and say *and when we lose, this is where we'll run*, because the next morning nine thousand people would be running there already.

So nobody said it.

So nobody had planned it.

---

He'd planned things before. Badly. Gullhaven's roofs, with white crosses and a bucket of whitewash. Corsa's hulks. Nobody else had, there either.

He lay in the dark and planned it.

---

North.

If the middle broke, the people would have to go north. Away from it. Not south, where the Bell was. Not east, into the dead Wyldmarch. Not west, into dead Hearthmere and whatever came out of the Mere.

North. Through Harrow's End, past the top field, up the Drover's Way to Teo's ford. Over the Scarfell's edge.

To here.

---

And then what?

The hold at Varrhold could take a few hundred, packed. The caves, a few hundred more. The ledges, nothing; they were ledges. In summer you could put people on the moor in tents and feed them on the flocks.

It wouldn't be summer.

He'd heard Rowan's clerks say it. The Accord would make its stand in deep winter, when the five came. In the snow. Whatever ran north would come up the Drover's Way into the Scarfell in deep winter with nothing.

They'd need somewhere warm. Under stone. Somewhere a Titan couldn't dig into.

He didn't know anywhere like that.

---

He told Ada on the two-hundred-and-forty-seventh.

In the morning, at the fire, with Wren out on the rim feeding Lisska bread she'd soaked in the spring, which Lisska ate to be kind.

He told her all of it. North. The Drover's Way. The Scarfell in winter. Nowhere warm.

She listened, mending, and didn't look up.

---

"Your father kept his head down," said Ada, when he'd done.

"I know."

"Paid his dues every year. Never spoke at a moot. Died in his bed."

"I know, Mam."

She bit the thread off.

"You never have," said Ada.

He didn't know if it was a reproach. He looked at her. She was looking at her mending.

"Where, then?" said Ada.

"I don't know yet."

"Then find out," said Ada.

---

He went down to the tarn on the two-hundred-and-forty-eighth.

To Aurveth.

---

She was on the pouring-stone. As she always was. Bronze, shining, enormous, lying along the black flat stone at the tarn's edge where the Clan poured its dead, with her chin on the water and both her wings straight.

He walked down the shore and sat on the stone beside her head.

*You want to know where it's warm*, said Aurveth.

*You heard.*

*I hear most things.*

---

Coldtarn never froze.

He'd known it all his life. Everybody did. The Clan burned its dead on the rim and poured them into the one water in the Scarfell that never froze, not in the worst winter, not when the burn froze solid to the bed and the snow lay to the crags' shoulders. The ash went down into black water.

Except once. The night the Coil passed, at the end of the spring, coming down out of the north-west. The tarn had frozen at its edges. In a ring. White, a hand's breadth thick, all round the shore.

And stopped. Short of the middle. The middle had stayed black and open, steaming in the cold, as if something under it were warm.

He'd seen it. He'd never asked why.

---

*Look*, said Aurveth.

He looked.

---

Through her. Down. Into the black water.

To her it was dark, and clear, and very deep, deeper than he'd thought, going down and down in the rock bowl of the tarn. Fish, a few, that Lisska had missed. Weed. Old bones on the bottom, white: the Clan's dead, three hundred years of ash gone to a grey silt on the floor.

And under the floor, rock.

And under the rock, warm.

---

He felt it. As he'd felt the heat of the Lit Shore through her, and the coal in the Last Titan's back. A warmth. Deep. Under the tarn's floor, under the rock, a long way down, running. Like a hot spring under a field. Like the spring at the back of the cave, but huge, and slow, and very old.

The Deep.

Running warm under Coldtarn. So that the water above it never froze.

---

*That's why*, he said.

*That's why*, said Aurveth. *I've lain here a hundred years and never looked. I didn't need to.*

*Is there more?*

*Wait.*

---

She looked west.

Through the tarn's western side. Where the crag came down steep to the water's edge, and at its foot the scree ran down into the shallows, grey, and Hollow Beck came out from under the scree and ran away south-west through the bowl's gap toward Varrhold.

Into the crag.

Rock. Rock. Rock.

And then lines.

---

Straight lines. In the rock. He saw them as he'd seen the walls under the Lit Shore's sand. Straight. Meeting at corners. A wall. Another, across from it. A floor between, flat.

Pillars.

Two rows of them. Square. Going away into the hill under the crag, in the dark, holding up a roof.

---

A hall.

Long. As long as two of Varrhold's halls end to end. Wide. Cut into the rock under the crag on the tarn's western side, under the scree, with the warm Deep running beneath its floor. Dry. He could see it was dry. Dust on the floor. Nothing on it. Nothing in it.

At its eastern end, steps. Going up. Into the crag's foot, under the scree, above where the beck came out.

And at the top of the steps, a door.

Shut. Sealed. Stone. Under fifty feet of scree.

---

*Somebody kept something here*, said Aurveth. *Once. A long time ago.*

*What?*

*I don't know. It's empty now.* She was quiet. *It's warm. It's under stone. A great deal of stone.*

*How many?*

She looked a long time.

*A few hundred*, said Aurveth. *Not thousands. A few hundred, if they lie close.*

---

He sat on the pouring-stone with his feet over the black water and thought about that.

A few hundred.

Not ninety thousand. Not the eight thousand the knight was bringing up the Sun Road. Not the clans.

A few hundred.

---

He couldn't open it.

He knew that before he'd finished thinking it. Fifty feet of scree on the door, and the door stone, and the stone old. Kharrak could melt the scree and the door with it, and the crag would come down. Lisska could blow the scree off and the crag would come down.

Only one thing opened stone without bringing the hill down.

---

He didn't go.

He thought about it. He sat on the pouring-stone all afternoon and thought about it. Getting up on Kharrak, flying south. Two hours. Landing by the lion at the eddy and asking her.

He didn't go.

They met when they had to. That was all. On the far shore because the gods wanted two hands. At Ironhythe because the kingdoms wanted to look at them. They didn't meet to ask each other things.

And he didn't want to see her face when he asked her.

---

*I'll go*, said Lisska.

From the rim. Above him. She'd been listening; she always was.

*She'll be angry.*

*I know*, said Lisska. *I like it when she's angry. She goes all still.*

---

Lisska went on the two-hundred-and-forty-ninth.

At dawn. Up off the rim, green, the size of a crag, and away south over the moor's edge into the grey, fast. Two hours. She'd have to land where the lion could see her. Kin-speech didn't carry over hills. Only in sight.

He sat on the rim and waited.

---

He heard it.

Faint. At the very edge of the bond, where it went thin with distance, as a voice goes thin across a valley. The shape of words, mostly. Lisska's, quick, green. Then the lion's, slow, deep, stone settling, so far off it was like feeling a cart go by on the road under the floor.

And then, through the lion, another.

Hers.

He couldn't hear the words. He could hear that it was hers. He could hear what it was like.

Cold. Still. Angry.

---

He saw it too.

For a breath. Through Lisska's eyes, at the bond's edge, grey and far.

The Vale. The camp, three leagues of it, on both banks. The eddy turning. And across the Vale, on the Hearthmere bank, from the river to the downs' foot, a long grey wall of stone, new, two men high, straight as a ruled line.

And on the wall, at the river end, a hill.

Lying along the top of the rampart. Grey. A great grey shape with its chin on its paws and its mane, scorched black on one side, hanging over the wall's edge. As if it had always been there. As if it were part of the land. A new hill on the Vale that nobody had named yet.

And against its foreleg, small, a woman. Asleep.

---

Lisska came back at dusk.

Tired. She came down on the rim heavily and put her head on the turf beside him.

"She's angry," said Lisska. Aloud. She did that, sometimes, when she wanted him to hear it in his ears too.

"I heard."

"She said yes."

"I heard that too."

---

*What did she say?*

Lisska told him. Exactly. She was good at that. In Maren's way of saying things, flat, with all the feeling taken out and put somewhere else.

*Tell him yes. Tell him I'll open it. If it comes to that.*

A pause.

*Tell him I hate him for asking.*

A longer pause.

*Tell him he's right.*

---

He sat on the rim with his hand on Lisska's nose.

*Is that all?*

*No*, said Lisska. *She said: tell him nobody else would have.*

---

He told Wren on the two-hundred-and-fiftieth.

At the spring. In the morning, before he flew south. She was sitting with her feet in the water and the milestone stone in her lap.

"I found somewhere," he said. "If we lose."

She looked at him.

"Under the tarn," he said. "There's a hall. Old. Under the crag. It's warm, because the tarn's warm under. If we lose, we go there. All of us. You and Gran and Bel and Pip and Nan. And Old Grey. And whoever we can."

"Can you get in?"

"The knight can. She said she would."

---

Wren thought about it.

"Is that true?" she said.

"Yes."

She looked at him. Steadily. As her mother had looked at him when she wanted to know if he was lying about the price of a ewe.

"Say it twice," said Wren.

---

"Yes," he said. "Yes."

He could say that one twice.
