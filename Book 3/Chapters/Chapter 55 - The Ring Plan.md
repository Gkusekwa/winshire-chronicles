---

They'd moved the camp six leagues in a day.

She'd thought it would take a week. It took a day. Ninety thousand people pick up a camp as water picks up a boat: all at once, and in the direction it's already going. On the two-hundred-and-forty-ninth she'd given the word at dawn, and by dusk the whole of it was strung along both banks of the Wend from the knight's new wall to the eddy, and the old camp downriver was trodden grass and ash and a great many lost boots.

Davin had counted the boots. She'd told him not to.

---

That was three weeks ago.

Now there were ninety-eight thousand. Davin's count, on the two-hundred-and-sixty-eighth. The column off the Sun Road. The coast's last people, flown in by dragons and wyverns together. The last farms of the Marches, who'd held on behind their own walls till the frost and then given up and come.

Two grey walls across the Vale, one on each bank, from the river to the high ground. And in the middle, the eddy, turning.

The middle of the world. She'd stopped thinking it was a strange thing to call a pool.

---

The council sat in Liss's hay-barn.

Above the new ferry house. On the Hearthmere bank, looking down on the eddy. It smelled of hay and of Liss's goats, who'd been moved, and complained about it from the next field. The door on its trestles had come up the river on a cart. Davin had carried the ink-pot himself.

On the two-hundred-and-seventieth, at noon, they sat to settle the line.

---

She'd drawn it on the floor.

In chalk. On the barn's flags, swept, the whole Vale from the downs on the west to the scrub on the east. The river down the middle in a long chalk curve. The eddy, a circle. The two walls. And the line, in short strokes, across both banks, with each kingdom's mark in its place.

She'd been up half the night doing it on her knees. Her knees told her so.

---

They stood round it. You couldn't see a map on the floor sitting down.

Thale, in his chain, leaning on a stick now. Pike, with her spectacles down. Mattias. Rennick. Aud Hallin with her one eye. Arn. The old man in his chair, which Davin had carried in and set at the map's northern edge so that he could see it.

The knight. At the map's foot. Grey, still, with her right hand in her coat now too.

And at the door, standing, not coming in, Keeper Rhun, with a wolf at each knee.

---

"The Wyldmarch," said Rhun. "The Elder sends me to say where we'll stand."

"Come in, Keeper."

"No." Rhun didn't move. "We'll stand. On your line. Where you put us. Not at your table." She looked at Pike, once, across the barn. "That's all I was sent to say."

"Then I'll put you where I'd have put you anyway," said Rowan. "On the Saltreach bank. Next to the river."

Rhun nodded. And stayed in the doorway. Listening.

---

She walked them through it.

West to east. With a stick, along the chalk.

---

Most of it they knew already. Hearthmere's knights at the western end, where the wall met the high ground, three hundred with the ones in from the north, the militia with them, and the greyharts to carry them back and forth along the bend all day; nobody wanted that end, and it was Hearthmere's war more than anyone's. Rennick's sellswords at the eastern end, where the wall met the scrub, with Black Ruth's wyverns and the Cranes' on the ground till they were wanted, paid from the Accord's chest on her seal.

She spent her time on the rest.

---

"Sarovel," she said.

Mattias straightened.

"The olifants," she said. "Sixty of them. As walls. They'll stand where I put them and not move. I saw them kneel in the road. They'll stand in the line the same." She looked at Mattias. "Will they?"

"They'll stand," said Mattias. "For their own. Not for a goad."

"Then their own stand with them. Your unchosen, on the bond-steeds."

"The steeds bolt," said Mattias. "At the Gate, they bolted."

"Then put them behind the olifants," said Rowan. "Where they've something big to bolt behind."

Mattias almost smiled.

---

"Then the centre."

She put the stick on the eddy.

---

"Kessmoor's batteries. Both banks. Round the hub." She looked up at Pike. "The engines. The bolt-throwers. The chilled chains, if it comes up under you, as the Furnace did at Gearmouth. Here. And here." Either side of the eddy, on both banks, in the middle of the line.

"Where the relic will be," said Pike.

"Where the relic will be," said Rowan. "If the Lamp brings him down here, he comes down in the eddy. In the old place. You'll be either side of it."

"Agreed," said Pike.

---

"The Saltreach bank."

She crossed the chalk river with her stick.

"Kessmoor's second battery, at the river. Then the Wyldmarch." She looked at the door. "Next to the river, Keeper."

"Next to the river," said Rhun, in the doorway.

"Then Isvaren."

Aud Hallin nodded once. "Thirty Wardens. The lynx." She paused. "And the cats."

"How many came in?"

"Twelve," said Aud Hallin. "Down the Iron Fells with the last of us, on the two-hundred-and-sixty-first. Sabrecats. From the College's pens at Vael, when the lower city went." Her one eye didn't blink. "They're not tame. They're not bonded. They follow the Wardens because the Wardens fed them on the road. They'll fight anything that comes at a Warden."

"That'll do," said Rowan.

---

"The air."

She didn't draw that. You couldn't draw the air on a floor.

"Each over its own. The Clan over the centre and both banks, wherever Arn says. The rocs over the Wyldmarch. The stormkites over Isvaren, and calling the turns down to everyone, as they did at the Thornwood. Nell Tallis's wyverns over Saltreach's end." She looked at Arn. "Nobody owns the sky. You share it."

"I know," said Arn.

"I know you know. I'm saying it so Davin writes it."

Davin wrote it.

---

"And the river."

She put the stick down on the chalk curve. The eddy. The water.

"The Lamp. The Lady, in the water. The lion." She didn't look at the knight. "And the three."

Nobody said anything.

That was the line. Six kingdoms and their beasts across the Vale, and in the middle of it, on the water, four things that weren't any kingdom's.

---

The old man explained the wards.

She'd asked him to. Nobody else understood it. She wasn't sure he did, entirely; he said so.

---

"The knight's oath," he said. From his chair. In his thin voice. "At noon. *Keep the watch.*"

He looked at the knight. The knight didn't look back.

"It was the jailer's charm, once," said Idony. "The College made it. Three hundred years ago. *Keep the watch below*, and *do not ask*, said at the noon bell by every knight in Greyhold, facing the river. It fed the chains. It fed on the chains. A many-voice charm, so that no one voice had to hold it." He moved his shaking hands. "The knight took the chains out of it. *Keep the watch.* Nothing below. Nothing about not asking. A watch. That's all."

"And at noon," said Rowan, "on the day."

"Every voice on the line says it. All at once. Ninety thousand. A ward." He nodded slowly. "It's how the Downs held. Your knights, at the corn exchange. A few hundred voices and a heartstone wagon. This is a great many more voices."

---

"Two," said Idony. "Not one."

"Why two?"

"The river." He looked at the chalk curve. "Running water breaks a spoken charm. Always. It doesn't matter how many voices. A ward can't cross the Wend. So: one on each bank. The Hearthmere half of the line, and the Saltreach half. Two wards. With the river between."

"And the river's where he'll be," said Thale.

"Yes," said the old man. "The river's where the elders will be. The wards hold the line. The elders hold the river."

---

"What feeds it?" said Pike.

She'd been quiet. She'd been writing. She looked up over her spectacles.

"The Downs had a heartstone wagon, Master Vael. A charm needs feeding, on dead ground. And this is the deadest ground in Winshire." She tapped the floor with her shoe. "He drank it for three hundred years. There's nothing under us."

"No," said Idony. "There isn't."

He looked at the barn door. Past Rhun. Toward the eastern meadow, where the Lamp stood in its ring of unicorns and wolves, too bright to look at, and had stood since the camp came up to the eddy. And the river, where the Lady lay.

"Their light," he said. "The Lamp's. The Lady's. What they are now is the Deep, Master Engineer. In the open air. As it was on the far shore. They'll stand with the line, and their light will go into the wards, like oil into a lamp."

"For how long?"

"While the voices don't doubt," said Idony. "And while the light holds."

---

Then the Bridle.

She made Davin read it. The minute. From the summer, from the barn behind the Wend. In his clerk's voice, so that everyone heard it, word for word.

*The Accord's fallback, called by the hands the Bridle, shall fire on the word of the Marshal of the Accord and on no other.*

---

"The Bridle's finished," said Pike. "On its flats, at Ironhythe. It comes down when you call it, Marshal. My surveyors have laid its footings." She nodded toward the barn's western wall. "On the Ferry Flat. You'll have seen."

"I've seen."

"And the resin," said Pike. "Ostrey's. Bought through Blackwater, under the Accord's warrant. In sealed casks. If the Lamp fails, and the Bridle's called, the smoke goes up to open him for it. As the Clan used it once." She folded her hands. "On your word, Marshal."

"On my word," said Rowan. "Both. The Bridle and the smoke. Only on my word. Only if the Lamp fails."

"Agreed," said Pike.

---

"And the batteries," said Rowan. "They hold the line. Whatever happens on the river, they hold their sector. They don't leave the line for the Bridle unless I say."

"Of course, Marshal."

"And the clause. Nothing taken off the field before the end."

"Agreed."

---

She watched Pike agree to everything.

She'd learned to watch Pike. Over the summer, over the autumn, she'd learned to note everything Pike said, and how fast. She noted it now. *Agreed. Agreed. Of course, Marshal.* Every condition. As fast as she said them.

She noted it, and was glad.

That was what she'd wanted. A fallback, on her word. Kessmoor's engines in the centre, holding. Kessmoor agreeing.

She'd have been worried if Pike had argued.

---

The farmer came in late.

He'd flown down from the north that morning; she'd seen the three come in over the ridge behind the Saltreach wall at dawn. He came in at the barn door past Rhun, who stood aside for him, and looked at the map on the floor, and at all of them standing round it.

Davin had put a stool for him.

The first time. At the map's eastern edge, at the Saltreach end, where the farmer's people were. A stool. With a cushion on it, which Davin must have got from Liss.

The farmer looked at the stool.

"I'll stand," he said.

He went to the back. By the ladder to the loft. And stood there, with his scarred hands at his sides, as he always had.

Davin took the cushion away.

---

She went over the line again for him. All of it. He listened from the ladder without saying anything, and at the end, when she said *and the three on the river*, he nodded once.

That was all.

---

The knight found her in the yard after.

At dusk. By the trough. The greyhart drinking. The cold coming down off the downs, and the eddy below the barn turning in the last light, slowly, the right way.

She had something under her arm. Long. In a scabbard. She held it out. One-handed. The right. Rowan saw her look at her own hand while she did it, as if to make sure it was holding.

---

"Haverly's," said the knight.

Rowan didn't take it.

"I know whose it is."

"It's no use to me," said the knight. Rowan had stopped listening for anything in her voice. "I don't fight with it. I fight with him." She didn't look back at the lion, lying along the wall's end below them on the far bank. "I've carried it two years. I've drawn it four times. It's been dark for three of them."

---

"He gave it to you."

"He's dead," said the knight. "He'd want it on a gate. Not in a lion's mane."

Rowan looked at it. The scabbard, plain, worn. The hilt, wrapped in leather gone black with two people's hands.

She took it.

---

She drew it a hand's breadth.

The steel was grey in the dusk. Plain. Down its length, cut into the flat, the strokes. The quickened letters that Haverly's smith had laid into it, that woke on live ground and burned white along the edge.

Dark.

---

"It's dark," said Rowan.

"It's dead ground," said the knight. "It'll be dark on the day."

She looked at Rowan.

"It's still steel," said the knight.

---

Rowan stood in the yard with it after the knight had gone.

Down the bank. To the ferry. Liss taking her across on the new rope, a dark shape on the grey water, turning with the eddy. Then up the far bank to the lion, and the lion's head coming up off the wall to meet her.

Rowan put the edge on her belt. Beside her own.

It was heavier than her own. It pulled her a little to the left.

---

She went out onto the Ferry Flat at midnight.

Alone. She didn't take a lamp. There was a moon, two days off full, and frost, and the whole flat lay white under it.

---

The footings.

Kessmoor's surveyors had come on the two-hundred-and-fifty-fifth. Thirty of them, with wagons and rods and chains and a great deal of white cord. They'd worked for ten days.

Twelve iron beds. Sunk into the turf of the flat in a ring, a quarter-mile across. Each one the size of a barn floor. Black iron, flush with the frozen grass. Where the engines would sit, when they came.

From each bed, a white cord. Pegged out across the turf. In to the middle. Twelve cords. Like the spokes of a wheel laid out on the ground in frost.

And in the middle, where the cords met, a timber cradle. Empty. Waiting for the ring.

---

And at the flat's edge, along the eddy's bank, six iron posts.

Tall. Black. Jibs on them, arms that would swing. For the chains. When the Bridle came, its chains would run out from the engines to the jibs, and the jibs would swing them out over the water.

Over the eddy. Over the old place.

Where he'd lie, if the Lamp brought him down.

---

She walked in along one of the cords.

To the middle. To the empty cradle. And stood in it, in the frost, with the white cords going out from her feet in twelve directions to twelve black iron beds, and the six posts against the moonlit water.

---

She thought of six chains.

She hadn't meant to. She'd meant to look at the footings and go to bed. She stood in the cradle and thought of six chains, black, in curves over the river below Greyhold's hill. From iron eyes in the rock to six posts on the Saltreach bank. A year ago. Her chains. Her cage. White lines inside every link.

*Better that than loose*, she'd said. She'd said it to the knight.

And the trap before that, in the first year, with Thorne's boar in it. She'd held a chain on that thing. Twice.

---

*Chains on a Titan are what started this.*

She'd said that too. In the summer. In the barn. To Pike. *I'll not put another chain on it.*

And she'd voted for the Bridle in the end, on a condition, because she needed the engines more than she needed to be right.

---

*A fallback*, she told herself.

If the elders fail. If the line breaks. On her word and no other. It's written. It's sealed. Davin minuted it, and she'd heard it read again today, word for word, and Pike had said *agreed*.

It was a fallback. It would sit here, on the flat, with its fires banked, and never be fired. The Lamp would go down into the eddy and take the stone out of his back, and the six would go into the light, and the Bridle would sit on its beds and go cold, and in the spring Kessmoor would take it apart again and carry it home.

She believed that.

She stood in the empty cradle in the frost, with Haverly's edge pulling her a little to the left, and believed it.

---

Then she walked back along the cord to the barn, and went to bed, and slept.
