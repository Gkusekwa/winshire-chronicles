---

A stormkite brought it at dawn.

One of the girls. Fifteen, grey felt, a face blue with cold. Her bird came down on the eastern wall by the lion's head at first light with ice on its long wings, and she slid off its back and couldn't stand, and sat on the rampart's top and said it with her teeth going.

"The river's frozen."

Maren looked at the Wend. Grey. Running. Turning in the eddy below the wall the wrong way.

"Not here," said the girl. "Up. North. From the border to below Eskby. Halfway to us. In the night." She put her hands under her arms. "Black ice. You can see through it. A yard thick. I came down over it at dawn. You can see the bottom."

---

Twelve leagues above the hub.

Eskby. A ferry village on the Hearthmere bank, with a stone landing and a chapel. Across from it Otterholt, on the Saltreach side, with its eel-weirs. Above them Trimley, with a mill. A dozen farms between. People who'd stayed behind their own walls through the autumn because their walls were good and the river was between them and everything.

Two thousand, Davin said, from the summer's count.

The river was between them and nothing now.

---

*Up*, she said.

Orrun was already getting up.

---

The first time the Wend froze she'd been at Greyhold, crossing to the lion at dusk in a punt.

She thought of it on his head, going north up the Hearthmere bank at his long stiff stride with the frost going by under her. Two winters ago. Three. She couldn't count it. The running ice jamming at the bend below the island. The white road across. A family walking out onto it in the dark because they couldn't wait. The man going through. Her going in after him with a rope, and the rope parting on the edge, and the black water, and something huge in the water dragging her out by the coat and gone before she could see what it was.

She'd known what it was, after. A long time after.

---

That had taken a month. The cold coming down out of the north. The ice running for ten days. The river locking from the edges in, a little more every night.

This had taken one night.

---

She saw it from the high ground above Eskby at noon on the two-hundred-and-seventy-third.

---

The Wend lay under her. All the way north. As far as she could see. Up the valley to the hills on the Isvaren border, where it came down out of the snows.

Black.

Not white. Not the white of river ice, crusted, rough, with snow on it. Black. Clear. Smooth as a pane of glass. So clear she could see the river-bed through it from the hillside: stones, weed, the dark shapes of drowned trees. Fish. Hanging in it. Not moving.

Frozen in the middle of swimming.

---

And on the banks, rime.

White. Feathered. On every reed and every fence-post and every bare branch for a quarter-league inland from the water. On the roofs of Eskby. On the chapel's bell-cote. Thick as fur. As if the river had breathed out, once, in the night, and the breath had frozen where it fell.

There was smoke from Eskby's chimneys. Not much. Thin.

---

They were in the chapel. Most of them.

She went down to it. Orrun lay on the hillside above the village with his chin on the frozen turf, because he was too big for the street, and she walked down between the houses with her hands in her coat.

The doors were shut. She knocked with her boot.

---

An old man opened it. The reeve, he said. Two hundred people behind him, in a chapel built for sixty, in the dark, round a fire on the flags with the pews broken up to feed it.

"The wells," said the reeve. "Froze solid. In the night. Every one. To the bottom." He looked past her at the river. "The beasts in the byres. Half dead by morning. Standing up. Frozen standing up." He looked back at her. At her face. At the grey on it, and the burn. "You're the lion's knight."

"Yes."

"Is it coming back?" said the reeve. "Whatever did this?"

"Yes," said Maren.

---

She'd meant to bring them south on the bank.

Two thousand people, with their carts, with what they had. On foot. Twelve leagues, down the Hearthmere bank to the hub. Three days, in the frost, with the old ones and the children.

She'd meant that until she saw the column.

---

It came down the ice at dusk.

Out of the north. Down the middle of the black river from the Isvaren border. She saw it from the chapel door: a long dark line on the ice, coming slowly, with low shapes at its head that were too big to be horses.

The reeve came out beside her.

"What's that?" he said.

She knew before Orrun told her. She'd seen them once. On Glasswater's shore.

*Tuskers*, said Orrun. *Kin. Far kin.* And something in his voice she hadn't heard since the Lit Shore. *They know me.*

---

Nineteen of them.

Shaggy. Red-brown. High at the shoulder, sloping to the back, like hills with a wind on them. Tusks that curled back on themselves like old roots. Walking the black ice slowly, in a line, the oldest at the front: a cow, grey round her eyes, her tusks yellowed and one broken short.

And behind them, people.

Four hundred, perhaps. In the long coats of Isvaren. On foot, and on sledges the tuskers were pulling. Walking down the middle of the frozen Wend in the dusk as if it were a road.

---

It was a road, Orrun said. To them.

*The river was a road*, he said. *Once. Before. The old road came down the valley and crossed at the fords. They remember. They walk where it was.*

The oldest cow stopped on the ice below the chapel. She lifted her trunk toward the hillside where Orrun lay. And Maren felt it, through her boots, through the frozen ground, through the stone floor of the chapel step she stood on.

A sound too low to hear. In her feet. In her chest. Like a cart going over a bridge a long way off.

And Orrun answered. She felt that too.

---

A woman got down off the first sledge and walked up the bank.

Grey. Thin. In a Warden's blue coat that was too big for her, over the black of a college magister's robe. Maren had seen her once. At Ironhythe. At the round table under the clock.

Master Beatrix Quell.

---

"Knight," said Quell.

She didn't stop. She walked up past Maren into the chapel door and looked at the two hundred people round the broken pews and turned round again.

"Vael's under ice," said Quell. "To the tops of the towers. The lower city went in the summer. The upper in the autumn, when it came back down the lake." She looked at the river. "We came out on the ice. Four hundred. All that would come. The herd agreed to bring us." She nodded at the old cow below. "Isvaren thinks it hires them. It doesn't. It asks."

"It followed you," said Maren. "The Coil."

"Yes," said Quell. "It's behind us. A day. Perhaps less. It's going south." She looked at Maren. "To you, I think. To the middle."

---

They loaded the sledges that night.

All night. By the light of Eskby's last fires. Everything that would go. Blankets. Flour. Children. The old people. The reeve's chapel plate. And across the river at Otterholt, and up at Trimley, the same. Torches going back and forth on the black ice in the dark, and people sliding, and falling, and getting up.

The tuskers waited on the ice in a line.

---

In the morning the Isvaren drovers put the traces on.

Rope. Plaited. Round the tuskers' great chests, under the forelegs, back to the sledges. Three sledges to a tusker. Four to the oldest cow, who wouldn't take fewer.

They didn't need goads. The drovers didn't carry any. They walked beside the tuskers' heads and spoke to them, low, and the tuskers rumbled back, and Maren felt it through the ice.

And they started south.

---

Down the middle of the frozen Wend. Nineteen tuskers in a line, sixty sledges behind them, two thousand people walking beside the sledges on the black ice. Slowly. At a tusker's pace, which is a man's.

Orrun walked on the bank. He was too heavy for the ice. A yard thick, and it would have gone under him like a skin of frost on a puddle.

She walked on the ice. At the back. Behind the last sledge.

She couldn't feel it under her boots. She watched it.

---

The Coil came at noon on the two-hundred-and-seventy-fourth.

---

She felt it before she saw it. They all did. The cold.

Not the cold of the morning, which had been bad. A cold that came down the river from the north like a wind, though there wasn't any wind. A cold that made the air hurt to breathe. That made the rime on the reeds along the bank grow while she watched, feathering out, thickening, racing inland across the fields in a white line.

A bird fell out of the sky.

A crow. Just ahead of her. Onto the ice. It hit and didn't move, and when she came to it it was frozen through, its wings half open, its eye white.

---

Then she saw the light.

Under the ice. To the north. Behind them. A pale light, moving down the river under the yard of black ice. Coming. Long. A quarter-league long. Coiling as it came, as a snake coils, and uncoiling, and coiling.

Where it passed, the black ice went white. Thick. Cracked white, like old marble.

---

*It's coming under*, said Orrun. *Under the ice. They're on the ice.*

She looked at the column. A league ahead of her. The tuskers. The sledges. Two thousand people on a pane of black glass, and the thing coming down underneath it.

*Stop it*, she said. *Make it come up.*

---

He went down onto the ice.

Off the bank. Onto the black river, behind the column, between the column and the light. And the ice went under him. She'd known it would. A yard of it, breaking under a hill like a sheet of sugar, black slabs going up on end and sliding.

He didn't go through.

---

The river-bed came up.

She felt it. Through him. Through her hand on the frozen bank where she'd knelt. The stone under the Wend, the old bed, coming up under his feet where he wanted it. Bond gift. Not a word. The stone was his. He stood on the river-bed as it rose under him, waist-deep in black water and broken ice, a grey hill in the middle of the Wend.

And stamped.

---

Again. And again. His great forefeet coming down on the ice either side of him and breaking it. A quarter-league of it. Across the river, from bank to bank. Black slabs going up and over and down. Open water, grey, churning, full of ice.

A broken stretch of river across the Coil's road.

It would have to come up.

---

It came up.

---

Out of the open water. In front of him. A head.

Long. Narrow. Pale. Like a pike's, if a pike were the size of a barn. Eyes like ice over deep water. And behind the head, coiling up out of the river, a body like a river itself, pale, scaled, frost on every scale, going back under the white ice for a quarter-league.

The Pale Coil.

---

Orrun took its head in his jaws.

She felt him do it. Felt his teeth close on the pale scales behind its jaw. Felt the cold come into his mouth. And he put it down. Its head. Down into the open water. Onto the river-bed that he'd raised. And held it there.

The Coil thrashed.

The whole river moved. The quarter-league of it under the white ice heaving up, coiling, the ice cracking for a league north. Black slabs. White slabs. Water going up in sheets and freezing in the air and coming down as hail.

He held it.

---

The cold came up the bond.

Into her. Through her hand on the bank. She felt it come. Not in her skin; she had no skin to feel with, any more. In her middle. Under her ribs, on the grey side, where the stone from the Thornwood lay.

Cold going into cold.

*Maren*, said Orrun. Through his teeth. Through the scales.

*I know.*

*It's my kin too*, he said. *All of them are. Every one.*

*I know.*

*It's so cold. It's always been this cold.*

---

The last sledge went through behind the column.

She saw it go. A league ahead. Where the Coil's thrashing had run up the river under the ice and cracked it under the tuskers' line. The last sledge, behind the oldest cow, sliding sideways, and the ice opening under it in a black seam, and the sledge going in.

Trimley's miller's. She'd seen them load it. The miller and his wife walking beside. And on the sledge, wrapped in a blanket, their girl.

---

She ran.

On the ice. She didn't know she could still run. She ran a league on the black ice, falling, getting up, falling, and she didn't feel any of the falls, and she came to the black seam in the ice with the miller lying flat on the edge reaching into the water and screaming.

The sledge was gone. The girl was in the water. A blanket. A face. Going under the ice's edge.

---

*Flat on the ice*, the river watch taught. *Spread your weight. Rope round you. Reach.*

She went flat on the ice. The miller's wife threw her a rope and she got it round her middle one-handed and couldn't tie it and the wife tied it. She went along the ice on her belly to the edge. She reached in.

With her right hand. The good one.

She couldn't feel it.

She couldn't feel the water. She couldn't feel if she'd closed her hand. She couldn't feel the blanket, or the girl, or anything. She looked. Her hand was in the black water to the wrist and she couldn't see what it was holding.

---

She went in.

---

The water closed over her.

Black. Full of ice. She knew it was cold. She knew it as you know a fact out of a book. She didn't feel it. She'd gone into the Wend in the first winter and the cold had been a fist that stopped her heart, and now there was nothing. Nothing at all.

That was what would kill her. She knew that too. She wouldn't feel herself die of it.

She looked for the girl.

---

A blanket. A pale face. Below her, under the ice's edge, going down. She went down after it. She got the blanket in her right hand and looked at her hand to see if it was holding and it was. She had her. She pulled.

The rope went taut. Then slack.

Then the ice above her was black and she couldn't find the hole.

---

And something came up under her.

---

Huge. Pale. Under her and the girl, coming up out of the deep water of the Wend's bed like the bed itself rising. Smooth. Cold. Not the Coil's cold. A different cold. Water's. Clean.

It lifted them.

Up. Through the black water. Through the hole in the ice. Out. Into the air. Onto the ice at the seam's edge, where the miller and his wife were lying flat, screaming.

---

She saw it.

This time she saw it. She lay on the ice on her back with the girl on her chest and the water running out of her and saw it.

A head. Long. Like a heron's. On a neck like a river. Pale as the moon on frost. Going back down into the black water through the hole in the ice. Slowly. With eyes the colour of deep water, that looked at her once.

The Lady.

---

The first time, she hadn't seen. Something huge in the water, dragging her out by the coat, and gone.

She'd known, after, that it was Orrun.

This was not Orrun.

---

The miller's girl was breathing.

Her name was Hessa. Nine. The wife told her, over and over, holding her. Hessa. Hessa. Hessa. She was screaming, which meant she was alive.

Maren lay on the black ice and couldn't feel the girl's weight on her chest.

She looked at her to be sure she was there.

---

Quell found her.

She didn't know how long after. Quell's face over her, grey, thin. Quell's hands. A coat going round her. The Warden's coat, too big, blue, heavy, over the wet.

"You're shaking," said Quell.

"Am I?"

Quell looked at her.

"Yes," said Quell. "Very badly." She pulled the coat tighter. "Can you not feel it?"

"No."

---

The Lady came up the river in the night.

---

She'd been at the hub. In the eddy. Lying on the water by the ferry rope since the camp came up, as she lay. And sometime in the night of the two-hundred-and-seventy-fourth she'd come up the river. Twelve leagues. The last of them under the ice.

Maren saw where she'd been at dawn.

A line. Down the middle of the black ice from the south. The ice gone grey over it. Soft. Rotten. Breaking up. And behind it, water. Grey water, running. The Wend, running again, in a long open channel down the middle of the frozen river, where the Lady had come up under the ice.

---

The Coil was still in Orrun's jaws.

He'd held it all night. In the open water he'd made, on the river-bed he'd raised. Its head down. Its body under the white ice behind it, coiling and coiling, slower now.

And the Lady came up beside him.

---

They were kin.

She saw it, as she'd seen the Lamp and the Horned Sun. The same shape. Long. Serpent. Pale. One cold and one not. One water and one ice.

The Lady wound round it.

---

She went round it in the open water. Round its neck, behind Orrun's jaws. Round its body, under the white ice. Coil on coil. Pale on pale. And the river boiled.

Not with heat. With cold and not-cold. Steam and frost going up together off the water in a white wall. The ice cracking. For a league. Two. North and south. The whole frozen Wend breaking up at once, slabs going up and turning over and going down, and grey water coming up between them, running.

---

*Let go*, said the Lady.

Maren didn't hear it. Orrun did. She heard it through Orrun. A shape. Light, in the water.

He let go.

---

The Coil went back north.

Under the ice that was left. Fast. Its pale light going away up the river, a quarter-league long, coiling, uncoiling, toward the Isvaren border. The Lady after it. A league. Two. Her long pale shape in the grey water behind it, driving it, as a dog drives a sheep that doesn't want to go.

Then she stopped.

And turned. And came back down the river, slowly, in the open water, and the ice behind her went to water as she passed.

---

The first time. Maren knew it was the first time. The old man had said so, at the barn. *They've never fought. Not since they stopped. They haven't had to.*

The Lady had fought.

---

Orrun came up out of the river onto the bank.

Slowly. Frost on his jaw. Frost white on his forelegs to the shoulder, thick as fur, the rime of the Coil's cold on his stone. He lay down on the bank above the river and put his white chin on his white paws.

*It's gone back*, he said.

*For now.*

*For now*, he said. *It'll come again. When he calls it.*

---

She'd seen the fire in the night.

She remembered it now. Lying on the ice in Quell's coat, after the girl, before the Lady. Looking away from the river. East. Over the hills of the Saltreach bank, under the Scarfell.

A glow. Red. Low on the sky, far off, beyond the hills. Like a town burning, if there'd been a town there to burn.

She'd known what it was.

---

Lisska had told Orrun, two days before, on the wall. The farmer was on the north road. On the Drover's Way, at the fords. Teo's ford on Hollow Beck, and the crossings below it. Burning the ice off them, so the road north would stay open in the winter.

For the other plan. The one nobody said.

She'd looked at the red glow on the eastern sky for a long time, lying on the ice. It was ten leagues off. Less.

Too far to help. Too far to see her.

She hadn't wanted him to.

---

They walked south on the two-hundred-and-seventy-fifth.

On the bank. The ice was going; nobody would walk it now. Two thousand from the upper Wend, and four hundred from Vael, and sixty sledges on the frozen fields, and nineteen tuskers pulling them, and the oldest cow at the front with her broken tusk.

Hessa on her mother's back, asleep.

Maren on the lion's head. In Quell's coat. Shaking, Quell said. She couldn't tell.

---

Quell walked beside the lion's foreleg.

"The Coil will come again," said Quell.

"Yes."

"When?"

Maren looked south. Down the Wend. Toward the hub, twelve leagues off, where the river turned in its eddy and the Lady was going back to lie by the ferry rope.

"When it's called," she said.
