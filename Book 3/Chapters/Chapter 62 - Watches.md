---

The word came on the two-hundred-and-ninety-seventh.

By semaphore. Down the Iron Road, arm to arm across the hills, from the Accord's tower at the Vale to the last tower at Ironhythe, and read off there by a clerk who wrote it on a slip and ran with it to Pike's office.

Wardle read it out at the evening's six, before the work-sheet. In his pleasant flat voice.

*Bring the fallback.*

*I. Rowan. Marshal.*

---

They left on the three-hundredth.

At dawn. Out of the Coldrun sidings. In the cold, with the frost on the cinders and the canal smoking.

---

He'd never seen the road-engines before.

Kessmoor kept them in a long shed at the sidings' end and didn't take them out for nothing. They came out that morning one after another, hissing. Iron. Each as big as a cart-shed, on iron wheels taller than a man, with a chimney at the front and a firebox under it and a man on a little iron platform at the back with his hands on two brass levers.

They didn't need rails. They went along the road on their own wheels, crushing it.

Twenty-seven of them. One for each string.

---

Four flats to a string. Coupled with chain. A road-engine at the front, hauling.

The twelve engines of the Bridle, one to a flat. The ninety-six lengths. The ring, on its double flat. A hundred and eight flats. Twenty-seven strings.

And on every flat, a green lamp on a pole.

---

Odo was on the towpath.

In his chair. Tam's chair, that Tam had built him at Millbrook out of a cart's wheels and an oak bench. He'd wheeled himself down from the smiths' barracks before dawn to see them go. He sat on the towpath by the first string with a blanket over his broken legs and watched the road-engines come out of their shed.

Tobin went over to him.

---

"You're not coming," said Tobin.

"I'm too big for a flat," said Odo. He didn't laugh. He'd always laughed. Like a bellows. He hadn't laughed since the news-wagons.

He looked at the seventh string.

"Seventh engine," said Odo. "The left sledge-runner. Under the flat. It's cracked. Hairline. Near the back bolt. I saw it when they lifted it." He looked at Tobin. "Watch it. On the bad roads."

"I'll watch it."

---

"Tell Tam," said Odo, "the canals are very fine."

He held out his big burned hand. Tobin took it.

"Tell him I'm minding the barracks," said Odo. "Tell him I'm well. Tell him not to make anything he can't see the use of." He let go. "He'll laugh."

Tobin didn't think Tam would laugh.

He said he'd tell him.

---

The first road-engine hissed and took the strain on its chain.

And the line began to move.

---

Down the Iron Road. Along the canal, east, out of Ironhythe, past the clock on its hill. The Hall of Measures behind its glass with its pendulum going. *Tock.*

Three thousand of his hands walking beside the strings. With crowbars. With ropes. With poles. For the bad places.

Ines beside him. Callis on the seventh flat, by its engine's firebox, with his shovel and his crate of grey stone, feeding it.

He didn't look back at Odo.

---

In Kessmoor the road was good.

Canal-side, paved, graded, with iron bridges. The road-engines went along it at a walking pace and the strings followed, and the green lamps swung on their poles, and at night they stopped in a line along the canal and the hands slept on the flats beside the warm engines.

At the border the road stopped being Kessmoor's.

---

Hearthmere's road was broken.

The Iron Road, they called it, all the way to Hallowmere. In Hearthmere it was an ordinary road. Gravel. Ruts. Cracked by the winter, and by the drain, and by a year of nobody mending it. The bridges were Hearthmere bridges, stone, built for carts and greyharts, and some of them had cracked when the ground died under them.

A road-engine and four flats with a heartstone engine on each weighed more than any bridge in Hearthmere had ever been asked to hold.

---

They shored them.

Every one. His hands went ahead of the line with timber off the flats and built frames under the bridges, in the rivers, in the cold, up to their waists in water with ice at the edges. Then the strings went over one at a time, slowly, the road-engine hissing, the timber groaning, the hands standing on the banks holding their breath.

Ten days.

---

At night on the downs, it was beautiful.

He couldn't help it. He knew what it was. He stood on a rise one night on the empty Candle Downs, burned black and white with frost, and looked back along the line.

A league of green lamps. In the dark. Swinging. One on every flat, a hundred and eight, going away down the road behind him in a long curve over the downs like a string of green beads somebody had laid on the land.

And the engines' fireboxes, red, low, under the green.

---

The seventh runner held.

He watched it. Every bad place. Every bridge. He went under the seventh flat with a lamp at every stop and looked at the crack Odo had seen, and it was there, hairline, near the back bolt, and it didn't grow.

Callis watched him do it. From the flat. With his shovel.

Neither of them said anything.

---

They came down onto the Vale on the three-hundred-and-tenth.

---

At night. Over the western downs. The Accord's camp below them in the dark, two lines of fire across the Vale on the knight's walls and a hundred thousand fires behind them. And the eddy in the middle, black.

The line of green lamps came down off the downs onto the Vale in a long curve, and the camp came out to look.

He saw them. Along the walls. On the banks. Thousands. Standing in the frost watching the green lamps come down, and the red fireboxes, and the long black shapes on the flats.

Nobody cheered. Nobody said anything that he heard.

They watched.

---

They set it in four days.

On the Ferry Flat. The water-meadow on the Hearthmere bank at the eddy's edge, where the surveyors had laid the beds in the autumn. Twelve black iron beds in a ring. White cords. A timber cradle.

The rail-cranes had come down on their own flats. They put them on short rails laid on the turf and the hands pushed them, and they lifted the engines off the flats one at a time and swung them over and set them on their beds. Then the chain, length by length, shackled, along the cords, in to the middle. Then the ring, into its cradle, with the stage on its top.

Then the six jibs on the eddy's edge took the chain-ends.

Wardle went round it all with his hands behind his back, whistling, and nodded.

---

Kessmoor's batteries were either side.

He'd heard of them. He hadn't seen them till now. On both banks. Round the hub. Bolt-throwers on wheeled frames, a hundred of them, with racks of black bolts tipped with grey heartstone. The chilled-chain engines, the ones that had turned the Furnace at Gearmouth. And the magazine: a long low iron shed on the Flat's western edge, with two guards at its door, day and night. One in Kessmoor grey. One in Hearthmere's.

The resin was in there, Wardle said. A hundred casks. With the Marshal's seal on every bung.

"On her word," said Wardle. "Only. As it says."

---

The Bridle sat in the centre of the line.

On the Ferry Flat, at the eddy's edge, with the batteries on either side of it and across the river, and the knight's walls going away west and east from it across the Vale.

Its engines banked. Warm.

He'd built it. He'd carried every link. He stood on the Flat at dusk on the three-hundred-and-fourteenth and looked at it, set, and didn't know what he felt.

---

Liss came on the three-hundred-and-eleventh.

With soup.

---

On her ferry. Across the eddy from the ferry house. A cauldron on the scow's deck, steaming, as big as a barrel. She'd made it in the ferry house kitchen with two of the camp's cooks and it had taken all afternoon.

She took it to the Saltreach-bank battery first. Then back across to the Ferry Flat. And ladled it herself. Into the crews' tins, one after another, with an iron ladle, two hundred tins.

Kessmoor's crews didn't know what to do.

---

"What's the price?" said a battery-master. A young Kessmoor man. With his tin out.

Liss looked at him.

"I'm not Kessmoor," said Liss.

She ladled.

---

She came every evening after that.

Every one. Across the eddy at dusk with her cauldron. Saltreach bank, then the Flat. The crews started looking for her across the water at the fourth hour after noon. They called her *the soup*. *Is the soup coming. Here's the soup.*

She gave Tobin a tin too. And Ines. And Callis, on the seventh engine's bed, who took it in both his crooked hands because he couldn't hold it in one.

She looked at Callis a long time.

She'd known him. At Greyhold. She'd ferried him across a hundred times.

She didn't say anything. She ladled.

---

The night before was the three-hundred-and-sixteenth.

---

He didn't know it was the night before. Nobody told the hands. But everybody knew. The old man's glasses at the ferry house window had been climbing for three days, people said, and the lion hadn't moved off the wall since noon, and the three on the ridge were lying with their heads up, all of them, looking west.

And the battery-masters met at Wardle's brazier at the eleventh hour.

---

Eight of them. From both banks. Wardle's brazier was on the Flat by the ring's cradle, and they came to it one by one across the frozen turf in their grey coats and stood round it with their hands out to the coals.

Tobin was on the coal-run. Hauling coal from the heap to the engines' boxes. He went past the brazier twice. The second time he stopped. Because they were doing something.

---

Watches.

Each of them had one out. Brass. Open. The same kind the crew-masters at Ironhythe carried, and looked at, and never let anyone else hold.

Wardle had his out too. He held it in his palm, face up, in the brazier's light.

"Ironhythe," said Wardle. Pleasantly. "By the Hall's clock. Before we left. Not touched since." He held it out. "Set yours."

They set them. One by one. Each man looking at Wardle's watch and turning the little crown on his own, and holding it to his ear, and nodding. Eight watches. All the same.

---

Wardle saw him watching.

He didn't mind. He never minded. He smiled, round his whistle, and held up his watch.

"Hall of Measures, Tobin," he said. "The truest clock in Winshire. If it says it's the hour, it's the hour."

---

"What's the hour for?" said Tobin.

Wardle put his watch away. In his coat. And as he did, Tobin saw something else in the coat. In the inside pocket. A corner. Folded paper, thick. Black wax.

Wardle saw him see it.

He took it out. He didn't mind. He held it up in the brazier's light so that Tobin could read the writing on it, in a clerk's hand, under the Assembly's black seal.

---

*To be opened on the hour the relic is in reach.*

---

"Fallback," said Wardle.

He put it back in his coat.

"If the Marshal calls it," he said. "If the old ones fail. We've all got one." He nodded round the brazier. The other seven nodded. Each touched his coat. "Orders. For the hour. So we're all doing the same thing at the same time, if it comes. Engines are like that. They like to be told the same thing at once."

He smiled.

"Feed your boxes, Tobin," said Wardle. And went back to whistling.

---

He fed his boxes.

---

He went to the seventh after midnight.

He didn't know why. It was on the coal-run, the seventh's bed was on his way. He went to it with his basket and climbed up beside the firebox, where Callis sat with his shovel and his crate.

Callis was awake. He was always awake. He sat with his back to the engine's warm side and his crooked arms across his knees and looked at the ring in its cradle across the Flat, with the green lamps round it and the stage black on its top.

---

"Tobin," said Callis.

Tobin stopped.

He'd never said his name before. Not once. Not at Ironhythe, not on the road, not here. *You wrote to someone.* That was all he'd ever said.

"Tobin," said Callis again. Very low.

---

Tobin waited.

Callis looked at him. His grey face. His eyes, which had been a knight's eyes once, at Greyhold, on the gate. He opened his mouth.

"When it's time," said Callis.

And stopped.

---

He looked away. Across the Flat. At the ring. At the stage on its top.

Tobin waited.

Callis looked at the stage a long time. Then he looked down at his hands. At the right forearm, knit crooked halfway. At the left wrist, thick and stiff. The hands the knight had broken.

"Nothing," said Callis. "Feed your box."

---

He fed the box.

He climbed down. He went back across the Flat with his empty basket. He didn't look back.

He thought: *when it's time.* And: *nothing.* And he didn't know which one Callis had meant.

---

At the second hour he saw the man on the stage.

---

He was on the coal-run again. At the heap, at the Flat's western edge, by the magazine with its two guards. Filling his basket. And he looked up, for no reason, across the Flat toward the ring.

Someone was crossing between the engines.

---

Tall. In a hood. Without a lamp. Walking between the engines in the dark where the green lamps didn't reach, not hurrying, as a man walks who knows where he's going and has every right to go there.

To the ring.

Up the step. Onto the stage.

---

He stood at the iron stand. At the front of the stage. Looking down. Into the ring.

Then he reached down.

Over the rail. Into the ring's middle. To its inner face, where Tobin had seen the old man with the graver kneeling behind a canvas screen at Ironhythe, cutting a line of little hooked strokes round the inside, from hollow to hollow.

He put his hand on it. Flat. On the script.

And stood there.

---

A long time.

Tobin stood at the coal-heap with his basket and watched. The two guards at the magazine door behind him were talking to each other about their feet. They didn't look.

The green lamps round the ring swung a little in the wind.

And the hand on the ring's inner face caught their light. Once. A glint. Not like a hand. Like metal.

---

He couldn't see the face. The hood. The dark. The man stood with his back half to the coal-heap, at the stage's front, with his hand on the script in the ring, and his head bent.

As a man bends his head in a chapel.

---

Then he took his hand away.

Came down off the stage. Walked off across the Flat between the engines, toward the batteries on the Flat's eastern side. In the dark. Not hurrying.

Gone.

---

Tobin didn't call out.

He stood with the basket on his hip and didn't call out. He didn't know why. He'd called out nothing at Ironhythe either. At the order on the nail. At the stage going up. *Fallback*, Wardle had said. And he'd let it go.

He'd written to the Marshal once. About Callis. She'd written back: *Pike says he was hired for bread. Watch him.*

He'd watched him.

He didn't know who to write to about a man in a hood with his hand on a ring.

He filled the basket. He went back to the boxes.

---

Before dawn Ines came and stood beside him at the eddy's edge.

---

He'd stopped. He didn't know for how long. The basket was empty at his feet. The frost white on the Flat, and on the engines, and on the chain along the cords. The batteries silent. The green lamps paling.

And across the eddy, on the eastern wall, at its river end, the lion.

---

Lying along the top of the rampart. His head on his paws. His mane over the edge. Grey, with the frost white on him, all of him, like a hill in the morning.

Very still.

And against his foreleg, small, a woman. The frost on her too. Not moving.

---

"Is it asleep?" said Ines.

Tobin looked at the lion. At its pale eyes. They were open. He could see them in the grey light, a long way off. Open, and looking west.

"I don't think so," said Tobin.
