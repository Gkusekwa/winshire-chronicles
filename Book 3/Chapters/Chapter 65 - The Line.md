---

She rode the wall all day.

From the river end to the bend. And back. On the white-blazed greyhart, along the wall's top, which was a road's breadth and had a road's surface now from ninety-eight thousand feet walking it. Davin behind her on a pony with his case. Four runners on foot, young, fast, who'd been corn-exchange porters at Hallowmere.

Haverly's edge on her left hip. Pulling her to the left. Dark.

---

She'd thought she'd command from the barn.

Every general in every song commanded from a hill. She'd thought she'd sit in Liss's hay-barn with the chalk map on the floor and the runners coming in, and point.

She didn't. She couldn't. You couldn't see a line from a barn.

So she rode it.

---

The Last Titan walked along the ward all morning.

She watched it from the wall's top. Three hundred paces off, on the other side of the shimmer. A hill, walking. Its great grey head against the ward's face, low, and the ward going white where it touched, in a patch the size of a field. Moving north. Toward the river. A pace at a time.

It wasn't hurrying. That was the worst of it. It walked along the ward as a man walks along a fence looking for the gate.

---

Where the white went, the people under it said the words.

She saw it first at the third hour. The white patch coming along the ward toward the stretch of line by the bend's eastern end, where the militia stood behind the wall: farm-men and porters and a forge's apprentices, with pikes. And as the white came over them, they said it. Not all at once, as at noon. Ragged. A few. Then more.

*Keep the watch.*

And the white held. Above them. The ward didn't bend.

---

She understood it then.

The old man had said it in the barn. *While the voices don't doubt.* She'd thought it meant at noon. It meant all day. Where the white came, the ward needed voices under it.

So she rode ahead of the white.

Along the wall's top, ahead of the Last Titan's head, to the next stretch of the line, and stood in her stirrups, and said it. *Keep the watch.* And the people there said it back. And the white came over them, and they said it again, and it held.

All morning. Ahead of the white. Saying three words over and over until her voice was a crow's.

---

The olifants stood.

At the Sarovel sector. Halfway along. Where the wall ran past the camp's western fires. Sixty of them, in a double row, behind the wall's top, side by side. Grey. Enormous. Their trunks curled. Their eyes shut.

The Last Titan came along the ward to them at noon.

---

She was there. She'd ridden ahead.

The white came over them. A spear's length over their heads, the ward going white, and the great grey head behind it, and the weight of it pressing.

The olifants didn't move.

She watched them not move. Sixty grey walls with their eyes shut, standing in the light. Their ears flat. Their legs set. As they'd knelt in the road at the muster, and in the Vintner's Gate, and wouldn't be moved; now they stood, and wouldn't be.

Behind them Mattias's unchosen on the white steeds. The steeds shying. Rearing. Screaming. The unchosen holding them, with their faces white.

And the people of Sarovel under the ward, behind their olifants, saying the words in their own accent.

---

The ward went white over the Sarovel sector and bent.

A breath. One. Down. Toward the olifants' backs. As a sheet sags under rain. And in that breath, she saw the weight come through it. The Last Titan's weight. Leaning.

Four of the olifants went down.

---

At the front. In the first row. Their legs going. Folding. Not kneeling; going. The weight of a hill coming through the ward onto their backs for one breath, and four of them going down under it onto their knees and onto their sides, and not getting up.

The others didn't move.

The unchosen said it louder. *Keep the watch.* Sarovel's accent, high, ragged, all of them. Mattias's voice among them, she could hear it, a young man's, cracking.

The ward lifted.

The white went on. Past them. North. Toward the river.

---

She rode on ahead of it.

She didn't look back at the four grey shapes lying behind the wall. She'd look later. She looked later.

---

The bend broke at the fourth hour.

Not the wall. The wall held; she'd heard from the runners about the stone lions in its face, and hadn't believed it, and had ridden back along the top at the third hour and looked down over the edge, and seen them. Hundreds. Grey. Crouched. Holding.

Behind the wall it broke.

---

The ground. Behind the bend, where the line turned back to the river. Where the Furnace had been pushing under the downs all morning. A gully opening, all at once, a hundred paces long, across the militia's lines. Steam coming up out of it, white, scalding. The ground falling in.

The militia falling back from it. Running. A gap in the line, a hundred paces wide.

---

She sent the greyharts.

She didn't have to say much. They were waiting for it. That was what they were for. Three hundred knights at the line's western end, on and off their beasts, with their hounds. The only thing on the Hearthmere bank fast enough to go where the line broke.

She stood in her stirrups on the wall's top and pointed.

And they went.

---

Along the bank. On the wall's top and at its foot. Three hundred greyharts at a gallop, grey, long-legged, white-blazed, the knights on them low, the hounds running beside. Along the line where it bent back toward the river. To the gap.

Wynn first. On her grey. With Ash and Sorrow running at her knee, the two great hounds, their tongues out.

---

They filled it.

She watched them do it. The greyharts going into the gap at the gallop and stopping, all together, in a line across it, and the knights coming off their backs with their swords and their spears, and the hounds standing in front of them, low, growling at the steam. A line of knights across a hundred paces of broken ground.

And the militia coming back. Seeing the knights. Coming back to stand behind them.

*Keep the watch*, they said. Ragged. Then not ragged.

The ward over them held.

---

At noon she'd seen the beast-flood.

Across the river. Through the shimmer of the Saltreach ward, from the wall's top at the river end. She'd seen it come and couldn't do anything about it but watch.

---

Out of the north. Down the Saltreach bank. Driven.

The Coil's cold had come down the river, and ahead of the cold, out of the dead Wyldmarch and the upper country, everything that lived there and could still run. Elk. Deer. Wild cattle, black and shaggy. Boar. Wolves gone wrong, grey, thin, white-eyed. Thousands. Running south down the Saltreach bank in one brown mass, as water runs down a hill, at the Wyldmarch's sector by the river.

Mad. White-eyed. Not seeing the line. Not seeing anything.

Coming at the clans.

---

The stonehorns turned into it.

She saw it happen. Ten thousand of them. Grazing behind the wall, as they'd grazed all morning, their plated heads in the frozen grass. Then up. All of them. The herd-men on the leaders, with their goads and their calls. Turning. North. Into the flood.

In a wedge.

Plated heads down. The great grey-brown backs together. A wedge of stonehorns, ten thousand, going into the brown mass of running beasts at a walk, then a trot. Not a gallop. Stonehorns didn't gallop. They didn't need to.

---

The flood broke on them.

As a river breaks on a bridge's pier. She saw it split. Round the wedge's point. The running beasts going left and right of it, east into the scrub, west into the river, away from the clans' line. Elk going down under the stonehorns' feet. Wolves going over their backs. Boar and wild cattle spilling east.

The wedge went on north. Slowly. Splitting it.

The flood went round.

---

Two hundred of the clans died. Davin said, at dusk. Where the flood had reached the line before the wedge had turned. Two hundred.

The rest didn't.

---

The Furnace came up at the fourth hour after noon.

---

She was at the river end. Coming back along the wall from the bend. She felt it before she saw it. The wall's top under her greyhart's feet, shaking. Not the Last Titan's walking. A deeper shaking. Under. Coming.

The greyhart stopped. Its ears flat.

And on the Ferry Flat below her, by the magazine, the ground split.

---

The western edge of the Flat. By the long iron shed where the resin was, with its two guards. The turf heaving up in a long swell. Splitting. Steam coming out of the split, white, then red. Rock. Glass. Molten glass, running out of the split onto the frozen turf like porridge out of a pot, and the turf going up in smoke round it.

And a head.

---

Plated. Like an ironback's. Like the great armoured burrowers she'd seen in Kessmoor's bestiaries as a girl. But the size of a barn. Black plates, overlapping, and between the plates, at every seam, red. Glowing. Molten. Its eyes small and white.

And round its neck, hanging, a chain.

Black. Kessmoor iron. Broken. The cage-chain from the Scald, that had held it for a hundred and seventeen days, still hanging off it a year later, its links glowing.

The Furnace.

Coming up under the batteries.

---

The chilled chains held it.

Kessmoor had known. That was the thing she'd think of after. They'd known it would come under them; it had come under them at Gearmouth. They'd built for it.

The chilled-chain engines were on the Flat's western side, by the magazine. Four of them. Squat iron machines with drums of chain, and the chain run out from the drums through iron tanks of water from the eddy, kept cold, and the chain coming out of the tanks steaming in the frost.

They threw it.

---

She saw the chains go. Out of the engines. Through the air. Heavy black lengths, steaming, thrown by some engine-arm she didn't understand, over the Furnace's head as it came up out of the split.

Round its neck. Round its forelegs. Pulling tight.

Where the chilled iron touched the glowing seams, the steam went up white with a scream like a kettle.

It held.

---

The Furnace dragged.

Toward the Bridle. She saw it. Not at the batteries. Past them. Toward the ring of engines on their beds in the middle of the Flat. As if it knew what they were. As if it wanted them. Dragging the chilled chains, dragging the engines that held them, a pace, two paces, across the frozen turf, the drums screaming.

And Kessmoor's crews fought for the Bridle.

---

She'd never seen them fight. They didn't, mostly. They worked their engines. Now they fought. Two hundred of them in grey, off the bolt-throwers, running across the Flat between the Furnace and the Bridle's engines with crowbars and shovels and fire-hooks, in front of a molten thing the size of a barn, as men run in front of a fire to save their own house.

The bolt-throwers fired. Point-blank. Black bolts tipped with grey heartstone going into the Furnace's plates and sticking there, glowing.

Eleven of the crews went under it.

The rest didn't stop.

---

She rode down off the wall onto the Flat.

She didn't know why. There was nothing she could do. She had a dark sword and a greyhart. She rode down anyway, to the Bridle's edge, and sat there, and the greyhart stood under her shaking, and she watched the Furnace drag the chains toward the engines.

And looked at the Bridle.

Banked. Its fires low. Its chains on the jibs. Its crews in front of it with crowbars.

Unfired.

---

And the Lady came.

---

Out of the north. Down the river. From the channel where she'd been winding with the Coil all day in the steam. Fast. Her long pale shape in the grey water, coming down past the Lamp at the eddy's head, into the eddy, across it, to the Flat's edge.

And up.

Out of the water. Onto the Flat. A neck like a river, rising out of the eddy, pale as the moon on frost, and a head like a heron's, and her eyes the colour of deep water.

She went round the Furnace.

---

Round its neck, where the chilled chains were. Round its plated body as it came up out of the split. Round and round. Cold. Water's cold. Clean. Pale coils over black plates and red seams.

And pulled.

Into the river.

---

The Furnace screamed.

Not like a beast. Like metal. Like the sound in Tam's forge when he put a red bar into the trough, but a thousand times, a barn-sized bar, a hill of it. The Lady pulling it off the Flat, out of its split, across the turf, its glass running behind it, into the eddy's edge.

Into the water.

---

Steam.

A column of it. Straight up. From the eddy's edge. White, then grey. A league high. Higher. Over the Flat, over the batteries, over the wall, going up into the cold sky until she couldn't see the top of it. And the noise. The scream of it cooling. The river boiling round it, grey, for a quarter-league.

Like iron at the forge.

Quenched.

---

When the steam thinned, it lay in the eddy's edge.

Black. All of it. The red gone out of the seams. The plates cracked across, every one, as quenched iron cracks when it's quenched too fast. The chain from the Scald still round its neck, black, cold. Its small white eyes gone grey.

Dead.

The Lady went back into the eddy and lay on the water by the rope as if she'd never left it.

---

The line cheered.

She heard it start on the Flat. Kessmoor's crews. Then the batteries across the river. Then the wall. Then all of it. Both banks. Ninety-eight thousand. Under the wards. The first one. The first destroyer of the battle. Killed.

She sat on her greyhart at the Bridle's edge and didn't cheer.

---

She was looking west.

Over the Hearthmere ward. Through the shimmer. At the Last Titan, on the other side of it, three hundred paces off, with its head against the white.

It had lifted its head.

---

Off the ward. Up. As the Furnace died. She'd seen it. A hill lifting its head, on its long neck, and turning it toward the eddy, toward the steam.

And holding it there.

---

*Every one we kill*, the old man had said, in the barn, in the autumn, with his logs on the wall. *He eats.*

She remembered.

She sat on the greyhart and remembered it, and looked at the Last Titan with its head up, and knew.

And she couldn't have done anything else. It had come up under the batteries. It would have had the Bridle's engines and the magazine and a hundred casks of resin and the whole centre of the line. Something had to stop it. Something had.

She didn't say it. To anyone.

She'd say it to the old man, after, if there was an after. He'd know already.

---

She rode back across the Flat at dusk.

Past the Bridle.

---

The crews were at their braziers. Back at their bolt-throwers. Counting their dead, laying them in a row by the magazine under grey coats. Eleven.

The Bridle sat in its ring on its beds. The twelve engines, banked. Their fireboxes red and low. The chain along the cords. The ring in its cradle with its stage. The six jibs at the eddy's edge, the chain-ends hanging still.

Unfired.

---

She was glad.

She sat on the greyhart and looked at it and was glad of it, all the way through. It hadn't been needed. The wards had held. The chilled chains had held. The Lady had come. The line had held. On her word, and no other, and she hadn't said the word.

It sat there, cold, a fallback. As she'd said it would.

---

Davin came up beside her on his pony with his case open.

"Sixty-one," he said. "At the bend. Knights and militia." He turned a page. "Four olifants. Two hundred of the clans, in the flood. Three wyverns. One roc. Eleven of Kessmoor's." He closed the case. "Day one, Marshal."

"Day one," she said.

---

Across the river, over the Saltreach bank, there was smoke.

Black. From the scrub, where the Sun had burned it all day. Going up in a long low bank against the last red of the sky.

And against it, wings.

---

Three colours.

Red. Bronze. Green. Turning over the scrub end, slowly, in the last light, low, the three of them together, against the black smoke. Coming back from the Sun's hills.

And among them, grey. Rust-grey and white.

Rocs. Three. Yeva's. What was left of the four. Turning with the dragons over the burning scrub, wing to wing, as if they'd always flown together.

---

She watched them until they came down on the ridge.

Then she turned the greyhart and rode back up onto the wall, and along it, in the dark, from fire to fire, to the bend, saying the words to anyone who was still awake.
