---

Ildra didn't leave.

Arn had thought she might. All the first night, in the hall, in the dark, with his arms round her neck and her head on his chest. He'd thought: she'll go. In the morning. When she understands I can't hear her. She'll go up into the snow and not come down, as the freed did.

She didn't.

---

She lay by him.

On the hall's floor at the back, among the pillars, with the other nineteen. When he got up she got up. When he walked down the hall to the door she walked behind him. When he sat by the stone of the door she lay down with her chin on his boot.

She looked at him.

All the time. With her small gold eyes. As she'd always looked at him. Since the pens, since the dying red head in the straw.

He didn't know what she was thinking.

He'd always known. That was the bond. You knew.

He didn't know.

---

The storm dropped on the three-hundred-and-twenty-fifth.

---

Idony said so. On his hurdle. With his three flat glasses on the stone by him, that didn't move any more, that he looked at anyway.

"It's dropped," he said. "The wind." He listened. "Snow. Only snow."

They listened.

Through the crack high in the door. Nothing. No roar. No sea.

Snow.

---

The knight opened a way.

---

He watched her do it. Everyone did. She'd sat on the block of fallen stone inside the door for three days and nights and not moved, not eaten, not slept that he'd seen. On the morning the storm dropped she got up.

She went to the door-stone.

The wall of fallen mountain that filled the doorway. Grey. Cold. With the words over it on the lintel, white with dust.

She put her hand on it.

---

She watched her hand go flat. He'd seen her do that all winter. She couldn't feel it.

She didn't move. She stood with her bandaged hand on the stone.

And the stone opened.

---

Beside the lintel. Up. A passage. Narrow. A man's width. Going up through the fallen mountain at a slant, through the grey, to the outside. The stone not breaking. Not falling. Opening. Moving aside, round the line of it, as water opens round a hand you push into it.

Grey light at the top.

Cold air coming down it.

---

She took her hand off.

She looked at it. Then at the passage.

"Not the door," said the knight. To nobody. To all of them. "Not yet. It's warm in here." She looked up the slanting passage at the grey light. "It's winter out there."

---

He went up it.

With Ildra behind him. She had to fold everything, and squeeze. She did.

Up through the mountain. And out.

---

Into snow.

---

The bowl was changed.

He'd known it would be. He hadn't known how much. He stood at the top of the passage on the fallen stone and looked at Coldtarn, where he'd poured his father and Ulric and every rider of the Clan for three hundred years.

The western crag was gone.

---

It had come down. All of it. The crag over the door, and the Scarfell's shoulder above it. A new slope. Grey stone under snow. From the bowl's western rim all the way down to the tarn's edge. Burying the old slope. The door's step. The beck's mouth.

And under it, three.

The lion. His kin. And the bronze one, white with frost, that had lain across the beck.

He couldn't see them. He knew they were there.

---

The tarn was smaller.

The fall had taken a third of it. The western shore, the shallows, the pouring-stone where Aurveth had lain a year: under the stone. The black middle still open. Still steaming. In a ring of white ice. Smaller.

And Hollow Beck coming out somewhere further west, under the new slope, black, running away.

---

He looked at the sky.

Grey. Snow coming down out of it, slowly, straight, no wind.

He looked at the rim of the bowl. South. Where the Way came over from the moor.

No smoke.

---

He'd looked at the sky over the Scarfell all his life. There was always smoke. Varrhold's. The ledges' fires. The herdsmen's in the high glens. Harrow's End's, south, faint, in a good winter. Somewhere, always. A thread of it.

None.

Not anywhere he could see.

---

Ildra brought him a hare on the second day.

---

She'd gone off at dawn. He'd watched her go. Up off the fallen stone, small, red-gold, into the snow. He'd thought: now.

She came back at noon.

With a hare. White. In her jaws. She landed by him on the stone and put it down at his feet.

And waited, as a dog waits. Looking at him. With her head on one side.

---

"Thank you," he said.

She looked at him.

She didn't know what he'd said. He could see that. She knew his voice. She knew it meant something. She didn't know what.

He picked up the hare.

There was no fire. There'd been none since Liss's lamp ran out of oil. Nobody had lit one. He held the hare a while.

Then he put it down in front of her again.

She looked at it. At him. Then she ate it.

---

He rode her down to Varrhold on the three-hundred-and-twenty-sixth.

---

He didn't know if she'd let him.

He went to her in the morning on the fallen stone and stood by her shoulder, where he'd always got up. He didn't say anything. Saying things was no use now.

She looked at him.

He put his hand on her neck.

She put her shoulder down.

---

She knew him. That was all. She didn't know what he wanted. She knew him, and he'd always got up there, and she put her shoulder down.

He got up.

---

She flew where his weight leaned. He didn't ask; he couldn't. Where he leaned. Down the beck. West. Out of the bowl.

It was like riding a horse. He'd ridden horses as a boy, before the bond. It was like that. You leaned and the beast went, because it trusted you, not because it heard you.

He leaned.

She went.

---

Varrhold was gone.

---

He saw it from the air. From a long way off. Where the beck came out of the Scarfell's foot into the valley, and the hold had stood on its spur for three hundred years. Stone. Turf-roofed. The great hall. The byres. The pens, that he'd opened. The ledges going up the cliff above, the Roosts, where the dragons had slept.

A slope.

---

Grey. Under snow. Coming down off the Scarfell's western shoulder above the hold, all the way down, over the spur, over the hall, over the byres and the pens and the ledges. Into the valley.

The mountain had moved.

The night it came down at Coldtarn, when the lion asked it. The Scarfell's whole western shoulder had moved. Not only over the door. Over everything below it, down the beck.

Over Varrhold.

---

He landed Ildra on it.

On the snow. On the grey stone under it. Where the hall had been, he thought. He wasn't sure. He walked on it.

Nothing.

No smoke. No tracks. No sound. Snow on grey stone, all the way across the valley, smooth.

---

The overflow had come here.

Kit had sent them. From the step. *The rest to the caves. The hold at Varrhold. The ledges. The byres.* Thousands. Down the beck in the snow, on the day of the storm. He'd led some of them himself. He'd sorted them on the shore and pointed.

*Anywhere with a roof.*

The hold had a roof.

And the big ones had gone with them. Thirty pairs. Too big for any door. He'd sent them down the beck with the families on their backs, to the Roosts, where they'd always slept.

---

He stood on the snow for a long time.

Ildra stood by him. She didn't know what this was. She put her head down and smelled the snow. She looked at him.

---

The chair was under there.

He knew where. If he'd known where the hall was, he'd have known where the chair was. At the hall's head. On its step. Oak. Black with age. His father's. His grandfather's. The Varr chiefs', for three hundred years.

Ulric wouldn't sit in it. Arn hadn't either. They'd offered it to him on the coast, with the rocs asleep on the turf, and he'd said no, and nobody else had wanted it.

It was under the mountain somewhere.

Nobody would sit in it again.

---

On the way back he looked at the caves.

Across Coldtarn's bowl, on its southern side. The hatching caves, where the farmer's family had lived, where the farmer's mother had stood in the mouth with a lantern lit in daylight, waving the overflow in.

The mouth had fallen in.

Snow over it.

---

He didn't tell the farmer.

The farmer knew. The farmer's eyes saw to the edge of the world. He'd seen it before Arn had.

---

They poured on the three-hundred-and-twenty-eighth.

---

The pouring-stone was under the fall, with Aurveth. They poured from the new shore. From the fallen stone at the black water's edge, where the mountain had come down into the tarn and made a new lip, grey, raw, under snow.

At noon. In the snow. No wind.

---

Everyone who could came up the knight's passage.

The Clan's riders, and their dragons behind them, quiet. The farmer. His girl, with the old grey on a halter. His mother. His sister and her two. The Merrin woman and her brother. The ferrywoman, with her iron pin in her pocket. The smith. The old man from the Eelweir. The clerk, with his case. The old man from Vael, carried up on his hurdle by four of Kessmoor's crews. A knight with a bandaged arm and a grey hound. A Keeper boy and a lame wolf.

On the shore. By the black water. In the snow.

The knight above them, on the fallen stone by the passage's mouth. Sitting.

---

There were no ashes.

Nothing to burn. Nothing to burn with. No fire had been lit since Liss's lamp went out; nobody had thought to. The dead were under the mountain, or in the tarn, or on a top field a long day's walk off, or on the Vale.

No ashes.

---

He remembered the old priest.

At the coast, in the summer. When Ivo Brack and Sunniva had gone into the sea with no bodies. *The Clan will pour for them at Coldtarn. Without ashes. There are words for that.*

He'd asked what they were.

The old priest had told him. He'd been surprised Arn didn't know.

*Then pour your hands*, the old priest had said. *You hold them out. Empty. Over the water. And turn them over. So the water knows you'd have poured, if you'd had anything to pour.*

He'd poured them so, at Coldtarn, in the autumn, with those words.

The old priest was dead. On the Vale, or on the road.

Arn remembered.

---

Gannet first.

---

The Merrin woman asked. She came to him on the shore before the names and said, "Saltreach gives to the water. Can I?"

"Yes."

He was on the shore under a cloak, by the fall's edge. He'd been there since the day of the storm; the mountain had come down a pace from him and not on him. The Merrin woman's brother and the smith carried him to the black water. Wrapped in his own coat. She'd put stones from the fall in it, in the pockets, and closed it over.

There was a purse in the inside pocket. Arn saw her see it, and leave it.

They put him in.

---

The black water took him. He went down slowly, as the ash went down, and was gone.

The Merrin woman stood at the edge and held out her hand, flat, palm up, as if there were something on it.

"Salt between us," said Kit. "Nothing owed."

There was nothing on her hand.

---

Then the names.

---

He said them. As the priest would have.

He stood at the black water's edge with Ildra behind him, and the riders behind her, and said them into the snow.

---

"Kharrak," said Arn.

"Lisska. Aurveth."

He looked at the farmer. The farmer didn't move.

---

"Ulric," said Arn. "Son of Rurik. Champion of the Varr."

"Gunna. Daughter of Tarne. Rider of the ledges. And Tamsin."

"Bryn Tolle. Ivo Brack, and Sunniva. Asta Dunn of the grey." He went on. Every rider he could remember. The ones at Gullhaven. At the Thornwood. On the Vale. Every one. He knew them all. He'd poured for half of them.

"All the riders," he said, at the end. "All of them."

---

"Ilse Rowan," said Arn. "Marshal of the Accord."

The knight with the bandaged arm put his hand on his hound's head.

"Tobin. Of Sarovel. Who led the hands."

"Gannet. Of Blackwater."

---

He stopped.

He looked up. At the fallen stone above the passage. At the knight, sitting on it.

She looked back at him.

---

"Orrun," said Arn.

---

She didn't move.

---

"And the beasts," said Arn.

He hadn't known he was going to say it. It wasn't in the words. The priest's words were for riders and dragons. He said it anyway.

"Of every kingdom. That died on the line. The hounds. The greyharts. The olifants. The stonehorns. The wolves. The bears. The rocs. The wyverns. The lynx, and the cats. The ones the Kessmoor sheds took." He breathed. "All of them."

---

"Ash," said the knight with the bandaged arm. Quietly. On the shore. "Her name was Ash."

"Ash," said Arn.

The old man from the Eelweir said some names Arn didn't know. Harrow's End names. The Merrin boy said *Hal*. The ferrywoman said one, very quietly, that nobody heard.

---

Then the other thing.

The thing they always said.

---

"Who pours?" said Arn.

---

Nobody answered.

Nobody had ashes. Nobody was anyone's heir. There was no son to come out with the urn. There was no urn.

He stood at the water's edge.

---

He held out his hands.

Empty. Over the black water. Cupped. As if there were ash in them.

And turned them over.

---

Nothing fell.

The water lay black and still and steaming under his empty hands.

*So the water knows you'd have poured.*

---

He looked up.

Along the shore. At the farmer, standing with his girl and the grey.

"Pour with me," said Arn.

---

The farmer came.

Across the snow. To the water's edge. Beside him. And held out his hands.

Scarred. Pink. Burned, and healed, and burned again. With a fire in them now, under the skin, that Arn could almost see.

Empty.

He turned them over over the water.

Nothing fell.

---

Then the girl.

The farmer's. Wren. She hadn't been asked. She came to the water between them and held out her small hands, cupped, and turned them over.

Then the old woman. The farmer's mother.

Then the ferrywoman. Then the smith. Then the clerk, putting his case down in the snow to do it. The Merrin woman. Her brother. The riders. Along the shore. One by one. Then all of them.

Holding out empty hands over the black water and turning them over.

---

The dragons didn't make their sound.

The low sound. That went down into the ground. That they'd made at every pour Arn had ever been to, that the Clan said was them pouring too.

They stood behind their riders in the snow, quiet. Looking at the water. Looking at their people's hands.

They didn't know what this was.

Nobody could tell them.

---

Ildra put her head down.

Past him. Over his shoulder. Her small red-gold head, down, to the water. To the place where his empty hands had turned over.

And put her nose to it.

He'd seen the farmer's green one do that. In the Thornwood. Breathe on the ash so it went into the ground.

Ildra breathed on the black water. A small breath. The steam on it moved.

She didn't know why she'd done it. He could see that. She lifted her head and looked at him as if he might tell her.

He couldn't.

He put his hand on her neck.

---

The old woman sang.

At the end. When the hands had all been held out and turned over and taken back. She stood at the water's edge in her shawl and sang. Alone. Thin. Old. The song every hearth in the Marches had.

*The old ones went up into the light, and the one fell into six.*

He knew it. Everyone knew it. They listened.

She sang it to the end.

To the last line.

---

And stopped.

Before it.

---

Nobody sang it.

*Till the six are one again.*

Nobody finished it.

The old woman stood at the black water's edge with her mouth open for the last line and didn't sing it, and closed her mouth, and pulled her shawl round her, and went back up the shore.

---

The knight sat on the fallen stone all night.

---

He watched her. From the shore. He didn't go back down the passage. He sat on the new lip by the black water with Ildra lying against him and watched the knight on the stone above.

She didn't move.

---

It was cold. The coldest night of the winter. He'd been born on the Scarfell; he knew cold. The riders who'd stayed on the shore had their cloaks over their heads and their breath freezing on their beards. Ildra was warm against him. Nothing else was.

The knight sat on the fallen stone above the passage in a blue coat too big for her. No cloak. No hood. Her hands in her lap.

Snow fell on her.

On her shoulders. On her hair, the grey band over her ear. On her lap.

It didn't melt.

She didn't brush it off.

---

She wasn't shaking.

He'd seen her shake on the Vale, in Rhun's cloak, with her lips blue. He'd seen her shake on the step, counting, in the storm.

She sat in the snow all night on the stone over the lion and wasn't cold.

He didn't know how he knew. He knew.

She sat there till the grey came up, and the snow lay on her shoulders like a mantle, and she didn't move.
