In Hearthmere they say a bond-hound is the most common reason a knight dies of grief, and they say it the way they say that rivers flood: not to frighten anyone, only so nobody is surprised. The orders keep more hounds than any other beast, because a hound will go anywhere a horse will, and a good many places a horse won't, and it will stand a patrol in the rain for nine hours and be glad at the end of it. The knights who keep them tend to be quiet people, or they become quiet. Their hounds tend to be big and rough-coated and plain to look at. Nobody paints them on banners. The Brackenwood foresters, who have kept hounds since before there was a Greyhold, say there are only two things a hound needs to know in its life, and the rest is nose. Hold, and let go.

Bracken knew both. He knew them better than most.

---

He thought in lines.

Everything had one. A hare had a line, laid down through the wet grass in the morning, thin and hot and zigzag, and you put your nose to it and it pulled you. A fox had a line, rank, going under hedges. A man had a line: boots and sweat and whatever he'd eaten, and his fear if he had any, all of it lying along the ground behind him like a rope somebody had dropped. Some lines were old and went cold under your nose. Some were so new they were still warm. Some went into water and stopped.

The lead was a line too. The leather one, that she put on him in towns. You could feel her at the far end of it, even when it was slack.

And there was the other line. The one that came out from under his ribs on the left side, low down, where the hair was thin, and went to her. It had no smell. It never went cold. When she was beside him it was short, and when she was across the yard it was long, and when she was asleep in the long stone house on the far side of the yard and he was in his stall in the barn, it went out through the wall and across the cobbles and in at a window and lay there, slack, and he could feel her breathing on the end of it.

He'd had it since the barn.

Not this barn. The other one, on the farm in the south, with the apple trees outside the door and the straw on the floor deep enough to lose a pup in. He'd been the smallest of six. The others were already fat, and climbed on him, and got to the teats first, and he'd learned early that the way through a heap of brothers was low and quiet and without any fuss. So he had been low and quiet in the straw one morning when the door opened and the light came in and a girl came in with it.

She'd smelled of milk and apples and straw. She'd crouched down by the litter, and all his brothers had gone tumbling at her, yapping, climbing on each other to get to her hands.

He hadn't. He'd put his nose up.

There had been a line coming off her. Not a smell. Something underneath smells. It came across the straw to him, very thin, and he'd got up on his fat legs and walked along it through the straw, past all of them, and sat down on her foot, because that was where it ended.

She had looked down at him. He'd felt her look.

And the line had gone in under his ribs, on the left, and stayed.

---

The Long Barn smelled of thirty-one beasts and one man, and the man smelled of all thirty-one.

Bracken lay at the back of his stall in the dark before the bell with his chin on his paws and took the barn in through his nose, as he did every morning, all of it at once and then one line at a time. The hounds near him: Grip, old, with the sour teeth; Ash and Sorrow, who were litter-mates and slept in a heap and smelled of each other; the young bitch at the end who belonged to a knight Bracken had never once seen look at her. The martens, musky, in their wire. The fox-thing that bit. The hawks. The hawk, the big one, who belonged to the boy with the clean boots and who was asleep now, because the boy was asleep. The greyharts down the far end, grass and musk and old bone. Merrow's ox, Dun, whose breath came slow and huge from the stall across the way, like the wind in the apple trees at home. The dray-beasts. And at the very end, behind the iron-banded door, the boar, which smelled of black earth and blood and of thinking, and which every hound in the barn kept one ear on all night.

The cat was up on the hay in the rack above Sorrow's stall. He knew it was there. He didn't look at it. He never looked at it. It had been in the barn longer than any of them and it went where it liked and it smelled of dust and warm stone and nothing that wanted anything from a hound, and he had decided, his first week, that if he didn't look at it, it wasn't there. That had worked for two years.

The bald man came down the barn with the lantern before the bell, as he always did. The bald man had no beast and every beast. He smelled of tar soap and hoof-paring and Ash's ear-salve and the boar's straw and Bracken's own blanket, and of the cold yard, and of not sleeping much. He held the lantern up at each stall as he passed. He didn't speak. When he got to Bracken's door he held it up a moment longer, and Bracken lifted his head off his paws an inch, and the bald man went on.

Then the fork-girl.

She came in after the bald man with the barrow, her boots loud on the cobbles. She started at the east end, as she always did: Grip, then Ash and Sorrow, then the young bitch, then him. He heard her coming down the stalls, and the fork going in and out of the straw, and the barrow wheel squeaking, and he smelled her long before she got to his door.

The fork-girl smelled of horse, and of the midden, and of the long stone house, and of the river. Of a punt: wet wood, pitch. And under all of it, every morning, the island.

He didn't know it was an island. He knew it was a place across water, because the smell had water all round it. Old stone, and moss, and wet leaves. And something in the middle of the stone and the leaves that was so big it didn't smell like a beast at all. It smelled like weather. Like a hillside. It was on her hands and her knees and the hem of her coat, faint, gone into her, and every morning when she came to his stall door with the fork it came in with her.

He'd smelled it the first day. That was why he'd let her in, in the end. Not for her. For it.

Anyone who'd been that close to that, and come back whole, could come in.

She came in now, without stopping at the door. She had for weeks. He lay at the back by the wall on his blanket and watched her fork the straw. She didn't talk to him. She never did. The bald man didn't either. They were the only two who had ever come into his stall and not talked, and he liked them both for it, though he'd not have gone so far as to get up.

She went out with the barrow.

Then the line went short.

---

She came across the yard in the half-dark with her saddle on her hip and her breath going up white, and he was on his feet at the door before she'd reached the barn, with his tail going low and slow, as it only went for her.

*Bracken.*

That was his sound. She said it out loud as well, when she was in a mood to, but mostly she said it on the line, where it came in under his ribs whole, without having to go through his ears at all.

Her words came that way. All of them. When she spoke, out loud, he heard the noise of it, as he heard the bald man or the old man with the short hand or the rooks on the wall, and then he heard it again, underneath, the meaning, coming along the line from her into him. When anybody else spoke it was noise and nothing under it. Rooks. He could tell from the noise whether a man was angry, or frightened, or lying. He could tell from her, on the end of the line, what she thought of it. That was all.

*We're going out,* she said. *All day. Pell's taking us up the river.*

He knew *out*. He knew *all day*. He went round her in the door of the barn and put his head under her free hand.

She smelled of boot-wax and wool and the long stone house. And under them, the straw. Milk and apples. Five years, and it was still there.

---

There were seven of them, and he knew them all by their lines.

The old man with the short hand, at the front, on the bay horse. Horse liniment and pipe and iron, and the hand that had two fingers gone, which Bracken had smelled once, close, in the yard, and found it smelled of nothing at all where the fingers should be. Grip's man, and Ash and Sorrow's man, the two from the gate, with their hounds at their stirrups. Grip trotting stiff and silent. Ash and Sorrow on either side of their man's horse, a body's width off, never further. The hawk's boy, with the hawk hooded on his fist. The one with nothing at his knee, sour, on a cob that hated him. The fork-girl at the back with the spare horses, on the grey with the knocking thing tied to its bridle that smelled of a turnip a very long time dead.

And her, on the pony. And him, at the pony's near side, at her stirrup, where she could put her hand down.

He loved this. He loved anything that went somewhere. The road was all lines, hundreds of them, crossing and going off into hedges and coming back, carters and drovers and dogs and a badger in the night and a stoat with a rabbit, and the river beside them the whole way, huge and brown and cold and full of its own smells that went by too fast to follow. He loped beside the pony with his tongue out and his nose going and the line from under his ribs short and warm, and he was as happy as a hound can be.

They went past a jetty, and a house on it, and a woman at a rope who lifted one hand. They went past a long stretch of bank, and out in the river, across the water, there was a low dark shape with stones sticking up out of it and trees.

The smell came off it across the water on the wind.

He stopped.

He stood in the road with his nose up and his torn ear flat, and the smell came, faint and broad, over the river. Old stone. Wet leaves. Weather. A hillside that breathed.

Behind him the fork-girl was looking too. He didn't need to turn round to know it. They both looked at the island.

*Bracken,* said Ide. *Come on.*

He came on.

---

They went into a great many places that day, and in every one the people smelled the same. Of their houses and their fires and their dinner, and over it, the thing. He knew the thing. He'd smelled it at the uncle's farm the winter the fox got in among the hens and the uncle sat up with a lantern every night after. It was a sharp smell, thin, that came off the skin. The people stood in their doorways and talked to the short-handed man and smelled of it, and looked at the river.

Late in the day they turned away from the river, inland, along a lane between hedges, and the light went grey and blue, and they came to a place with stone houses round a patch of grass, and a pump, and a forge at the bottom of the grass with its doors open and the red coming out.

There was an old man going round the grass with a staff, stopping at posts. At each post he said a noise, the same noise each time, short and flat, and the top of the post went white.

Bracken didn't like that. He never had. When the knights said their bought words, the noise came out of them with no line under it, nothing in it at all, like a bark from a dog that's been taught to bark, and then something happened anyway. The bald man never said them. Bracken watched the old man go round and the posts go white, and his hackles came up a little, along his back, and went down again.

There were beasts here. He took them in. A great old ox, somewhere along the water, as old as Dun and smelling like him, of slow breath and turned earth. Two greyharts in a paddock behind a little building, strange ones, not the barn's, white and musky and frightened of everything. And a hound. In the forge, or behind it. A big dog, a smith's dog, coal-dust and burnt hoof and a bitch it had been with not long ago.

The short-handed man stopped his bay by the pump. The smith came out and talked. The pony stood. Ide's hand came down onto Bracken's neck, idle, the fingers in the rough of his coat.

Then the wind came up the lane.

It was hardly a wind. A stir. The cold air coming up off water, as it does at dusk, a breath of it, up between the hedges from the bottom of the lane. And in it.

Men. Many. Wool and sweat and leather, and the river, and tar, and wet wood. Boats. And the thin sharp smell, a lot of it, gone sour, the fear that men get when they've been waiting in the dark a long time. And over all of it, on their coats, in the wool, a smoke.

Sweet. Burnt sweet. Like the comb the uncle took from the hives in autumn, if you threw it on the fire.

He'd smelled it once before. In the summer, on a road, by a little stone shrine, where men had taken a crate off a cart and carried it into the trees. The crate had a beast in it, and the men had that smoke on them, and he hadn't known what it was and he hadn't liked it then. He liked it less now. It got into his nose and went on going in, further than a smell should go.

His head came up. His ear went flat. He went still all over.

He was looking down the lane.

Along the line, he felt her feel him. Felt her hand go still on his neck. Felt her look where he was looking, and see nothing, and know.

"Instructor," she said. Quietly.

He heard the noise, small, in the cold. And under it, the meaning, coming in under his ribs: *something's there, something's coming, look.*

Then wood on stone, from the bottom of the lane. And a voice, cut off.

---

They came up out of the dark at the bottom of the lane all at once, and they didn't come at the seven.

He'd have understood that. A man coming at you was a line you could take. These came up the lane and broke into pieces, some one way and some another, round the little building, along the water, across the grass, quick, as hares break in a field when the dogs come, except that hares run from something and these were running to it. There was a noise above all of it that he didn't know. A high thin noise, two short and then quiet. It came from up on the roof of the forge.

The short-handed man shouted. Noise. Ide moved under it, and the pony moved under her, and the line went taut toward the middle of the grass where four men were standing with iron in their hands.

"*Darrow, with me!*"

That was her other sound. Her knights' sound. When the short-handed man said it she always moved.

Bracken went with her. There was nowhere else he would have gone.

A man came round the pump.

He came low and fast at the pony's legs with something in his hand that smelled of oil and edges, and the pony shied, and Ide's weight went sideways in the saddle and her fear came down the line all at once, short and hard, a jerk.

Bracken took him.

He didn't think about it. There was nothing to think. He went in at the arm with the edge in it, as he'd been taught, and his teeth went through the wool and the sweet smoke in the wool and into the arm, and the man went down on the stones by the pump with Bracken on him, and the edge fell out of the hand and rang.

He held.

That was the first thing. Hold. He had the arm, and the man under him, and the man's line, sweat and the smoke and blood now, hot, in his mouth. The man was shouting. Noise. The man's other fist hit him in the head, and again, and the man's boot came up and caught him in the near shoulder, hard, a crack that went all down that leg. He held. He set his feet and leaned his weight back and the arm came with him and the man shouted higher.

Everywhere round him there was noise. The high thin noise from the roof again. One long one. Horses. A woman, screaming over and over, and a child. The hawk, screaming too, somewhere over the water. The greyharts. Hooves. He didn't have room for any of it. He had the arm.

And the line. It was there, along the edge of everything, taut and hot. She was close. She was on the pony, still, behind him, up. She was frightened, and not of the man, and she was angry, and under the anger there was something else, wide and cold and hard to stand near, that came from her whenever he had his teeth in something. He didn't know what that was. It came from her into him, and he held anyway.

The man under him stopped shouting and started to make a different noise, lower.

*Let go.*

It came in under his ribs. Not loud. She never said it loud.

He let go.

He opened his jaws and took his teeth out of the arm and stepped back off the man, and stood, with the blood in his mouth and his leg shaking and his hackles up, and the man got up off the stones holding his arm and ran, low, past the pump and down the lane, and Bracken stood and watched him go and didn't go after.

That was the other thing. Let go. It was hers. He'd never once not.

*Good,* she said. *Good. Stay with me.*

He stayed with her.

---

It went on a little while longer and then it stopped. The high thin noise from the roof went one last time, long and falling, like a curlew over a marsh at home, and all the men went down the lane together, walking, not running, and the lines of them went down into the water and stopped.

And under them, going down the lane with them, the smith's dog.

He smelled it go. Coal-dust and burnt hoof, and fear, high and thin, and a rope, and the sweet smoke all round it. Down the lane between the hedges to the bottom, where the water was, and onto wood that rocked, and then out, away, along the water, fainter, fainter. The greyharts went too, and the great old ox, slow, and the boats went off into the dark down the water with all of it, and the lines went with the boats and thinned out over the water and were gone.

He stood by the pump with his near shoulder hurting and smelled where the smith's dog had been.

There was a barn burning. There was a man sitting in a doorway with his face in his hands, who smelled of the great old ox, all over, and of nothing else, and who didn't move.

---

They rode back along the water in the dark.

Nobody talked. The short-handed man had blood on his face and a strip of cloth round his head that smelled of Ash and Sorrow's man. Grip's man had an arm that smelled of his own blood. The hawk's boy had the hawk on his fist with the hood off, and kept stroking it with one finger, and the hawk let him, which it never did. Ide rode at the back, beside the fork-girl on the grey. Bracken loped at her stirrup. The near leg was bad. Every time it came down he felt the boot in it again. He didn't stop. He didn't make a sound.

She kept looking down at him. He felt her look.

The fork-girl looked too. She smelled of running, and of something she'd seen that had frightened her worse than the men, and under it the island.

"He's all right," said Ide.

The noise, and then under it the meaning, plain. He looked up at her.

"It's the raider's," she said. "He let go when I told him." The line went quiet for a while. "He always lets go when I tell him."

The fork-girl made a short noise. Rooks.

Then Ide said, lower, so low that if it hadn't come down the line too he wouldn't have heard it at all over the hooves, "I'd have let him keep going. If it'd been me on the ground. I'd have let him."

He understood every word of it.

He understood *let him* and *keep going* and *me on the ground*. They came in under his ribs one after another, and they lay there, and the thing that came with them was the wide cold thing, the thing that was hard to stand near, the thing that came from her whenever he had his teeth in something and that he'd never known the name of. He knew it now, a little. It had her in it, on the ground, and him over her, holding, and nobody to say *let go*.

He didn't like it. He put his head down and went on along the road with the bad leg coming down hard every stride, and he didn't look up at her again until they could smell the tower.

---

At the tower there was a fire and a great many men and food, and he lay on the stones of the yard by the pony's feet while she saw to the pony, and then on the floor by the fire with his back against her legs while the men made their noise. A man by the fire made a lot of noise, and the others laughed. The short-handed man didn't. Bracken felt Ide not laugh too. She didn't think it was funny, whatever it was; she thought it was frightening, and a little bit wonderful, and the line was full of something high and far off that he didn't understand at all. Wings, maybe. She'd been thinking of wings lately. Something big in the sky.

He put his chin on her boot. The near leg throbbed.

Then home, in the morning, the long way down the water, past the island again. He didn't stop for it this time. He looked.

---

In the barn the bald man went over the near shoulder with his big rough hands, slowly, the whole of it, from the withers to the elbow, pressing, and Bracken stood for it and looked at the wall. The bald man smelled of tar soap and the boar's straw. When he was done he put salve on it, the stinking salve, and didn't say a single bought word over it, and went away.

The leg got better. It took five days. The fork-girl mucked him out each morning and looked at the leg and didn't say anything either.

The days went as the barn days went. The bell. The bald man's lantern. The fork. Ide across the yard with her breath going white, and the line going short, and *Bracken*, and the yard, and the sand, and the posts. At the posts she worked him at his work, slow because of the leg, as he'd learned it at the uncle's farm and at the school after: go, and hold, and let go, and come back to her knee. Go, and hold. Let go. Back to her knee. He could have done it asleep. He did it anyway, every time, as if he'd never done it, because she asked.

In the afternoons she sat on the long bench under the wall with the others, with his head on her knee, and an instructor talked.

He remembered one of those. In the summer. The short-handed man at the front, with a spear, talking and talking, and the cat up on the wall top in the sun, where nobody had put it. He hadn't understood any of the short-handed man's noise. Only her. And at one place in the noise, her hand had stopped on his head.

Not taken away. Stopped. Gone still between his ears, as if it had forgotten what it was for.

And the wide cold thing had come down the line into him for the first time. There had been no man on the ground. Something in the short-handed man's noise had made her see a girl, a long way off, on stones, dropping. He had felt the girl drop, through Ide. As if somebody had cut the line.

He'd got up off the bench that day, and put his whole head in her lap, and she'd put her other hand on it too, and after a while the cold went.

He thought about that sometimes now, lying in the straw. It had a line, that afternoon, and the line went somewhere he didn't want to follow.

---

In the evenings she came to his stall.

Not every evening. Most. After the bell for the end of the light, when the yard went dark and the long stone house filled with noise, she'd come across to the barn with a lantern and a board and a little pot that smelled of soot, and let herself in at his door, and sit down in the straw with her back against him. He lay along the back wall on the old grey horse-blanket with his legs out, and she leaned on him, against his ribs, on the left, where the line was, and put the board on her knees and made marks on it with a stick.

She was talking to the people at home. He knew that. Not out loud. But the line was full of them while she did it. Her mother, big and red and loud, who smelled of bread and the dairy and the uncle's farm. Her brothers. The apple trees. The barn with the straw in it. All of it went by along the line while the stick scratched, warm, and the soot smell came up off the board, and he lay with his chin on his paws and let it go by.

Some evenings, the fork-girl went by too.

He could feel her in it. The fork-girl, who had stood on her own at the back of the year with nobody beside her. Ide had thought about her a great deal since the morning the bald man told her about the stall. She'd thought about her lying in the long stone house with her side bound, and taken her something hot in a bowl. She thought about her now, writing, and the line went warm and a little sorry, and he felt Ide make a mark for her on the board.

*You let her in,* Ide said to him, one night, out of nowhere. *Didn't you. First. Before anyone.*

He thumped his tail once on the blanket.

*Why her?*

He didn't have a way to tell her. The island. Something on the island as big as a hill, that breathed. She didn't know about it. The fork-girl had never told her, and she couldn't smell it, being only a person. He held it up as best he could and pushed, and he felt it go along the line and come out at her end as nothing much. A smell of wet leaves. A cold.

*Hm,* said Ide. She went back to the board.

He sent her the only thing he usually sent her. He didn't send much. He wasn't a hound for it.

*Here.*

She put her hand back without looking, and found his ear, the torn one, and rubbed the stump of it between her finger and thumb, where the badger had had it off his first winter. *I know,* she said. *I know you are.*

---

On the seventh morning after the pump, the barn knew before the bell.

He woke in the dark and lay still with his nose up. Something was wrong in the smell. Not a new smell. A smell that had stopped.

Dun.

The slow huge breath from across the way, like wind in the apple trees, that had been there every night he had ever slept in the barn. It wasn't there. He lay and listened for it, and smelled for it, and it didn't come. There was the old ox's body, and the straw, and the warmth still going out of both. There was the old man on his stool in the corner of the stall, very small, smelling of old man and old ox in equal parts, so that it was hard to tell where one stopped. There was the cat. Curled up in the straw against the great grey belly, warm, still. And something else he had no smell for: a hot stone, a stone that had been told to be warm and was, that the bald man had put in there at the first cold nights.

The old man had his hand on Dun's face.

All down the barn the beasts lay awake and said nothing.

The fork-girl came in with the barrow before the bell and stopped in the door of Dun's stall, and he heard the old man make a small noise to her. Rooks. Then the fork-girl went fast out of the barn, and came back with the bald man, and then Ide was there too. The line went short in a rush and he was up at his door.

She didn't come to him. She went into Dun's stall.

They carried the old man out between them. The bald man and the fork-girl and her. Through the barn, past his door. He smelled the old man go by, close, and the old man was very light. He felt that through her. How light. Like carrying a bundle of hay. Like carrying nothing. The line was full of it: how small the old man was in her arms, how his hands were, how he'd sat with the ox all those mornings, and the ox was in the straw behind her not breathing, and the old man was still breathing, still, a little, and was going to stop.

She knew he was going to stop. That was what was coming down the line, ahead of any grief: knowing.

*He's going to stop,* she thought, to nobody. She didn't mean to send it. It came anyway, as the strong ones did.

He stood at his door with his nose through the bars and watched them carry the old man out into the grey yard.

---

The old man stopped at the change of the watch, at the end of the day. Bracken felt it through her. She was in a room with the old man and some others when it happened, and the line went quiet, and then her end of it went tight and hot and wet, and he lay in his stall with his chin on his paws and took it, all of it, as it came, and didn't move.

That night nobody in the barn moved.

The fork-girl came in for the evening muck and every beast was awake and still. The hounds lay in their stalls with their chins on their paws. Grip, Ash and Sorrow in their heap, the young bitch at the end. The greyharts with their heads down. The big hawk on her perch with her feathers flat, not bating, not anything. Even the boar, behind its iron door, with its small red eyes open, looking across at the stall where Dun had been.

He looked too. Dun's stall was empty. They'd taken the old ox out in the afternoon on a great wide sledge with all the men pulling, and the straw still held the shape of him, a long hollow, and the smell, going cold now.

And in the hollow, curled up against the place where the great grey body had lain, the cat.

It was looking at him.

He looked at the wall.

---

The next day there was a fire on the shore at dusk.

He didn't go down to it. Ide had gone with the others and left him, and he hadn't asked. He stayed in his stall and smelled it come up from the river on the cold air and in at the barn door. Wood and oil, and the old man, and the ox. Together. One smoke. It went up straight; he could tell from how it came in, thin and even, without any wind in it. Every beast in the barn smelled it and lay still.

Through the line, Ide stood on the shore in a great many people and watched the fire and didn't cry. Something came off her as she stood there that was not the wide cold thing exactly. It was quieter. It was the old man and the ox going up together in one smoke. It was a thing she was looking at hard, as if she were trying to learn the way to somewhere.

She came to his stall after. Late, when the long stone house had gone quiet. No lantern, no board. She let herself in and sat down in the straw and leaned against him, on his ribs, on the left, and pulled her knees up.

"They went together," she said out loud, to nobody.

*They went together.* The noise and then the meaning. He knew *went*. He knew *together*. Together was him at her stirrup. Together was the line short.

She was quiet. Her back was against his ribs and he could feel her breathe there, through his own side, and the line between them was so short it was hardly a line at all.

*Bracken.*

He lifted his head.

*If it's me,* she said, *first. If it's ever me. You're not to...*

And stopped.

She didn't finish it. He felt her not finish it. He felt her take the end of it and hold it in and not send it, as she could when she wanted, and what was left in the line was only the shape of the start, and something underneath that came out anyway, because the strong things always did. A road. Him, going on along it. Her, not on it.

He didn't know *if*. He'd never known *if*. There wasn't a smell for it.

He put his head down across her knees, the whole weight of it, and lay there.

After a while she put her hand on him. Then she laughed, small and wet, at nothing, at herself, and said, "You great lump," out loud, and rubbed his ear.

He didn't go to sleep until she went.

---

The cold came down the river after that, a little more every night.

He felt it in the morning in the yard. The cobbles were hard and loud under his nails. The water in the trough had a skin on it that cracked when you put your nose in. The smells were sharper, and stayed lower, and didn't travel. The river had a new smell: ice. Out at the edges, in the reeds, where it ran slowest. It made a sound at night that came all the way up to the barn, a long slow grinding, like the uncle's cart on the stones.

The fork-girl smelled less of the island. Some mornings she smelled of it and some she didn't. The mornings she didn't, she smelled of not sleeping instead.

Ide worked him at the posts in the cold. Go, and hold, and let go, and come back. Her breath went up white. The line was quiet, these days. Quiet and short and steady, and every so often, out of nowhere, while she was cleaning her boots or rubbing his ear or sitting on the bench with his head on her knee, she would think of the shore, and the one smoke going up, and the start of the thing she hadn't finished. And then she would put it away.

He let her. He didn't push at it. He was not a hound for pushing.

---

On the last day the short-handed man came across the yard in the afternoon and made his noise, and Ide went for her saddle.

*Up the river,* she said. *Back to the tower. Tonight. All of us.*

He knew *tower*. He knew *tonight*. His tail went.

They went up the road in the last of the light, the seven, the same as before, with Grip and Ash and Sorrow loping and the hawk hooded and the grey with its old turnip knocking. The sky was clear and very high, and green at the bottom where the sun had gone, and the cold came down out of it as they went, so that by the time they got to the tower the grass at the side of the road was crisp under his feet and breaking.

There was the yard, and the fire, and the men. There was food. There was the floor of the guardroom, by the hearth, where they all lay down in their cloaks, the seven, close, and the hounds among them.

Ide lay on her side with her back to the fire. He lay along her front, against her, with his back to her chest and his head under her chin, and she put her arm over him. The line was so short it was nothing. He could feel her heart through his own back, on the left, under his ribs.

Outside the frost came down.

He heard it come. It came down on the yard and the tower and the road and the river all at once in the night, like a hand laid flat, and everything that had been soft went hard. He felt the stones of the floor go cold under his hip. He heard the river change its noise, out in the dark, going slower, thicker. Ide slept. The fork-girl, two bodies off, didn't; he could smell it on her, the not-sleeping, and the island faint, and the river.

He slept a little. Not much. A hound in a strange place sleeps with one ear.

---

The fork-girl went out before the light.

He heard her get up and step over the others and go to the door, and the cold came in at the door, a great slab of it, and went out again when the door shut. He lifted his head. Ide didn't wake. He put his head down again across her arm.

He was lying like that, with his nose toward the door, when it came.

Wood on stone.

From down below the yard, at the bottom of the steps, where the water was. Faint. And then again. And a voice, cut off.

He was on his feet before the fork-girl shouted.

---

The shout came in through the door and the whole floor came up. The short-handed man first, with his iron already in his hand, and the river man behind him with a long pole, and Grip's man, and Ash and Sorrow's man, and the boy and his hawk and the sour one. The door banged open. The cold. Ide came up off the floor in one move with her iron, and he was at her knee before she was standing, and they went out.

The yard was white.

It was white all over, every stone, the steps, the rail, the trough. It went in at his nose like water, so cold it had no smell at all for a breath, and then it had every smell at once, all of them sharp and close, lying flat along the ground in the frost where they'd been laid. The fork-girl was at the top of the steps with a lantern and a stick. The steps went down from the edge of the yard to the water, steep, white, and at the bottom there was a stone place by the river with the long boats tied to it, grey, and two other boats, low ones, that hadn't been there in the night.

And men. Twenty. On the stone place among the long boats, with axes. Masked. Wool and sweat and tar and, all over them, gone into their coats, the sweet burnt smoke.

The short-handed man and the river man and the others went down the steps, sliding, and the men on the stone place turned round.

Ide stopped at the top of the steps. Behind the fork-girl. He stopped with her, at her knee, with his hackles up from his skull to his tail.

Then the hounds came up out of the low boats.

---

He smelled them, and there was nothing to smell.

That was the first thing and it was the worst. Six. They came up over the sides of the boats onto the stone place, one after another, big, rough-coated, brindled, heavier than him, with iron round their necks, and they had bodies and feet and teeth and he could see them, and they had no line.

A hound has a line. Every hound he had ever known. You put your nose to a hound, any hound, the meanest yard-cur at the meanest farm, and it came off them strong: what they'd eaten, and who their bitch was, and whether they were afraid of you, and what they wanted. These smelled of iron, and the sweet smoke, thick, and of the barge, and of hound, plain hound, the body of it. And nothing else. Nothing that wanted anything. Nothing on the end of anything. As if you'd put your nose down to a line on the grass and found it laid by something that had never been there.

They didn't bark. They came up the stone place in a row in the frost without a sound and they all looked the same way at the same moment, and he felt every hair on him stand.

In the stern of the near boat there was a man in grey with his arm out.

That man had a line. Tallow, and boot-leather, and the sweet smoke, old, gone deep. No fear in it. Nothing much in it at all. Standing there with his arm out over the water, not watching, and when the arm moved, the six moved. All of them. One dog, with six bodies.

They went at the men from the tower. Two at Grip's man, two at Ash and Sorrow's man, two at the short-handed man. And Ash went in at one of them, giving tongue, and opened its shoulder to the bone; he smelled the blood go, hot, a great gout of it in the cold. And it didn't turn. It didn't stop. It didn't make a sound. Its own blood went down its leg into the frost and it went on at Ash and Sorrow's man as if the blood were somebody else's.

On the end of the line, Ide was frightened.

It wasn't the short jerk of the pony shying. It was low and long and climbing. She was looking at the six with no lines and she was understanding something and she didn't want to understand it.

He pressed against her knee. *Here.*

Her hand came down and found his neck and gripped the coat there, hard.

---

The short-handed man went down.

Bracken saw it. At the bottom of the steps, on the stone place, with his iron, against a man with an axe, and one of the six came at him low from the side, and took his leg, and he went over on his back on the frozen stones. The hound with no line on his legs. The man with the axe over him, and the axe going up.

And the line from Ide.

It came all at once, without a word. The wide cold thing, the one that was hard to stand near, but turned round, the other way out, pointed at the short-handed man on the stones. She was going to go. He felt her go in herself before her body did. Her weight came forward onto the top step with the iron in her hand. She was going down the steps into it.

She didn't tell him to.

He went first.

---

Past the fork-girl's knee, a rush, down the white steps, low, fast, his nails going on the frost and not caring, silent. He didn't make a sound when he went for something. He never had. He was down the steps and across the stone place before the axe came down and he hit the man with the axe at the knees with his whole weight, shoulder first, the good shoulder, and the man went over backwards off the edge of the stone place into the river, a great flat shout of water, and the axe went spinning away across the stones.

Bracken turned.

The two that had been on Grip's man had left Grip's man. Both at once. The arm in the stern had swung, and they came at him across the stone place, low, together, silent.

He fought them.

He'd been taught. At the uncle's farm by the old dog there, and in the straw with his brothers, and at the posts with her, five years, go and hold and let go. He went under the first one and took its foreleg and threw his weight and it went down, and the second was on his back with its teeth in his neck, in the thick of the coat, and he twisted out from under it and its teeth tore and he didn't feel that. It came again. He met it.

It didn't feel anything. He could tell. He bit it and it didn't flinch, and it didn't smell of hurt, and it didn't smell of anything. It was like fighting a hedge. Like fighting the wind off the river. It came at him and came at him with nothing behind its eyes, and the first one was up again with its foreleg dragging, and coming, and not minding.

The line was taut and burning. She'd started down the steps after him. He could feel her on the steps behind him, coming, slipping on the frost, with her terror in him like a second heart, beating against the first.

He got the second one by the throat.

He got it as he'd got the poacher's arm on the road in the wood, two winters ago, when the man came at her with a knife and the knife went down his muzzle and he didn't feel it then either. He got under the jaw and in, and his teeth met in the thick of the throat, and he set his feet on the frost, and he bore down.

It went down under him. Its feet went on scraping at the stones, and its body went on trying, as a cut snake does, and he held. Hold. Hold. He had it. He had it by the throat on the stone place in the frost with the river going by black and slow and the ice grinding out at the edges, and his own blood running in his eyes, and he was not going to let go, nobody was going to come up these steps behind him while he had it. Hold.

Then the noise from the boat.

*Thock.*

---

He didn't hear it hit.

He felt it. In his left side. Low down under the ribs, where the hair was thin, where she leaned her back against him in the evenings when she wrote to the people at home. Where the line came out.

It went in there.

Everything stopped. The stone place stopped. The river. The man in the stern with his arm out. The hound under his jaws, still scraping. All of it went a long way off and very small, as things do when you're looking at them from the top of a hill, and he stood on the frost with his feet set and the throat in his teeth and didn't move.

Something was going out of him.

Out of the hole under his ribs. Not blood; there was blood, but that was nothing. Something under the blood. It was going out down a slope, as a line goes down into water, and he couldn't follow it and couldn't stop it. It didn't hurt. It was cold. It was the coldest thing. It was cold and going down.

And the line went with it.

He felt that. He felt it as plainly as he'd ever felt anything. The line from under his ribs to her, ten paces up, behind him, on the steps. It had never once in five years gone anywhere but to her. Now it was going down. Down the slope with him, out through the hole, the far end of it still fast in her, and it was drawing. Pulling. Taking her end with it, down.

He felt her come.

Not her body. Her. He felt her stop on the steps and put her hand to her side where his hole was, and fold round it, and go white all through, and start to come down the line after him. As if he'd gone off along a road in the dark and she'd got up and was following. As if the line were a lead and he'd pulled.

He hadn't pulled. He hadn't meant to pull.

He didn't want her on this road. There was nothing on it. It was cold and it went down and there was no end to it that he could smell. He had the throat in his jaws and he was holding, and holding was the thing he did, and it was the wrong thing. It was the thing that was pulling her.

He had to let go.

She hadn't said. She wasn't saying. She couldn't; she was on the step with her mouth open and nothing coming out of it, and on the line there were no words at all, only her, coming. Nobody was going to say *let go* to him.

He'd never let go of anything she hadn't told him to. Not once. Not the poacher, not the man at the pump, not the arm or the leg or the throat of anything, in five years. It was hers. Let go was hers. Hold was his and let go was hers and that was how it went and how it had always gone, and that was what she loved, that was what she told people. *He always lets go when I tell him.*

He let go anyway.

He opened his jaws. Slowly. Gently, as he always did, when she said. He took his teeth out of the throat of the hound with no line, and it lay on the stones under him and didn't get up.

And with it, the only way he knew, he tried to let go of her.

He didn't know how. A hound doesn't. There was nothing in him for it but the one thing, the opening, the taking out of the teeth. He did that. He did it with all of himself, as he'd done it at the pump and on the road in the wood. Let go. Let go of her. Leave her on the steps where she was. Don't pull.

The line didn't let go.

He felt it not. It stayed fast. It went on going down out of the hole in his side into the cold, and her end came on along it, slowly, and there was nothing he could open to stop it.

---

His legs went, the back ones first, and then the front, slowly, and he lay down. On the frost, on his side, on the stone place at the bottom of the steps, with his head toward her. As he'd lain down by the fire in the uncle's kitchen, the winter of the badger, when he was small and his ear hurt and she put a sack over him. As he lay at the back of his stall on the old grey horse-blanket in the evenings, with his chin on his paws, when she was late, and the line went out across the yard to the long stone house and lay there slack, and he could feel her breathing on the end of it.

Up the steps, somebody screamed.

He knew who.

Everything else was a long way off now. The stone place. The boats. The men from the tower coming down with their poles. None of it near. The steps were near, though. White. And on them, ten paces up, her, standing, with her iron in one hand and the other pressed flat against her side, under her ribs, on the left, where his hole was. Her face was the colour of the frost.

She looked down at him. Her mouth opened and nothing came out.

Then her knees went, and the fork-girl dropped the lantern and caught her, and went down with her on the steps, and held her.

Her eyes were open. She was looking at him past the fork-girl's shoulder.

He felt her look.

He could feel her on the end of the line. Breathing.

He looked up the steps at her, as he'd looked up at her on the road in the wood with the poacher's arm let go, and the blood in his mouth, and his tail going a little in the leaves. *Was that right?*

It was cold. The frost was coming up into his side from the stone. The line went down and down and didn't end, and somewhere along it she was coming, slowly, not able to stop, and he couldn't do anything about it now. He'd let go. He'd done the one thing.

There was only one other thing a hound knows how to do, when his girl is behind him on a road and coming.

He put his chin down on the frost, facing the steps.

And waited for her.
