The Clan says a chief's younger son is born with his hands empty, so that he can carry things for other people. They say it kindly. It means he'll never sit in the chair, and he may as well be useful.

Teo Varr had always liked the saying. He liked being useful. He liked a great many things that his brother thought were beneath a Varr, such as lambing, and dancing at fairs, and sitting on the third ledge of the Roosts with his legs over the edge to watch the riders go out, and he had never minded his empty hands at all until the night they carried his father into the hall.

---

Haldor Varr came down into the hall from the chamber where he had died, on a bier of ash-poles, carried by six of his old riders, with his face uncovered as the Clan's dead always are. He had been a long time going. He had been a big man and the sickness had taken the big off him slowly, a little each month, like snow going off the moor, until by the end there was only the nose left and the hands, and the hands lay on his chest now crossed over the hilt of his sword.

The whole hold stood when he came in. That was the custom. Every bench in the long hall scraped back at once, and the sound went up into the scale roof and hung there.

Teo was standing at the foot of the dais, beside his brother. He saw it because he was looking for it. He'd been looking for it all day. When there was trouble in a room his mind always went out ahead of him, to the place where it would start.

At the right-hand table his uncle Ulric sat on with a cup in his hand and watched his brother go by.

He didn't do anything. He didn't say anything. He sat, as a rock sits in a beck, and the hall stood up round him. And the men at his table, who had begun to rise, looked at him and sat down again, one after another, slowly, until the whole right-hand table was sitting and the rest of the hall was standing, and there was a line through the room that hadn't been there in the morning.

Arn's hand closed on Teo's arm above the elbow.

"Don't," said Teo, low.

"He's sitting."

"I can see he's sitting. Everybody can see he's sitting. That's why he's doing it." The bier went up the dais. The riders set it down on the trestles in front of the chair. "Let him sit. Father won't mind. Father's past minding."

Arn let go of his arm. He walked up the dais after the bier, and turned at the top, beside the chair, with their father lying at his feet, and said to the right-hand table in a clear carrying voice, as if he were calling the dues, that his uncle had been waiting thirty years for that chair and could wait another thirty.

Nobody laughed. Nobody moved. Ulric drank from his cup and set it down.

Teo stood at the foot of the dais and thought, very clearly: *there it is, then.* He thought it as a rider thinks it when his horse puts a foot in a hole at the gallop, before he's come off, while there's still a moment to choose how he'll land.

His father's grey riding cloak was over his own arm. He'd brought it down from the chamber because somebody had to and nobody else had thought of it. He went up the dais steps past his brother, and past the bier, and threw the cloak over the chief's chair, the black oak one with the dragons on the arms, so that it covered the seat and the back and hung down over the carved feet.

Then he turned round to the hall.

"He's still using it," he said. "Give him the month."

A few people laughed, as people laugh at a burning, too loud and grateful for it. It wasn't much. It was enough to get everybody sitting down. He sat on the edge of the dais with his feet on the step and his back against his father's bier, and drank whatever was put in front of him, and smiled at both tables, and didn't look at the chair.

The next evening they lit the pyre in the yard, and it burned for eleven days.

---

He couldn't fly the border. He'd known he wouldn't.

On the third day the Clan flew the Marches for Haldor, as it had flown them for Rurik and for every chief before, and Teo sat on the third ledge and watched them go. The third had the best view. It didn't, really. He'd sat there since he was six, and when he was fifteen and the yearlings had walked past him on the Choosing Ledge without stopping, he'd gone back to it and sat there laughing, because what else was there to do, and he'd found that he liked it better than any ledge a dragon might have carried him off.

Seven went out. They came off the Roosts one after another and dropped into the hollow and opened their wings above the falls, bronze and green and black, and went away south-west down the line of the hills toward the river, low, so that every farm in the Marches would feel them go over.

Three, his grandfather had had. Three was the custom. Uncle Ulric had said five. *The Clan's bigger than it was in Rurik's day.* And Arn had said seven, then, if we're counting, and looked at his uncle, and Ulric had looked back and said seven, and after that nobody could say three without it sounding like a side.

Three of the seven were Ulric's. Teo knew the riders. He sat on the ledge and watched them diminish over the moor, seven, all together, and knew that every miller and every drover and every old woman at a gate along the river would count them, and talk.

They came home after dark, in ones and twos, tired, along the river and up the beck. The last was Brann Holt, who had flown the tail of it, and he came down on the yard instead of the Roosts, which you didn't do, and got off his dragon and walked straight into the hall in his flying leathers with the cold still on him.

"Fires on the river," he said. "Upper Rush. Three barns, or four. Burning when I came over." He looked down the hall, at the right-hand table and the left. "Somebody's been at the farms."

---

They argued about it till midnight.

Ulric said Upper Rush was a day's ride and the fires would be out before anybody got there, and that it would be Saltreach sellswords off the river, and that the hold didn't send riders out in the dark for every barn on the Wend. Arn said the hold had been taking Upper Rush's wool since before Ulric was born and Upper Rush would want to see who came. Ulric said, *Then go*. Arn said he wasn't leaving the hold with his father on the pyre and his uncle at the table. They went round it, and round, the two of them, at their two tables, with the hall listening, until Teo stood up.

"I'll go," he said.

Both of them looked at him.

"I'm the one who doesn't matter," he said. "I can be spared. And if it's sellswords, I'd like to know whose." He didn't look at Ulric's table when he said it. He didn't need to.

He took a dozen of his father's old riders, men who had ridden with Haldor for thirty years and had nobody left to ride with, and Asger Brodd at the front of them with his grey beard in two plaits. They went down the Drover's Way by lantern, past the falls, over the edge and down through the night on the long slant of the moor, and they came down into the river valley with the sun coming up behind them and the smoke still lying in the hollow at Upper Rush. He hadn't slept. He'd had his father's death in his mouth four days, and now this, and he rode the last league with his jaw so tight his teeth hurt.

The hollow was full of people. They came down the slope from the lane into it and nobody heard them. Everyone was working. The barns were black ribs in grey ash, and men were dragging iron out of them, and a woman was cooking something over a fire made of bits of her own barn, and children went about with cups.

And in the far paddock, under the alders, a man was sitting on the grass.

He was sitting with his back to the hedge, which no horseman in the Scarfell would ever do near a frightened beast, and over him stood a dray-mare the colour of wet sand, enormous against the sky. She was shivering in long ripples from her withers to her quarters. She had her head down. She was breathing on his hair. He had his hand up flat on the side of her face, and he was talking.

Teo stopped the bay at the paddock gate.

He couldn't hear what the man was saying. It was low and steady and didn't go anywhere. It sounded like a man telling another man about the weather across a market table. The mare's ears went back and forth with it. Every time the man paused, she pushed her nose at him, and he started again.

There was a big man at the gate, with tears on his face and a halter in his hands, staring as if he'd been shown a calf with two heads.

Teo had seen the horse-master at Varrhold settle a beast like that. It took a morning, and a whip in one hand and a bucket in the other, and the settling-words going the whole time, the old ones, until the beast gave in because it was too tired not to. He'd never seen it done like this. He'd never seen a man go into a paddock with a frightened beast that size and sit down and let her come to him.

He watched the mare decide. That was what it looked like. She stood over the man and shook and thought about it, and then her shivering went quieter, and quieter, and stopped.

The man looked up.

Teo knew the face. It took him a moment. It was a face that didn't push itself at you: brown, weathered, older than it was, a farmer's face with the eyes kept low. Harrow's End, at lambing, two springs back. A coat held out to him over a gate, when he'd come out of the beck blue to the lips with a ewe in his arms. He'd given it back with a hole in it.

"Don't get up," Teo said. "You'll frighten her. Or me."

---

He asked what the man had said to her. The man said bees. Teo laughed, and couldn't stop for a moment, and it was the first time he had laughed since the bier came into the hall.

Then Hollin said, *Ask your uncle*, and nobody was laughing.

Teo turned round slowly. He heard the riders behind him go still: the creak of saddles stopping, a horse blowing. He'd heard that stillness in the hall the night the bier came in.

He said what a Varr says. That the Clan doesn't come at night or wear masks. That his father never hid his face from this valley in thirty years and neither would his brother and neither would he. He said it quietly, because his father had always said things like that quietly, and because his voice would have shaken if he'd raised it.

And then, because he was honest, and because the big stubborn man by the barn had lost his ox and deserved better than a speech, he said the other thing too. That his uncle's men weren't his father's men. That there were some among them he didn't know.

It came out of his mouth before he'd looked at it. He heard it go out into the yard and couldn't call it back. Behind him, he felt Asger Brodd look at the back of his head.

*There are some among them I don't know.* It was true. There'd been new faces at the east fire these last nights. Men from the coast, Ulric's people said, come up for the winter. Ulric was mending the walls.

He put it away. "But masks?" he said. "No. Not that. That isn't us."

---

His men walked the yard and the ashes. Two of them went down the slope toward the river and were gone a while, and came back, and told him in low voices what they'd found. A cart track, heavy, iron-tyred, going down to the bank. Not to the ford. To the bank.

He went and stood at the paddock gate and looked at the river.

Then Lisska came over and spoiled everything, as Lisska did. She came down the hill out of the sun, quick and dark-green and pleased with herself, and went round the hollow three times, so low the ash came up off the barns in a sheet, and the mare went up on her hind legs screaming, and the children by the wall burst into tears, and every man in the yard ducked and swore. Teo would have ducked too if he hadn't been born under the Roosts.

The farmer was on his feet with his hand on the mare's neck before Teo had seen him move. Talking again. Bees, still. The mare came down.

And in the middle of the yard a girl of about nine stood with a cup of water in each hand and her head tipped back, watching the dragon go round, with a face like the first day of spring lambs. Lisska saw her. Teo saw Lisska see her: the long head tilting on the turn, the eye going down over the yard, and stopping.

"Imra," he said, to nobody. "She's meant to be scouting the river."

The girl came and stood against the farmer's leg and told him the dragon had looked at her. Only her. Teo smiled at her. He couldn't help it. *Lisska likes children,* he told her. *She likes anything small that isn't afraid of her.*

"Yours?" he said to the farmer.

"Mine."

"She's got good sense. Most people run."

---

Before he left he stood by the gate with the farmer and told him about the cart track, and watched his face, and saw that the man had already seen it. Last night. In the dark. By the light of a burning barn, with nobody asking.

"And you didn't say."

"Nobody asked."

Teo laughed, shorter. He found he liked the man very much. It was an odd kind of liking. He liked most people; he couldn't help that, it came on him like a cold. But most people, when they met a Varr, did one of two things: they looked at him, hard, to see what he wanted, or they looked away from him at somebody behind him, to see who was really in charge. This man did neither. He looked at the river. He'd look at you when he had something to say, and not before, and you had the feeling he'd been looking at everything the whole time, and keeping it.

Across the river there were riders on the Hearthmere bank road. A long column, small with distance, with the sun on their helmets, going north. And on the water a long boat with six oars, pulling hard upriver against the current toward them, as if there were still something to be done.

"Now they come," Teo said.

"They never cross," said the farmer.

Teo watched the boat. He'd never seen that many knights on that road in his life. *Somebody over there is frightened of something.* He said so. He asked the farmer, if he saw anything else, to come to the hold and ask for him. Not his uncle. Not his brother. Him. He held out his hand.

The farmer looked at it. There was something going on behind his face, a long way back. Teo saw it and didn't know what it was. Then the hand came out and took his, and it was hard and dry and warm, and the man said, "If I see anything," and Teo held on a moment longer than he needed to, because he didn't want to ride back up the Way to the hold just yet.

"Come to the harvest fair," he said. "I'll be at yours. I'll be at all of them, this year."

He meant it. Ulric had said it the morning after the pyre was lit, at the east fire, chewing: *Every village in the Marches wants to see a Varr smiling at it before the moon's out, and you're the only one of us can do it.* It had been half an insult. Teo had taken it as a job.

He swung up and lifted his hand to the yard, and they went up the lane and over the top of the hill, and on the long ride home, with his eyes shutting in the saddle, he found that what he kept seeing wasn't the ashes, or the ruts, or the knights. It was a man sitting down in a paddock with his back to a hedge. Not walking at the mare. Letting her come.

The hold ate for a month. That was the custom too. Thirty days of open tables in the long hall, paid for by the Marches, and every night the bard got up by the hearth and sang Haldor's deeds.

By the end of the first week there were two fires in the yard.

Nobody built them. Ulric's men had always sat on the east side of the pyre in the evenings, because that was the side nearest the gate and Ulric's old steading, and Arn's had always sat on the west, nearer the hall. Then one night somebody lit a fire on the east side to keep warm, and the next night the west side lit one too, and after that there were two, and a space between them round the pyre, ten paces of black ground with nobody on it.

Arn's men sewed a red thread into their mourning bands. For the blood, they said, Haldor's blood, the line. Ulric's didn't. In a week you could tell, across the yard in the dusk, which band a man wore, and people began to look at a man's arm before his face.

Teo walked the space.

He hadn't decided to. It was only that every time he came into the yard he had to cross it, and it seemed foolish to walk all the way round. So he walked across it, through the middle, past the pyre, with a jug. He stopped at the east fire and asked Ulric's riders about their horses, and at the west fire and asked Arn's about their women, and he laughed at both, and filled cups at both, and went on. After a while he understood that people were watching him do it, both sides, and that something in them let go a little when he did, and he went on doing it.

It was like walking out on Coldtarn's ice in a hard winter, when the edges froze. You went easy and careful at once. You didn't stop in the middle.

The night the bard came to Gullhaven, the bee man was in the hall.

Teo had seen him at the door with the rain on his shoulders, staring up at the scale roof as if it might come down on him, and gone down the middle of the hall to him with two cups, and sat him on a bench by the doors among the drovers, and talked. He talked about his father. He found it easy with this man, because the man didn't need him to stop. He said his father had taught him to swim in Coldtarn, which no sane man does, and had been a terrible singer, and had cried at Teo's mother's pyre where everyone could see. He said the Clan burned its dead high up, so the smoke could be seen, and the size of the fire was the size of the life. A chief, eleven days. A chief's son, one night.

"One night," he said, and laughed. "I'd better make it a good one."

He told him about the two tables, and the chair, and walking up and down the middle like a dog between two fires. He said what he always said, that everyone was kind to him because he was the one who didn't matter.

"You matter," the farmer said.

Teo didn't know what to do with that, so he told him not to say it where Arn could hear.

Then the bard sang Haldor in the front at Gullhaven, and somebody at the east table shouted *That was Ulric*, and Arn stood up.

Teo was halfway up the hall before he'd thought about it. That was how it happened, when it happened. He didn't think. He found he was moving, and his mouth was already open, and something was coming out of it, and he listened to it come out with as much interest as anybody. *He was at the back with the ships and he was at the front with the shore.* It was even true, more or less. He'd heard it from both of them, drunk. He held up the jug and said, sing it with both of them, it's longer and it's better and we've got all month, and the hall laughed, and the bard took it up.

When he came back down to the bench by the door, his hands were shaking so that he spilled ale on the drover beside him. He put them under the table. The farmer was looking at him and saying nothing, and Teo was grateful for both.

---

He took him up the cliff path after, because you couldn't come to Varrhold and meet nobody.

Sigrun looked at the farmer as she looked at everything, as if it were a field she was thinking of buying. Dag said *farmer*, which from Dag was a speech. Lisska came down off the ledge above and put her head over Imra's shoulder a hand's width from the man's face, and the man froze like a rabbit, and then he talked to her. To her, not to Imra. He told her that the small one had talked about her all the way home and said her colour was like the beck in spring.

Lisska blinked. Then she lay down with her chin on her claws and kept her eyes on him.

Teo saw Imra look at the farmer differently after that. He saw Sigrun see it. He didn't know what any of them had seen, but he felt it go round the ledge like a draught.

At the back of the cave Aurveth breathed out, long and warm, and the brazier smoke went flat across them, and Sigrun didn't blink. Teo looked at her, the old grey head and the scarred eyebrow, and the huge bronze jaw behind her that her hand went to without looking.

"They'll go together, those two," he told the farmer, low, and laughed. "You watch. The day Sigrun dies, Aurveth'll lie down on top of her and that'll be that, and the priest'll have to build a pyre the size of the hall."

"I can hear you," said Sigrun.

"I know. I'm being fond."

They sat till dark. Below them, in the hollow, the two fires burned on either side of the great pyre-fire with the dark between, and you could see from up here how the people stood round them in two crowds, like two flocks on either side of a beck that won't cross.

"Two fires," Sigrun said, quietly, looking down. "Varrhold's had one fire for four hundred years."

Nobody answered her. Teo was thinking of the ford. He'd been thinking of it all evening, under the talk and the dragons, as a man keeps coming back to a sore tooth.

"It's thirty days," he said aloud at last. "Thirty days of bread and ale and singing. That's a long time. Anything can happen in thirty days. They'll come round. Uncle Ulric's not a fool, and Arn's not a fool when he's sober." He heard himself, and believed himself, and went on. "I'll get them in a room. I'll get them at the ford, where nobody's watching, and make them talk till they're sick of it."

"And if they don't come round?" said Imra.

He looked at the dark between the fires.

"Then I'll stand in the middle," he said, "and they'll have to go through me."

Dag Rorke stopped cleaning his axe. Behind them Kharrak made a noise like a quern. Aurveth opened one great gold eye and looked at Teo, and he looked back at her, and it was like being looked at by the Scarfell. He grinned at her. She didn't close it.

At the new moon they carried the ash up to Coldtarn.

Teo had been there more times than he could count. He'd learned to swim in it. He'd come out blue and gasping with his father roaring with laughter on the shore. He knew its black and its cold and the way the cliffs stood upside down in it. He'd never seen it with the whole Clan on its shore and the dragons on every ledge round the bowl, hundreds of them, so still that the first time one shifted and a stone fell into the water, half the people on the slope jumped.

He stood near the priest, apart from both, with his head bare and his hands clasped in front of him, because he didn't know what else to do with them.

He knew it would come at the pour. Everybody knew. He'd lain awake the night before thinking what he would say, and thought of eleven things, and they'd all gone out of his head on the climb.

The priest said the words. The plain ones, and then the old ones that only priests still had, that keep a dead chief down in the water. Teo heard his father's name go out over the tarn. He heard his grandfather's. *Haldor, son of Rurik.* He bowed his head when the gods were named and kept it down.

"Who pours?" said the priest.

Arn stepped forward and put his hands on the urn, and Ulric's voice came across the stone, slow and calm. *The champion pours.*

Teo didn't hear most of what came after. He heard it as a man hears a beck he's fallen asleep beside. *Soft in the head. Doubt. Look around you, boy.* He was watching Arn's hands. He saw them come off the urn. He saw the right one go down to the hip.

He heard the slope step back. Hundreds of people, all at once, a sound like shingle when the water pulls off it.

Somewhere up on the cliffs a dragon hissed.

And then he was walking. He didn't remember deciding to. He walked out along the black stone, slowly, because if he went fast he'd trip, and he stood by the urn between his brother and his uncle. He put one hand on it. It was cold. Plain grey stone, rough under his palm, cold as the tarn. He'd thought it would be warm. There'd been eleven days of fire in it.

He held out his other hand to his uncle, open.

"Pour with him," he said.

Ulric looked at the hand. He didn't take it.

Teo said it again. He said they were both right. He said the son's to pour and the champion's to pour and they were both here, and both his. He said, let the Clan see two hands on it. He looked at Arn and said *Brother. Please.* He said they could go back to the hold and hate each other in private like decent people, and he heard someone on the slope laugh, one short choked laugh, and stop.

And Ulric put out his huge hand and set it on the urn.

Teo felt it arrive. The stone moved a little under his own palm, as the weight came onto it. Then Arn's hand came onto the other side, and Teo took his own away and stepped back behind them and bowed his head, so that nobody would see his face.

They tipped it together. The ash lay on the black water in a great pale shape and drew in and went down. And the dragons on every ledge opened their mouths and made a sound that went into the rock and up through the soles of Teo's boots, lower than any roar, and he stood behind his brother and his uncle with his head down and his eyes shut and let it go through him.

---

He didn't go down with Arn. He couldn't, yet.

He went up the slope against the crowd going down, with people touching his arm as he passed and saying his name, and he smiled at them and didn't hear them. His legs had gone strange. He went up to the rim, where Sigrun was sitting on a flat rock under Aurveth with her cloak round her, and sat down on the stones beside her, and put his head between his knees.

"Not on my rock," said Sigrun.

"I'm not on your rock. I'm beside your rock."

She let him be. After a while he lifted his head and looked down at the tarn, black and still again, with the cliffs standing in it. The black stone was empty.

"Did you see them?" he said. "The slope. When he put his hand on it."

"I saw."

"They all breathed out. All of them. I heard it." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand. "That's what did it. Not me. Him and Arn, they'd have gone on glaring at each other over the pot till the moon came back. But there were all those people on that slope watching, and he couldn't be the man who wouldn't put his hand on his brother's ashes. Not with them watching." He looked at his palm. "It held because they saw it."

Sigrun said nothing. Aurveth's great head was lowered over the bowl behind her, the eyes on the water.

"I said I'd get them at the ford where nobody's watching," he said. "I was wrong. That's the wrong way round. If nobody sees it, it didn't happen. Uncle can say afterwards Arn wouldn't take his hand, and Arn can say Uncle came with a knife, and who's to say different? Me?" He laughed. "I'm the younger son. I'd say anything to stop them. Everyone knows that."

"So."

"So it has to be seen. People who aren't his and aren't Arn's. People who'll go home to their villages and say *I was there, they shook hands in the water, I saw it.*" He thought of a man sitting in a paddock with his back to a hedge, looking at the river, keeping everything. "Farmers. People who pay the levy. The bee man. He's here, did you see him? Halfway up, at the back, looking at his boots. He'd stand at a ford and say nothing, and the whole Marches would believe whatever he said he saw."

Sigrun turned her head and looked at him then.

"Go down," she said. "Your brother's waiting for you at the bottom of the path, and he's pretending he isn't."

He went down. At the bottom of the path, Arn was standing with his men, looking at the tarn, and when Teo came down off the last of the stones he fell in beside him without a word and they walked down to the hold together. Halfway down Arn said, without looking at him: "Brother. Please." In Teo's voice, nearly. And then, in his own: "You've never said please to me in your life."

"I've never needed anything from you in my life."

Arn walked on a while. "Don't make a habit of it," he said.

---

Ulric was harder. Ulric was a wall, and you couldn't talk to a wall about a wall.

So Teo didn't. He'd watched a man sit down in a paddock. He went to the east fire on the night after Coldtarn and sat down on the bench beside his uncle without being asked, among the big grey men and their axes, and talked about horses.

Ulric bred horses. It was the only soft thing about him. He had a line out of a grey stallion his father had brought back from the coast raids, big-boned horses with hard feet and bad tempers, and he could tell you every mare in it back six generations, and nobody ever asked him. Teo asked him. He asked about the grey stallion, and about the mare that had foaled twins in the snow the year Teo was born, and whether a bay with a white sock behind was luck or the other thing. Ulric looked at him as if he were simple. Then he told him. He told him for an hour, in his deep slow voice, with the fire going down, and his men round the fire listened with their mouths shut, because they'd never heard him say so much.

Teo didn't say a word about the ford. Or the chair. Or Arn. He said good night, and went back across the dark ground between the fires with his jug, and went to bed.

The next night he did it again.

There were more men at the east fire every night. He noticed that. Hired hands, Ulric's spokesman Raske called them, red-bearded Sten Raske who'd never in his life called anything by a soft name: men taken on for the winter, for the walls, for the sheep. They sat at the back of the fire in grey armbands with their faces in the dark, and when Teo looked round at them they nodded to him, a hired man's nod to a chief's son, and he nodded back. Coast men, most of them. He could hear it in how they said *aye*. Gorm Askell, the steward, came to him on the third day with his inky fingers and his sour long face and said that was twenty since the funeral, twenty, and how was the hold to feed them through the winter, and Teo said ask my uncle, and Gorm said he had, and Teo said then you've your answer.

He didn't think much of them. He'd think of them afterwards, a little, at night: the faces at the back of the fire he didn't know. He'd turn them over and put them away. A man mending walls takes on men. A man who wants a chair takes on men too. Both could be true. Either way it was a reason to get Ulric into the water and out again with a peace, before the walls were mended.

On the third night at the east fire, Ulric stopped in the middle of a mare and said, without turning his head:

"What do you want, boy?"

"Nothing."

"You've sat at my fire three nights. Your brother's men think you've gone over. Mine think you've come to count us." Ulric drank. "What do you want?"

Teo looked into the fire.

"Grandfather and his brothers used to settle things at the ford," he said. "On the Way. Hollow Beck, below the falls. When they were young, before anybody was chief of anything. Whenever they fell out they'd ride down there and stand in the water, and nobody else was let come, and they'd stand there till they'd either shaken hands or knocked each other into the beck, and either way they came home together." He turned the cup in his hands. "And when you and Father fell out, he took the pair of you down there and did the same. Father told me. He said you knocked him in once. You were twelve."

"Eleven."

"Eleven."

Ulric said nothing for so long that Teo thought he'd lost him. The fire was down to embers. His men had gone off to their beds one by one while he talked about mares, and there was nobody left on the bench but the two of them and Raske asleep at the end of it with his red beard on his chest.

"With who," said Ulric.

"You. Me. Arn. In the water. Six men each, on the banks, no closer. And people to see it, from the villages. Farmers. Witnesses." Teo looked at him at last. "So nobody can say afterwards it went different."

"Arn won't come."

"Arn will come."

"And if I come, and he doesn't, I've stood in a beck like a fool with half the Marches watching." Ulric's big face didn't change. "I'll come up the Way, then. From the south. From the Ram's Head. I'll not ride down it behind your brother like his dog."

"From the south," said Teo. "Yes."

"When?"

He hadn't thought of when. He thought of it now, sitting by the fire with his uncle's eyes on him, and it came to him at once, all of it, clear and whole, as things did when he didn't plan them. After the fairs, because he'd promised the fairs. Four days after Harrow's End, because he'd be riding home from Harrow's End and could carry it with him, the bee man and the miller and whoever else. And noon. Not dawn, not dusk, when a man can say he didn't see. Noon, with the sun on the water and the whole moor lit.

"Four days after the Harrow's End fair," he said. "At noon. When the sun's on the water and everyone can see."

Ulric looked at the fire.

"Your grandmother," he said at last. "Hild. My mother. She'd come down to the ford with a basket after, when we'd done. Every time. Bread and cold mutton. Didn't matter who'd knocked who in." He drank, and put the cup down. "Noon, then."

Teo walked back across the dark ground between the fires afterwards with his jug, and halfway across it he stopped by the black heap of the pyre and stood there, alone in the middle, and laughed out loud, once, and the men at both fires looked up.

---

Lisska had stopped coming to the stable roof.

He noticed it some days after Coldtarn, crossing the yard. She'd sat on the lower stable roof since she was a yearling, whenever he was in the yard, to be annoying. She liked the warm slates and the smell of the horses coming up through them. He'd throw apples at her and she'd catch them and drop them back on his head.

"Get off my stable, you green menace," he said to the empty roof, out of habit, and felt foolish.

"She's sulking," said Imra, when he asked. "She sits on the cliff and won't come down into the yard. I asked her why and she asked me why I keep asking." Imra shrugged. "She's in a mood. She wants the small one with the two cups and she can't have her."

"She can have her at the fair," said Teo. "I'll bring her a message. Tell her I'll bring her a message."

"She says you don't understand anything," said Imra, and laughed. "She says that about everyone."

---

The bee man came back with the first of the new barley, eleven days after the tarn, with the sand-coloured mare in the traces and her ears flat at the Roosts. Teo saw him from the yard and felt glad all through, like a drink of something warm.

"Wren says hello," the farmer said, looking as if he'd like to be under the cobbles.

Teo took him up the cliff.

Lisska came out of the cave when she saw him like a pour of green water and put her nose against his chest so hard that he rocked back, and Teo had to catch his arm. Imra went quiet after, and said in an odd voice that Lisska liked him. *She's never said she likes anyone who wasn't Clan. Not once. In nine years.* And at the back of the cave Aurveth opened her eye and kept it open.

Teo sat down by the brazier and pulled the farmer down beside him and told him.

He kept his voice down. He was careful up here, with the wind going out over the hollow and the two fires below. He told him he had Ulric. He told him about the ford, his grandfather's ford, old ground. Six each side, on the banks. Just the three of them in the water. And witnesses.

"People who aren't Ulric's and aren't Arn's," he said. "Ordinary people. If they see us shake hands in the water, then everyone in the Marches will know it happened, and nobody can say afterwards it didn't. You understand? It can't just be us. It has to be seen."

He watched the man understand. He watched him look at Sigrun, and Sigrun look back at him and say nothing, and he thought, *oh. She told him.*

"Will you come?" he said. "As a witness?"

"When?"

"After the harvest fairs. After yours. Then four days after that. At noon, when the sun's on the water and everyone can see." He said what he'd come to say: that he knew the man didn't like to be in anything; that he wasn't asking him to say anything; that he was asking him to stand there and see it.

The farmer looked down at the hollow a long while. Teo waited. He'd learned that much in a month. You sat down and you waited.

"I'll come," said the farmer.

---

They went down in the late afternoon. Teo went ahead, talking over his shoulder, because he couldn't stop. About the fair, about Hob, about what he'd say to Arn, who was the last of it and the hardest. He was lit up. He could feel it in his own face, like a fever, and he didn't care. At the bottom, where the path came out on the yard by the lower stable, he turned round on the last step.

"Four days after your fair, Fenn," he said. "Noon. The ford on Hollow Beck. Don't you dare forget."

He said it loud. He wanted to. He wanted the yard to hear it, the stable, the stones, the hollow. He wanted to hear it said out loud himself.

There was a man sitting on an upturned bucket by the stable door, three paces off, mending a halter. One of Ulric's hired hands. He looked up as they passed and nodded, pleasant. Teo nodded back. He didn't look at him. He was looking at the farmer's face, to see if he was pleased.

The farmer's face had gone very still. It often did. Teo thought nothing of it.

---

At the gate he held the mare's head himself, because the farmer had his hands full of rope. She leaned her long sad face against his chest and blew. There were boatmen by the gatepost, Saltreach men in their clothes of all colours, complaining about a Greyhold girl and a duskcat, and some of the hired men with them, laughing. Teo didn't listen. He'd had a month of listening to men complain.

"Four days after your fair," he said. "At noon. Bring Hob. Bring the old man with the knees. Bring the girl, if you like, if she wants to see a dragon again." He grinned. "And Fenn. Thank you. You've no idea what it means. Nobody else I've asked has said yes without looking at Uncle first."

The farmer opened his mouth. He had something in it. Teo could see it there, behind his teeth, like a dog under a cart deciding whether to come out. He waited.

"Teo," the farmer said.

"Mm?"

"Be careful. At the ford."

Teo laughed. He couldn't help it. "Careful? Fenn, I'm going to stand in a beck with my uncle and my brother and make them shake hands. Careful's the last thing I'll be." He let go of the mare and stepped back and lifted his hand. "Go home. Tell the small one Lisska says hello."

He watched the wain go down the Way toward the falls with the mare walking quick and glad in the traces. *Be careful.* He thought it was a kind thing to say, and that the man must think him very young, and that he probably was.

---

That left Arn.

Arn wasn't a wall. Arn was a fire in a high wind. You couldn't sit by him and wait; you'd be burned, or he'd go out. He sat every night now at the left-hand table, not eating, drinking steadily with his eyes on the chair under its grey cloak, and his young men round him watching him like a pot.

Teo tried him sober, on the second day after the bee man went. Arn said he'd stand in no beck with a man who wouldn't stand for his brother's body.

Teo tried him at the chair, that night, late, when the hall was empty but for the two of them and the dogs. He got him drunk. It wasn't hard. Arn had been drunk for a month. He sat on the dais step beside him with a jug between them and their father's cloak on the chair above them, and talked about the ford. About their grandfather knocking his brothers in. About Ulric being eleven. Arn laughed once, the first time Teo had heard him laugh since the bier, and then he cried, angrily, with his face in his arm, and said he'd go, he'd go, he'd put the old bastard in the beck himself, and then he was asleep with his head on the step.

In the morning Arn didn't remember. Or said he didn't.

So Teo took him to the falls.

He woke him before light and got him on a horse, which took two grooms, and rode him three leagues down the Way to where Hollow Beck came off the Scarfell in its long white drop, with the spray going up off the pool at the bottom so cold it made your teeth hurt to breathe it. Arn was sick twice on the way. At the pool Teo got down and pulled him off his horse and walked him into the water, boots and all, and when Arn swore and fought, Teo put his hand flat on his brother's chest and pushed, and Arn went under.

He came up roaring. He came up out of that pool with his hair in his eyes and his mouth wide open and the cold knocking every word out of him, and went for Teo, and Teo let him, and they went down together, the two of them, in the white water under the falls, punching each other like boys.

They lay on the stones afterwards, shaking, too cold to talk.

"You're twenty," said Arn at last, through his teeth. "You're twenty years old and you've just drowned the next chief of the Varr in a pool."

"Half drowned. You'll know the difference when you're sober."

Arn lay and looked up at the falls.

"He didn't stand," he said. "When they brought Father in. He sat there with his cup."

"I know."

"Everyone saw."

"Everyone saw you walk up the dais and tell him to wait thirty years, too." Teo turned his head on the stones. "That's what it's for. The ford. So they see something else."

Arn said nothing. He was crying again, or it was the spray. He'd always lost to Teo, when they were boys: at running, at swimming, at who could hold his breath longest under Coldtarn, at who could throw a stone furthest over the Roosts. He'd never once minded. He minded everything else in the world, and never that.

They rode home soaked and frozen and got into the hall at noon, and Teo got him drunk again, carefully this time, two cups and no more, at the left-hand table, with his young men watching. And when Arn was warm and loose and red in the face, Teo said, quite loudly, so the table could hear: "Four days after Harrow's End. Noon. You and me and Uncle, in the water."

Arn looked down the hall at the right-hand table. Ulric was there, eating as if the food had insulted him.

"Gods help me," said Arn. "Yes."

---

The next night Teo went across to the east fire and made Ulric say it again, in front of his men. Noon, four days after Harrow's End, six on each bank. Ulric said it, flat and slow, looking at the fire, and Raske beside him said it after him, as if it were a thing to be written down, and Teo filled every cup at that fire himself and went back across the dark ground between the fires with his jug empty.

He didn't sleep. He sat on the third ledge until the sun came up, with his legs over the edge and his coat round him, and watched the hold wake below him. The two fires had gone to ash. Somebody was raking the pyre-scar. Gorm Askell crossed the yard with a ledger under his arm, and a dog followed him, and he kicked at it without looking. Lisska was on the cliff to his left, high up, curled on a ledge by herself. She lifted her head when he looked up at her. She didn't come down.

At noon he took four riders and went down the Way to the fair at Harrow's End.

---

He was late. He was always going to be late, with fourteen leagues and the Ram's Head in between, and he came up the lane in the dusk with the bonfire already going in the top field, roaring at the sky, and the fiddle going, and the whole village round it. He'd only just swung down at the gate when a girl in a blue dress came at him out of the dark at a dead run.

He caught her under the arms and swung her up. She weighed nothing. Nine years old, all elbows and hair.

"The small one!" he said. "The one with the two cups! Lisska sends her greetings and says you're to stop growing, because she likes you the size you are."

Lisska had said nothing of the kind, so far as he knew. Lisska had said he didn't understand anything. But Imra had said Lisska remembered her, and he thought that came to the same thing, and he thought Lisska wouldn't mind him improving it a little.

The girl stood in the lane and looked up at him and couldn't speak. Over her head, at the back of the crowd by the hedge, he saw her father with a cup in his hand, standing as if he'd been planted there, and he couldn't speak either.

---

He danced. Gods, he danced. He hadn't danced since before his father took sick, and it went into his legs like ale. He danced with the farmer's sister, who laughed at him, and with an old woman of eighty who danced like a yearling, and with the farmer's mother, who said she didn't dance and then did, a slow turn round the fire with her shawl over her arm. He put a small boy on his horse. He danced with a girl of four on his shoulders with her fists in his hair, shrieking.

And when the one-eyed fiddler played the slow reel for the children, he bowed to the farmer's daughter at the edge of the firelight, properly, as he'd have bowed to a chief's wife at the hold, and held out his hand. She took it. She put her other hand flat on his arm, and she didn't watch her feet once. She watched his face, all the way round the fire, with a long level look that went right into him, as if she were storing him away somewhere to keep.

He thought: I'll remember this. When I'm old. When there's one fire again in the yard, and Arn's in the chair, and Uncle's at the table beside him grumbling about his mares. I'll remember the bee man's girl looking at me as if I were worth keeping.

---

Later the old woman sang.

She sang from her stool with her hands in her lap, and the whole field went quiet for it, even the drunk ones. Teo went and stood at the hedge beside the farmer to listen. *Before the six there was one.* He knew it. Everybody knew it. But she sang the hush in it, the old way, *hush now, the last one's sleeping, hush now, you'll wake it if you weep*, and he hadn't heard it sung with the hush since he was very small and his mother's mother sang it over him in the dark at the back of the hall, in a voice like this one, thin and a little cracked, and he'd lain awake afterwards, frightened of something under the floor.

She sang the last verse fast. He noticed. He thought she meant to.

He told the farmer about the ports, where they banged their cups on the tables and sang the last line over and over, *till the six are one again*, like men promising each other something. He'd heard it in Wendmouth in the spring. It had made the back of his neck go cold, and he hadn't known why then either. The farmer said his mother always sang the last verse fast.

"Maybe they both knew something," Teo said. "Old women always do."

---

Walking down to the horses, Hob caught them up at the gate with a jug and a lantern and a story: a Greyhold boat at sunset, a little skiff, putting one person onto the spit of the Old Ruin, where nobody goes.

Teo turned and looked out over the river. He couldn't see the island. He couldn't see anything but black water and the far bank and the few lights of Greyhold, small and steady, down the river. *Somebody over there is frightened of something.* He thought of the long boat pulling hard upriver with its six oars, the morning after Upper Rush, too late.

"What happens?" he said.

Hob opened his mouth and found he didn't know, and said *bad things*, and went back to the fire.

At the horses Teo stopped with the reins in his hand. The farmer had had something on his mind all evening. Teo had watched it in him through the whole fair, a hand that kept going to a coat pocket and coming out again. He asked. He thought for a moment the man might tell him.

"At the ford," the farmer said. "After. When it's done. There's something I want to give you. I'll give it to you then."

Teo looked at him in the lantern light. *A gift*, he thought. He had a sudden foolish idea that it was a coat. A coat without a hole in it. The man looked like a man who'd give you a coat.

"After," he said. "All right. I'll hold you to it."

He told him to tell the small one that she danced better than any girl at the hold, and that it wasn't flattery, and Lisska said so too. Then he turned the bay and lifted his hand, and they went down the lane at a walk, five horses, five lanterns, and at the bend he looked back once and saw the farmer still at the gate, and above him in the top field a small figure in a blue dress, standing, watching the lane.

---

The last three days of the thirty went fast.

He rode back up the Way through the night and the next morning, and slept in the afternoon like a dead man, and woke in the dusk to Haki shaking his shoulder. Haki was eleven and did the bay, and loved the bay, and loved Teo, in that order. He said Teo's uncle had sent to say he'd ride down to the Ram's Head on the third night, with his six, to lie there and come up the Way at noon from the south, as agreed, and was that still the arrangement, and Teo said it was, and Haki said he'd thought so, and that Skerry had a stone bruise on his near fore and he'd poulticed it and it was nothing, and was Teo taking Skerry.

"I'm taking Skerry."

"Can I come?"

"No."

"Can I come to the bottom of the Way?"

"No. Go to bed."

The hall was quieter those last nights. It was as if both tables were holding their breath. The bard sang Haldor's last fight, and his death, which was a poor song because it had taken a year and had no fighting in it, and nobody shouted out. On the second night Arn came down off the dais and walked the whole length of the hall, through the dark space round the hearth, and stopped at the right-hand table, and stood there. Ulric looked up at him.

"Noon," said Arn.

"Noon," said Ulric.

Arn went back to his table. The whole hall breathed out, and Teo, at the bench by the doors, put his face in his hands and laughed till the drover beside him thumped his back.

He told Asger Brodd that his own six were to come down in the afternoon, after the three had met.

Asger didn't like it. He stood in the yard with his grey plaits and his arms folded and said that a Varr didn't ride to a meeting with nobody at his back. Teo said a Varr wouldn't, but this one was going to. Arn would have six, and Ulric would have six, and two sides was what the Marches needed to see. If there were three, every miller on the river would be counting which side the third stood on.

"And who stands behind you?" said Asger.

"Nobody needs to. I'm in the middle." Teo grinned at him. "That's what the middle's for."

Asger looked at him a long time. He'd taught Teo to sit a horse. He'd taught him to hold a knife, and to throw an axe, badly, and to drink, rather better. "Afternoon," he said at last, as if it tasted of something.

"Bring the good ale."

"I'll bring your body home if you fall in that beck," said Asger, "and that's all I'll bring."

---

On the third night the tables were full for the last time, and the last of the thirty days went out in a great deal of ale, and the bard sang them all, Haldor's deeds from the first raid to the last, with both brothers in the front at Gullhaven. Teo sat by the doors and sang with the drovers. He didn't drink much. He wanted his head in the morning.

Before the end of it, Ulric got up from the right-hand table and walked down the hall with his six behind him, big men with their axes, and stopped at the bench by the doors.

Teo stood.

His uncle looked down at him. He had his riding cloak on, and he smelled of horse and wet wool and woodsmoke, and his face was the face it always was, the Scarfell on a bad day.

"Noon," said Ulric.

"Noon."

Ulric put out his hand, and for a moment Teo thought he was going to take him by the shoulder, which Ulric had not done since Teo was nine. Instead he put it on the back of Teo's head, briefly, heavily, as a man puts a hand on a dog he's fond of and doesn't want to be seen to be.

Then he went out into the dark, and Teo heard the horses go out of the gate, seven of them, and down the Way toward the Ram's Head.

---

He didn't sleep. He hadn't thought he would.

He lay a while on his bed in the chamber off the hall with his boots on, and listened to the hold go quiet. The last of the drinkers. The dogs. Somebody singing, badly, in the lower stable, and stopping. When he couldn't stand it any longer he got up and went out and climbed the cliff path in the dark with one hand on the rail, and sat down on the third ledge.

The moon was up. It had come up late, from behind the Scarfell, a few nights past the full and going lopsided, and the whole hollow lay under it white and black: the long-houses in their ring, the scale roof of the hall shining like a wet beast, the scar in the yard where the pyre had been, raked out, the two dead fires. Hollow Beck went down the hollow with the moon on it in pieces. Somewhere below, over the falls and past them and down over the Scarfell's edge, it ran across the Way at the ford, over the brown stones, under the alders.

He thought of the ford with the sun on it. He thought of what he'd say.

He'd say nothing much. That was the thing he'd learned. He'd stand in the water and let them come to him. Not walk at them. He'd be there first, standing in the beck, so that when they came over their two rises, Uncle from the south and Arn from the north, the first thing either of them saw would be him, in the middle, with his hands empty. And they'd ride down to the water, because they couldn't not, not with the witnesses on the slope, the miller and the bee man and whoever the bee man brought, watching. And they'd get down. And they'd come into the water. And he'd hold out his hands, one to each, as he had at Coldtarn, and wait.

And then after, on the bank, the farmer would give him whatever it was. And Grandmother Hild had brought a basket of bread and cold mutton, and Teo had nobody to bring a basket, so he'd see to it himself: Asger would bring the ale, and he'd bring the mutton, and they'd sit on the bank, all of them, six and six and the witnesses, and eat it.

He laughed, alone on the ledge, at himself.

Something moved on the cliff above him. A long shape, dark against the moonlit rock, two ledges up and a little to the left. Lisska. He hadn't known she was there. She was lying with her chin on her claws, and her eye caught the moon and shone, pale green, looking down at him.

"Hello, menace," he said.

She didn't come down. She lay and looked at him. After a while she made a small sound, high and short, with a lift at the end of it, and then put her head down on her claws and didn't make it again.

"I don't understand anything," he told her. "Imra says you say so. You're right. I'll bring you an apple."

---

He went down to the lower stable a little after the third hour by the stars.

It was dark down there and smelled of horses and old hay. Somebody had left the door open. There was a bucket by it, upside down, and he barked his shin on it in the dark and swore at it and left it where it was. Most of the stalls were full. Ulric's were empty, the seven that had gone down to the Ram's Head.

Skerry was awake. Skerry was always awake when Teo came, and put his head over the door and lipped at his coat for apples. The near fore had Haki's poultice on it still, bound neatly in sacking, and was cool when Teo ran his hand down it. Haki was asleep in the straw of the next stall, curled up, with his mouth open. Teo stepped over him.

He saddled the bay himself, by feel, in the dark, the good saddle with the grey cloth under it and the black hound stitched on the cloth, because it was a day for the hound. He hung his sword on the saddle, where it would be no use to anyone, which was the point. He put his hand on his own knife at his belt, the good one with the hound on the pommel that his father had given him at fifteen, the year the yearlings walked past him, and left it in its sheath.

He led the bay out into the yard. Haki didn't wake.

The hold was asleep. The moon had gone over toward the west and the shadows of the long-houses lay long across the yard toward the gate. He walked Skerry across the middle of it, past the pyre-scar, between the two dead fires, where nobody walked. At the gate the man on watch got up off his stool with his lantern and looked at him, and at the bay, and at the sky.

"Early," he said.

"Always," said Teo, and the man laughed and opened the small door in the big one for him, and Teo led the bay through it out of the ring and onto the Way.

---

The Way went down from the hold along Hollow Beck, and he rode it at a walk in the moonlight, with the beck loud on his left.

He'd ridden it a thousand times. He'd ridden it drunk, and asleep, and once backwards on a dare. He knew every stone. He knew the place where it bent away from the water and came back, and the place where the old drovers had cut steps in the rock for their beasts, and the cairn where somebody's grandfather had been killed by a falling sheep, which was the truth, though nobody believed it. Three leagues down, the falls came up out of the dark, white, roaring, with the spray hanging over the pool in the moonlight like smoke where he'd half drowned the next chief of the Varr, and he grinned and rode on.

Below the falls the Way went on down beside the beck through the heather, league after league, and came out at last at the Scarfell's edge, and the whole of the Marches opened out below him at once, grey and vast and quiet, with the moon gone low over it and the river somewhere far off at the bottom of it all, a line of grey.

It was very cold. The air had that thinness in it that comes at the end of summer up here, when the first frost is waiting in the hollows for the sun to go. His breath went up white. Skerry's went up white. He rode with his collar up and his hands under his arms by turns, and let the bay pick his way.

Somewhere over the moor a curlew went up, calling.

He thought about his father. Not the bier, or the hands on the sword, or the big going off him like snow. His father on the shore of Coldtarn when Teo was six, in his shirt, roaring, *Kick, boy, kick, the bottom's a long way down,* and Teo kicking, blue, furious, and coming up out of the black water into his father's two hands. He thought his father would have laughed at all of this. The ford, the farmers, the bee man, the noon. He'd have laughed and said, *You'll never do it*, and then come down to the bank himself to watch.

The sky was getting grey behind the Scarfell.

---

It came slowly, the light. It came up the eastern sky the colour of ash, and then the colour of the inside of a shell, and the moor came up out of the dark round him, brown, with frost lying white in every hollow and along the north side of every stone. The beck came back into sight beside him, grey, then silver. Geese went over very high, going south, in a long crooked line, talking among themselves, and he tipped his head back on the saddle to watch them go, as he'd watched the riders go out from the third ledge all his life.

He came over the last rise above the ford.

And there it was below him: the long slant of the Way going down to the water, and the beck going wide and shallow across it over the brown stones, and the alders leaning over on both banks, and the shieling on the slope above with its turf roof grey with frost, and the ford-house dark, and the gorse. Nobody there. Nobody on either bank. The slope where the witnesses would stand, empty. The far rise where Uncle would come over, empty. The water going over the brown stones, and the frost white on the stones of the bank, and the first of the sun just coming up over the moor on his left and laying his shadow and the bay's across the slope, long and thin, nearly to the edge of the water.

First. He was first. He'd always been first.

He sat a moment at the top and looked at it. He thought of his grandfather, and Ulric aged eleven, and the beck going over all of them.

Then he gathered the reins and lifted his hand to the empty ford, as he lifted it to every yard he rode into, and rode down the long slant of the Way to the water, where the sun had just reached it.
