On the pass they say a stormkite weighs what a promise weighs, and that a girl who rides one has to keep herself as light. They say it to girls who are being weighed. It isn't true about the kites. A grown stormkite weighs less than the boy who mucks out the toll-house mules, which is very little for a thing that spreads forty feet of wing, and less than any man who ever tried to sit on one. But the drovers on the tusker road have been saying it for two hundred years, and the pass-watch says it after them, and by now it is the kind of thing that is true because everyone says it.

The kites were on the High Crown before the colleges were on their hill. They came down off the snowfields in pairs in front of weather, grey and fork-tailed, not beating their wings, falling and catching and falling, so that people in Vael looked up from the fish-market and said *there's a front coming* a day before any cloud showed. They roosted on the crags above the pass, where the cold air poured off the snow at dawn and they could step off a ledge into it and go down the mountain on it like sledges on a packed road. They had roosted there when the pass-stone was set. They had watched the tuskers come down to it every winter since.

Above the pass-stone there is a crag with ledges in it, cut deeper by hand where the kites had begun them, and a hut hung inside with felt, and a beam with a hook at one end and a pan at the other. The pass-watch calls it the Kite Stair. The colleges call it the Pass Wardens' roost, and write it down that way in the register at the lake gate, with the names of the riders and the beasts and the gifts.

Runa's line in the register said: *Runa, of the toll-house. Kite. Weather, a day off.* And a thumbprint in ink, because the clerk had come up the Stair to take it the week Tatter chose her, and her hands had been too cold to hold the pen.

---

She was weighed at the new moon, like everybody.

Tilde did it. Tilde did everything at the roost that needed doing in a voice. She was old and small and brown as a walnut, with hands that had been frostbitten on three fingers and a way of looking at a girl as if she were a cloud that might do something.

"Up," said Tilde.

Runa got into the pan. The beam swung. Tilde put the stones on the hook, one, two, the half, and the little one with the hole in it that had been somebody's spindle-whorl a hundred years ago. The beam came level, and hung, and went on hanging.

"Six and a quarter," said Tilde.

"I've not eaten since yesterday."

"I can see that. You'll fall off." Tilde took the stones down. "Six and a half is the line. You know it. I know it. The kite knows it, which is the point." She looked at Runa's wrists, as she always did, as if wrists were where a girl kept her future weight. "You'll grow. Everyone grows. Grete grew."

Grete was seventy and deaf and sat in the hut door all day carding felt. She heard her name and looked up and smiled with no teeth.

"I'll not grow," said Runa.

"No," said Tilde. "Of course not." She hung the stones back on their nails. "Go and eat something. Not much."

---

Tatter was on the third ledge, where the morning sun came first.

He was young. He'd been a yearling the summer she was eleven and had sat on that ledge all through her twelfth year looking at her whenever she came up with the medicine-satchels for Tilde. She hadn't known he was looking. Nobody had. Kites looked at everything, all the time, with their heads on one side. Then one morning in the spring, with snow still on the road, he had stepped off his ledge and come down the Stair on foot, which no kite did, walking badly on his short legs with his great wings half open and dragging, and put his head against her chest.

She'd felt him come in. A coldness, high up, and a great deal of room.

*Light,* he'd said. That was the first word. As if she were weather and he'd been reading her for a year and had finally decided.

He had a split in the left fork of his tail where something had taken a mouthful out of it before she knew him, so that the fork flew ragged, and Grete had called him Tatter, and it had stuck before Runa could think of anything better. She didn't mind. It suited him. He was untidy in the air, and quick, and he turned harder than Slate or Mallow and came out of it laughing, if a kite could be said to laugh. She thought it could. She felt it.

She sat on the ledge beside him now and put her hand in the soft grey down at the base of his neck, under the long feathers, where it was warm.

*Heavy,* he said. He meant the air. He always meant the air.

"I know."

*Heavy down there. Over the flat cold.* He turned his head and looked at the lake. *Full. Too full.*

---

The flat cold was what he called Glasswater, and he'd called it that since the summer the island came.

She had seen it come. Everybody on the pass had. It had come along the High Crown from the east in high summer with the sky emptying ahead of it, every bird in the north going flat in the heather, and the kites on the Stair pressed into their ledges with their wings over their heads like old women in a hailstorm. It had gone along the snowfields so high and so huge that its shadow lay on the mountain like a second mountain, and come down on the lake a mile out from the city's quays, and folded its wings, and put its head on its paws, and lain there.

An island. Grey. Breathing.

The Blue College had sent an order up with the evening owl. *No kite over the water.* Tilde had read it out in the hut, and Grete had asked what, and Tilde had shouted it at her, and Grete had said, "Well, they'd not go anyway," which was true.

They wouldn't. Runa had tried, once, the second week, on a bright morning with good air, to take Tatter out along the shore. He'd gone. He'd gone as far as the first of the city's roofs and then turned so hard that she nearly came off, and gone back up the mountain in a long steady climb without being asked, and when she'd got off on the ledge her hands were shaking and so was he.

*There's a hole in the air,* he'd said. *Over it. Everything goes down into it.*

She'd thought about that a great deal. She couldn't see a hole. She could see the lake, very blue, and the ferries going round the island in their long wide curves, a quarter-mile off, as you'd take a sledge round a crevasse. And the city's lamps at night, going brown at the bottom of the hill by the water, a street a week, and white at the top, too white, until the line between was so sharp you could have walked it.

Tatter said the white was full and the brown was empty and both were wrong. That was as far as he could tell her. A kite thinks in air. What isn't air, he had no words for.

---

On the day the lion came she'd been in the air over the High Crown's flank with physic for the shepherds at the Saddle, and she'd seen them first.

The tusker herd, on its summer road along the snow-line, thirty beasts in a long red-brown string with their calves inside it. And at the front, beside the old cow, something grey and not a tusker, walking. She'd taken Tatter down for a look. He hadn't wanted to. He'd gone down in a wide nervous spiral, high, and at the bottom of it she'd looked down and seen a lion the size of a hay-wain and a woman walking at its shoulder with her hand on its mane, and the woman had looked up.

Only that. Looked up, with a hand over her eyes. A knight's sword. A Hearthmere coat.

Runa had told the pass-watch, and the pass-watch had sent the owl down, and two days later the lion-woman had come down the pass road on foot and stood at the lake gate in front of the register, and old Brannagh, who'd been on the gate since Runa's mother was a girl, had looked at the lion a long time and said *pass*.

Runa's mother had told her that, at the toll-house, ladling soup. "He didn't write it down," she'd said, in a voice as if somebody had walked through the toll-house in muddy boots. "Brannagh. Didn't write a word."

---

The day the hill went out, she was on the Stair with the long glass.

It was the roost's glass, brass, older than Tilde, with a crack in the eyepiece that made everything a little doubled at the edge. Tilde let her have it because her eyes were young. Tilde stood at her shoulder and made her say what she saw.

"They're on the terrace," said Runa. "In front of the Hall of Rings. In a ring."

"How many?"

"I don't know. A lot. A hundred. Grey gowns." She moved the glass. "There's one in the middle by himself."

"Doing what?"

"Standing." She looked. He was very small at that distance, and doubled by the crack. A thin dark shape beside something on a stand. "Looking at something."

"The lamps," said Tilde.

The lamps were white. Every one on the hill, at noon, in the sun, so that the windows of every tower burned paler than the sky behind them, and you could see them from the Stair as you could see snow on a far peak. The lower city underneath them was dark. It was dark, from the water to the line, as it had been for a week, and the people in it gone.

They'd gone up all morning. She'd watched them. The fish-market and the dyers' yards and Ferry Street and the steps, the whole low shore of the city, emptying uphill in long lines with bundles, past the line of the lamps, and settling on the streets above it like snow on a ledge. She'd seen a chair on two poles going up Rope Street with an old woman sitting in it bolt upright. She'd seen the ferries come up out of the water, one after another, up the stone slips on their rollers, and turned over, keel-up, along the top of the slips, black, in a row.

"Somebody told them," said Tilde.

"Who?"

"Somebody with sense," said Tilde, and took the glass.

---

Tatter knew before any of them.

She felt him go flat on his ledge. Through the bond, all at once, the high cold room he lived in went small and dark, and she turned round and he had his wings over his head and his face in the rock.

On every ledge of the Stair the kites were doing the same. Slate. Mallow. The unbonded ones on the high ledges, a dozen of them, grey bundles pressed into the crag.

*Pull,* said Tatter. *Down. It's pulling down.*

She turned back to the city.

Every lamp on the hill went out.

All at once. From the crown to the line. Like a lid going on. And a sound came up off the lake a breath later, across the water and up the mountain and into the Stair, a crack so huge that Runa felt it in her back teeth and Grete in the hut door dropped her carding combs and said, "What? What?"

The ice was breaking.

It had lain on the lake for a week. In the last week of summer. A skin, then a pane, out from the island in a ring, and every morning the ferries breaking channels in it with poles. Now it broke from the island outward, all of it, in long lines, and lifted, and turned over in plates. And the island stood up out of it.

She had not known it had legs. She'd thought of it as an island so long. It stood up on the lake with the ice sliding off it in sheets, and opened its wings, wider than the water, and the broken chains on its forelegs swung, and she could see them from the Stair without the glass.

Three wingbeats.

She counted them because Tilde was counting them, under her breath, beside her. One, and the air came up the mountain in a wave and every kite on the Stair screamed. Two, and it was over the lower city, so low its shadow went over all of it at once. Three.

It came down on the hill. On the east side, by the tall blue tower.

The tower went under its forefoot.

Runa watched it go. The tallest tower in Vael. It went down like a snow-cornice when you stamp on it, all at once and then in pieces, with a long roar coming up the mountain after it and a cloud of grey dust going up off the hill into the noon.

Then the thing put its head down into the hill, between its paws, and she felt Tatter feel the ground open.

---

Afterwards she could never say how long it was. Tilde said a quarter of an hour. Grete said all afternoon. Runa had the glass by then, because Tilde had sat down on the ledge with her back against the rock and wasn't using it.

It lifted its head out of the hill. Slowly. With dust coming off it. And there was something in its jaws. Small, against it. Grey and round.

It held it up.

Then it went back the way it had come, three wingbeats, and lay down in the broken ice where it had lain, and put its head on its paws with the round thing still in its mouth.

"What's it got?" said Runa.

Tilde didn't answer. She was looking at the hill, at the place where the blue tower had been.

"There were people on that terrace," Tilde said.

---

The surge came up at dusk.

Tatter felt it coming before she did, and it was the first time since the spring that she'd felt him afraid of something he wanted. He came off his ledge and sat on the lip of the Stair with his wings half open and his head stretched toward the city, and she felt the pull in him like a calf at a rope.

Then every lamp in Vael lit at once.

White. All of them. The hill, the lower city, the dead lamps that had been dark for weeks, the quays, the steps, so that the whole city stood up out of the dusk round the lake, white as a snowfield at noon. And the roost lamp on its iron bracket at the hut door, which had burned brown all summer and gone out a week since, came on white in the same breath, and Grete cried out and put her arm over her eyes.

A yearling went past Runa's head.

One of the unbonded ones off the high ledges. Young. She heard the wind of it and ducked, and it went down the Stair in a long fast slant with its wings back, straight at the roost lamp, and hit it.

The sound was small. Smaller than she would have thought. The bracket rang. The yearling fell on the step under the lamp and opened one wing and closed it.

Then the others came off the high ledges.

Tilde was faster than any of them. She had the feed-sack off its nail and over the lamp before the second kite was halfway down, and she held it there, both arms round the bracket, with the white light coming through the sacking in a hundred points, while the kites came down round her and pulled up at the last breath and went screaming back up the crag. Mallow was off her ledge. Slate. Grete was shouting.

"Hold him," said Tilde, through her teeth. "Runa. Hold yours."

She had her arms round Tatter's neck already. She didn't remember doing it. He was pulling. Not hard. If he'd pulled hard she couldn't have held him, nobody could. He was pulling as you lean on a door you know is locked, and she said into the down at the base of his neck, *no, no, it's a lamp, it's only a lamp, no,* and felt him hear her, and lean, and lean.

*Full,* he said. *It's so full. It's all down there.*

"I know."

The cold came up the mountain then. Off the lake. A wind. In the last week of summer, a wind off Glasswater with frost in it, coming up the pass road and over the pass-stone and into the Stair, so cold that it took her breath, and the yearling on the step under the sacked lamp was white with rime before it was quite still.

---

In the morning the island went.

She was asleep against Tatter on the ledge with Grete's spare felt over both of them, and she woke because he lifted his head, and the frost cracked off the felt.

Grey light. The city's lamps still white, paled by the morning. And out on the broken lake, the island standing up again in the ice-fog, turning its great head to look at the city. Then it went up, slowly, as if it were tired, and turned west, and came along the lake toward the pass.

The sky emptied ahead of it. She felt it empty. Every kite on the Stair went flat.

It went over the far shore low, so low that she saw the pines bend on the hillside above the little villages there, and on along the snow-line under the High Crown, north of the pass, half a league off, with the sun coming up behind it and its shadow running out ahead on the snow. She lay flat on the ledge with her face in Tatter's feathers and felt the cold of its shadow go by on the snow below the crag. It was colder than the frost.

When she looked up, it was a grey speck in the west, going away.

*Gone,* said Tatter. And then, after a while, puzzled, which was a thing he was often: *Still full, though.*

---

It was full for five days.

She learned what that meant from the frost. Every night it came down on the pass as if it were the dead of winter, and in the morning the whole crag was white with it, the ledges, the felt hut, the kites' backs, crusted, so that they cracked when they stretched. It lay on the lower city too. She could see it through the glass. On the roofs of the empty streets by the water, grey-white, and it didn't go when the sun came up.

The ice came back on the lake, thicker every dawn.

On the second morning there was snow on the pass. Snow on the summer road, at the end of summer, down to the toll-house. The tuskers' road, that they wouldn't walk until the first of winter.

And on the second morning the lion came up out of the city.

She saw it from the Stair. Up through the empty lower city and the crowded streets above it and out on the pass road to the lake gate, with the woman walking at its shoulder. The Wardens at the gate stood back. It went through, and past the toll-house, and lay down in the road on the far side of the iron-rail fence, in the snow, with its chin on the frozen ruts.

Then the crag hummed.

She felt it through the soles of her feet. Through the rock, through the ledge, through Tatter's body under her hand. A sound too low to be a sound, going up the road under the snow, up the pass, up toward the High Crown. The tuskers' rumble. She had felt it all her life, every winter, when the herds came down to the pass-stone and talked. She had never felt it come from below.

*Big,* said Tatter, with his head on one side. *Big, and slow. Calling something.*

"The herd."

*Is it?* He listened. *Then they'll come.*

Her mother sent her down at noon with a can of soup for the gate, because the pass-watch couldn't leave and the wind had the frost in it. Runa went down the zigzags on foot in the snow with the can wrapped in felt and stood at the fence and looked through the rails.

The lion lay in the road. It was bigger than she'd thought from the air. Grey as the pass-stone, with a mane like broken slate, and its eyes shut. The woman sat on the frozen ruts beside its head with her back against its shoulder and her knees up. She was younger than Runa had thought. She had a Hearthmere coat with frost on the shoulders, and a knight's sword across her knees, and she was looking at the snow on the pass with no expression at all, as people look at a road they're going to have to walk.

Brannagh came out of the toll-house and took the soup off Runa without a word and drank half of it standing by the fence.

"Does she want some?" said Runa.

Brannagh looked at the woman. He looked at the lion. He drank the other half.

"She'll not ask," he said, and gave her the can back.

---

The herd came down out of the snow at noon on the fourth day, and the wild kites left.

She saw both. She was on the Stair with the glass, watching the city, because there was nothing else to do: the roost's flights were stopped, and the satchels of physic for the Saddle and the far huts were going up by mule, slowly, a day where a kite would have taken an hour. That morning the dyers had gone back down into the lower city. She'd seen them, a long line with handcarts coming down Rope Street, and somebody trying to stop them at the bottom of it, and the line going round him. Four nights in the frost on the steps, with their houses and their vats down there and nothing happening. She'd have gone too.

Then the rumble came up the road under her feet, and she turned the glass on the pass.

The tuskers came out of the snow by the pass-stone. Thirty of them, red-brown, white along their backs, with their breath going up in clouds. The old cow first with her broken tusk. They went past the stone and down the zigzags to the lake gate without stopping, and she watched Brannagh come out of the toll-house and look at the old cow across the fence, and take the bar off the gate himself, and swing it open, and stand beside it.

She was watching the herd go through the gate when the kites went over her.

All of them. Every unbonded kite on the high ledges, and the ones from the crags beyond the Stair, and some she'd never seen, from further up, off the High Crown. Two dozen. More. They came off the rock above her all at once, without a sound, and went out over the pass in a long grey string, west, not sliding, beating their wings, which kites never did if they could help it. Low and fast and all together, along the snow-line, where the island had gone.

They didn't come back. She watched until they were a thread, and then nothing.

Tatter was standing on the lip of his ledge with his wings open.

She went to him. She put her hands in the down at his neck. He was shaking.

*Go,* he said. *Go, go. They're right. It's coming up.*

"What is?"

*The cold. Under the flat cold. It's coming up under it.* He was leaning out off the ledge, into the air, and the air was empty, and he wanted it so badly she could taste it, like snow in the mouth. *Go west. Go with them.*

"I can't."

She meant: I'm bonded to the toll-house. I'm fourteen. My mother's down there ladling soup.

He knew what she meant. A bond doesn't leave anything out. He stood on the lip of the ledge with his wings open and the empty sky in front of him, shaking, and she stood with her arms round his neck, and after a long while he folded his wings, slowly, joint by joint, and got down off the lip and put his head against her chest, as he had the first morning.

Slate was on her ledge with her wings tight to her sides and her eyes shut. Mallow was in the hut with Grete, under the felt, which no kite had ever done.

Tilde stood at the top of the Stair and looked west after the wild ones until there was nothing to look at.

"Well," she said. "That's not weather."

---

The two of them came up the zigzags in the dark, without a lantern.

Runa heard them before Tilde did. A woman's voice, low and short, saying *mind there* and *that one's loose*, and a man's answering, and the scrape of somebody who walked carefully, as if every stone on the road might turn. She was on her ledge with Tatter. She sat up and watched them come up the last of the road past the pass-stone in the starlight, two shapes, one square and one thin, and stop at the foot of the Stair and look up at it.

"Who's in charge here?" said the square one.

Tilde came out of the hut. She'd been asleep. She had her felt round her shoulders and her hair down, and she looked at the two of them from the top step with her arms folded.

"I'm the one that's awake," she said. "Who's asking?"

"Nessa Vael. I keep the *Heron*." The woman came up three steps. She was broad and sunburned under a scarf, with her sleeves rolled up in the frost, and she stood on the Stair as if it were a boat that might move under her. "This is my brother."

Tilde looked past her at the thin one.

He had a scarf over his face against the cold. He pulled it down. In the starlight Runa saw a long face, grey and young and tired, looking older than it should.

"Reader Vael," said Tilde.

"Yes."

"There's a summons out for you. From the Hall of Rings. It came up with the owl."

"I know. Three."

"Four," said Tilde. "As of tonight."

He didn't seem surprised. He seemed, Runa thought, a man who'd stopped expecting anything else to surprise him, and was mildly interested to find that there were still numbers he didn't know.

"Your wild kites," he said. "Have they gone?"

Tilde was quiet a moment.

"At noon," she said. "All of them. West."

He shut his eyes. He did it as Tilde did sums for the weighing-beam, with his lips moving very slightly. Then he opened them.

"And the ones that stayed?"

"Ours are bonded. They stay."

"Do they want to go?"

Tilde didn't answer that. She looked down the Stair to Runa's ledge. To Tatter, with his head up, his ragged fork stirring in the frost.

The reader looked too. He looked at Tatter as if the kite were a page he'd read once in a copy and was seeing now in the hand of the man who wrote it.

"Then it's tomorrow," he said. "Before noon, I think."

"What is?" said Tilde.

"Whatever's under the lake." He said it quite plainly, as if he were saying the depth of a channel. "At the Moot, in the Wyldmarch, the birds went at dawn, and the forest got up after. Kites feel weather a day off. The College has their flights charted for two hundred years. One of my oldest charts..." He stopped. "It doesn't matter. Tomorrow. I'd thought the fifth day. It's the fifth day."

---

They came into the hut, because Tilde said she wasn't going to talk on the step in the frost like a toll-clerk.

Grete sat up in the felt and looked at them with interest and said, "Who's this, then?" and Tilde said, "Nobody," loud, and Grete said, "Oh, him," and lay down again. Mallow was under the felt with her. A kite's eye looked out at them from a fold, round and black.

Runa sat in the door with her back against the post. Nobody told her to go.

Nessa didn't sit. She stood in the middle of the hut with her arms folded, looking round at the felt and the hooks and the weighing-beam, and then at Tilde.

"There's a village on the far shore," she said. "Stell. You'll know it."

"I know it."

"It's my landing. Mine and my father's and his. Twice a day, salt and flour and whoever's going." She said it fast, as if she'd said it to herself all the way up the mountain. "On the last day the ferries ran I took a woman over. Bettrys. A midwife, from Lamp Street. Jenet at Stell was near her time and there's nobody that side does it. I said I'd fetch her back the next morning. And the next morning my brother came down to the steps."

"I watched you pull the boats up," said Tilde. "Through the glass. All of you. It was well done."

"He said don't cross. He said don't take the boat out." Nessa's jaw worked. "So I didn't. And there's been ice on it every day since, from the island to the steps, and now from the steps to the far side, a floor of it. You can stand on it. Nobody's going to walk a league over that. Over where that thing was lying." She looked at the wall of the hut as if she could see through it to the lake. "They've had nobody since. Not a word. They'll have seen the tower go, from there. They'll have seen the lamps. They'll not know anything else."

"And you want a kite."

"I want them told."

"Told what?"

Nessa looked at her brother.

He had sat down on the felt-chest by the door, near Runa, without being asked, with his hands between his knees. He had something in one of them: a little green glass, a bottle-neck with a bulb at the bottom, the kind the College's tide-readers carried, that the pass-watch called *quicksilver-fiddles*. He wasn't looking at it.

"Get up off the shore," he said. "All of them. Up the hill as high as they can get, by the middle of the morning. And stay there until dark. Don't go down to look." He turned the glass in his fingers. "And tell them to send to the next villages along. All along that shore. I don't know how far."

"Why the middle of the morning?" said Tilde.

"Because if it's later than that, I'll have been wrong by a little, and nobody minds that." He said it without any smile at all. "And if it's sooner, I'll have been wrong by a great deal, and then it doesn't matter what I said."

---

Tilde said no.

She said it sitting on the weighing-beam's block with her old frostbitten hands on her knees, looking at the floor of the hut, not at either of them.

"There's an order," she said. "Since the summer. No kite over the water."

"The order came from the Blue tower," said Nessa. "There isn't a Blue tower."

"The order isn't in the tower." Tilde looked up. "And never mind orders. You've seen the lake. The whole city's lit like a smithy. The night the hill went, a yearling went into my roost lamp. It's under the scree now, if you want to see what a kite does in this. They'll not fly in it. They'll fly into it." She looked at Idony. "You know that. You said you'd read the charts."

"In a surge they fly into walls and lamps," he said. "Yes."

"And over the water there's a hole in the air. That's what they say. Ask any of them." Tilde nodded down the Stair at the ledges. "Tatter told Runa so in the first week. A hole, and everything goes down into it."

He turned his head and looked at Runa.

It was the first time anybody had looked at her since they came in. She found she didn't like it. It was a measuring look. Not unkind. As if she were a column of numbers he had to get right.

"Did he?" he said.

"Yes," said Runa.

"Is it still there?"

She didn't know. She thought about it. She thought about Tatter on the lip of the ledge at noon with his wings open, shaking, and what he'd said. *It's coming up.*

"He says it's full," she said. "He says it's not a hole now. He says it's coming up under the ice."

The reader looked at her a moment longer. Then he nodded, once, to himself, as a man nods at a sum that's come out.

"Thank you," he said, which nobody had ever said to her for telling them what Tatter said.

Tilde stood up.

"No," she said again. "I'm sorry for your landing. I am. I'll not send a child over that on a kite that's been shaking all day, on your say-so, with the Hall of Rings sending owls up after you." She looked at Idony. "I'll not say you were here. That's what I'll do."

He stood up too. He didn't argue. He seemed to Runa a man who had argued a great deal in his life about the wrong things and had stopped, lately, arguing about the right ones.

"Then I'll go down and tell the dyers again," he said. "They won't listen either." He put the little glass in his coat. "Thank you for the fire."

There wasn't a fire. He didn't seem to notice.

---

Nessa stopped in the door.

Runa was still sitting against the post, and Nessa looked down at her, at the felt and the bone horn on its cord at her neck and her wrists sticking out of her sleeves.

"Have you eaten?" said Nessa.

Runa was so surprised that she told the truth.

"Not since this morning."

"Why not?"

"I'm weighed at the new moon."

Nessa looked at her for a long moment, as she might have looked at a sail with a hole in it.

Then she put her hand in her coat and took out something wrapped in a cloth and pushed it into Runa's hands. It was heavy and soft and smelled of woodsmoke and lake.

"Whitefish," said Nessa. "Smoked. He won't eat it." She jerked her head at her brother, who was already going down the steps in the dark, carefully, as if every one of them might turn. "Nobody in this family eats."

She went after him. Runa heard her voice going down the zigzags, low and short, *mind that one, I said mind it*, until the frost took it.

---

She didn't sleep.

She lay on the ledge with Tatter and the whitefish in her coat and looked at the city. It was very bright. Every lamp in it white, burning cold, the hill and the lower city and the empty quays all lit, and the lake beyond it black and white, black where the old broken ice had frozen into it at angles, white where the new ice lay smooth, and nothing on it. No island. No ferries. A floor, a league across, to the far shore, where there were no lamps at all.

Stell was over there. She'd seen it from the air a hundred times before the summer. A bench of shingle under a steep hill of pines and scree, twenty roofs, a jetty, a sheepfold on the shoulder above. The *Heron* coming in to it, small, with her brown sail, and somebody on the jetty taking the rope.

*Full,* said Tatter, in the dark. *Coming up.*

"How long?"

He didn't understand *how long*. He never had. Kites knew *soon* and *not soon* and *now*. She asked it another way.

"Is it now?"

*No.* He turned his head under his wing. *Morning. When the air comes down off the snow.*

She lay and thought about the weighing-beam. Six and a quarter. About her mother at the toll-house, who had wanted her to be a cook, and had cried the morning Tatter came down the Stair on foot, and had said, after, *well, you'll be up there and not down here, at least, and up there you're light*. About the yearling on the step under the sack, white with rime.

About a woman called Bettrys, from Lamp Street, standing on a jetty on the far shore every morning in the frost with her bag, looking across a league of ice for a brown sail.

Some time before dawn she unwrapped the whitefish and ate it. All of it. It was very good. She licked her fingers in the dark and lay with her hand on her stomach and felt how heavy it was, a quarter of a stone, she thought, at least, and found she didn't mind.

---

The air came down off the snow at first light, as it always did.

She felt it before she saw it. A stirring along the ledges, the cold sliding down off the High Crown over the crag and the pass-stone and on down the mountain toward the lake, heavy and steady, a river of it. The kites' road. Every morning of her life it had come down like that, and the kites had stepped off into it and gone down the mountain on it.

She strapped Tatter's girth by feel. Grey felt, wide, under his wings and over the hollow of his back, the buckles cold enough to take the skin off. She had done it a thousand times. Her fingers knew it. He stood for it with his head up, shaking a little, the frost cracking off his long primaries every time he moved them.

*No,* he said.

"Yes."

*Over the flat cold?*

"Yes."

He was quiet a long time.

*It's coming up,* he said. *I said.*

"I know. That's why."

She felt him not understand it. Then she felt him understand it, which was worse, because a kite knows only that the air has changed and the right thing to do is to go with it, and what she was asking him was to go against it, into it, and he could find nothing in himself for that. And then she felt him look for it anyway. Feel round for it, in the high cold room where he lived, as you'd feel in the dark for a step.

She didn't know whether he'd find it.

She lay down along his back with her cheek in the down of his neck and her hands in the ruff and her knees in the hollow behind his wings, where she belonged.

At the top of the Stair the hut door opened.

She didn't look round. She knew it was Tilde. She knew it by the cough. She waited for her name, and for *get down off that kite*, and for the weighing-beam and the stones and the end of everything.

Tilde said nothing.

Runa looked round.

Tilde stood in the hut door in her felt with her hair down and her arms folded, and looked at her, and at Tatter, and didn't call.

And then all the birds in Vael went up at once.

---

She heard them first. A roar, coming up the mountain from the city, like a gale in pines, though the morning was still. Then she saw them. Off every roof and tower and gutter and window-ledge in Vael, off the broken hill and the white-lit colleges and the frosted lower city, off the steps and the slips: rooks and pigeons and gulls and sparrows and the lantern owls out of the observatory towers, every bird in the city, in one black cloud, going up off it together. They came up the lake shore and up the pass road and over the pass-stone and over the Stair so thick that the dawn went dark, and the sound of them was the only sound there was. Going west. After the kites.

Tatter's wings came open.

She felt it go through him. Everything he was, at once, turning west. The whole sky going one way, the right way, every wing in the world, and him in it.

*Go,* he said. *Oh, go.*

"The other way," she said into his neck. "Tatter. The other way."

He stepped off the ledge.

---

They went down into the morning air and it took them, as it always did, the cold river off the snow, and for a long breath she didn't know which way he'd go. The birds were all round them. Above, below. A rook went past so close that its wing struck her arm. Tatter's wings were full out, forty feet of them, grey and stiff, and the air pouring down the mountain was under them, and the birds were pouring west through it, and he hung.

Then he turned.

Down. Down the mountain on the cold air with the birds going over him, against them, out of the black cloud into clear grey light, so fast that her eyes ran and the frost on his feathers broke off in her face like salt. Down the pass road. Over the zigzags. Over the toll-house, with its chimney smoking, and her mother in there, and the iron-rail fence and the lake gate standing open.

*Wrong,* said Tatter. *Wrong, wrong.* But he was doing it.

---

The city came up at them, white.

She'd known it would. She hadn't known what it would be like. Every lamp in Vael burning cold in the grey dawn, on every corner, in every window, and each one of them a pull, she felt it through him, a pull like a slope under a sledge, down, toward it. Tatter's head kept going to them. A lamp on a corner of the pass road. A lamp over a door. The great white windows of the Library on the broken hill above, burning, so bright that he dipped toward them, and she pulled his head round with both hands in the ruff and said *no, out, out over the ice, out*, and he went.

He went out along the lower city's shore, low, over the frosted roofs. She saw it all, in pieces.

The dyers' yards, at the water's end, and smoke going up from a chimney in the low street behind the fish-market. Somebody had found something to burn.

The fish-market, full of tuskers. Thirty of them standing among the dead stalls in the frost with their breath going up, and the calves in the middle, and every one of them with its head turned toward the lake.

The slips. The whole ferry fleet keel-up along the top of them in a row, black, like sledges stacked against a barn wall for the summer. And one of them broader than the rest, tarred, with something lashed to her keel: a wicker cage, and in the cage a small round shape, and two eyes in it, white as the lamps, staring out over the ice.

The *Heron*. Nessa's ferry. Going nowhere.

And at the water's edge beyond the old fish-quay, on the white shingle, a grey heap she knew was the lion, lying down. And beside it, standing, a dark shape with a light in its hand. Not a lamp. A thin white line, held upright, as long as an arm, burning.

Then they were over the ice.

---

The pull stopped.

All at once, as if she'd come off a slope onto flat snow. There were no lamps on the ice. There was only the ice, grey-white and black, a league of it, with the old broken plates frozen into it at angles where the island had lain, and the far shore a dark line under the snowfields, and the sky over it pale.

Tatter levelled out. She felt him do it. Felt him find, out over the ice, that the air was only air, that nothing was pulling, and his wings eased, and his long primaries spread like fingers.

Then he felt what was under it.

She felt it through him. Far down. Under the ice, under the water, under the bottom of the lake, so far down that it was less a thing than a weight. A turning. Slow, and huge, and cold, colder than the ice. Coming up.

*There,* said Tatter, very small. *There. I said.*

He went across the ice so fast and so low that his shadow ran under them on the grey floor like a fox on snow, and she lay flat on his back and didn't look down.

---

Stell had smoke from three chimneys and a man on the jetty.

They came in over the shingle bench with the pines going up steep behind it and Tatter put his wings back and stalled, badly, untidily, his ragged fork splayed, and came down on the shingle in front of the ferry-house in a sprawl that put her off his back and onto the stones on her hands.

When she got up, the man on the jetty was looking at her.

He was old. Older than Tilde. Long and stooped, in a fisherman's smock, with a lantern hanging unlit from a hook on the jetty-post beside him and a boathook in his hands, and he stood at the end of the jetty where the ice began and looked at a girl in grey felt who'd fallen off a stormkite on his shingle.

"You're from the pass," he said.

"Yes."

"Is that a kite?"

"Yes."

"Over the water," he said. "They don't." He looked at Tatter, who was standing on the shingle with his wings half open and his head turned out toward the middle of the lake. "Everybody knows they don't."

Behind him, at the door of the ferry-house, a woman had come out. Middle-aged, plain, in a good Vael coat with the cuffs frayed and a bag on her arm, a stout leather bag with brass corners. She had a shawl over her head. She looked at Runa and then past her, across the ice, toward the city.

"Is she coming?" said the woman.

Runa knew who she meant.

---

She told them on the shingle, and then she told it again in the ferry-house kitchen, to more of them, and then she stood on the jetty and shouted it, because by then the whole of Stell had come down to look at the kite.

Twenty houses. Fisher-folk, and two shepherd families from the shoulder, and a man who made nets. Children. A very old woman in a chair who was somebody's grandmother. And a young one at the back with a bundle, Jenet, white-faced and furious, with a baby six days old on her shoulder who had been born, Runa learned, on the night every lamp across the lake went white.

They had seen everything. That was the trouble. They'd seen the ice break and the island stand up and the tower go down, from across the lake, small. They'd seen the lamps go out and come on. They'd had the island fly over them at dawn so low that it had flattened the pines above the fold, and stood in their doors after with their hands over their mouths. And then five days of frost and nothing. No ferry. No word. Only the lamps, white, across the ice every night, and their own dark windows.

And now a girl off the pass, on a kite, telling them to leave their houses and go up the hill in the frost because a reader nobody had heard of said so.

"What reader?" said the net-maker.

"The tide-reader. From the College of Tides."

"Which one?"

She didn't say his name. She didn't know why. She thought of four owls from the Hall of Rings.

"The one who told the ferry-folk to get off the shore," she said. "Before the tower went. He told them the day, and they went, and they were up on Candle Row when it came down, every one of them. I saw them through the glass."

The net-maker looked at her.

"Nessa," said the old man from the jetty. Dorran. Somebody had called him Dorran. "That's Nessa's brother. The College one." He said it slowly, as if he were reading it off a slate. "He used to come over with her as a boy and sit in the bow and look at the water through bottles."

"He's not right," said somebody.

"He was right about the tower," said Bettrys, in the ferry-house door. She hadn't come out. She stood with her bag on her arm.

The net-maker looked at Runa's felt. At the bone horn.

"Is it a Warden's order?" he said.

And Runa, who had never lied to a grown person in her life except about whether she'd eaten, heard herself say: "Yes."

---

It moved them. She'd known it would. In Isvaren a Warden's order moved anything: mules, herds, magisters, weather if it could have been written down. The net-maker turned round and started shouting. The shepherd families were already going, because they'd wanted to go since the island flew over and been waiting for somebody to tell them. Two men brought the grandmother's chair. A boy of about ten in a red cap came and stood in front of Runa and asked if he could touch the kite.

"No," she said. "Can you run?"

He could run. She sent him along the shore path to the next hamlet, and told him to tell them the same, and tell them to tell the next, and he went off along the shingle under the pines like a hare, shouting before he was out of hearing.

She watched him go. There were two more hamlets along that shore that she knew of, and smoke beyond them that might have been a third. She couldn't go to them. Tatter was standing on the shingle with his wings open and his head turned to the middle of the lake, and he hadn't moved since they came down, and she didn't know if he'd go up again.

The village went up the hill. Slowly, in a line, up the goat-path through the pines and over the scree to the sheepfold on the shoulder, with bundles and blankets and the chair, and the frost on the path so thick that they went on hands and knees on the steep bits. Jenet went with the baby inside her coat, swearing, and stopped every twenty steps to look back at her house.

Dorran didn't go.

He stood on the jetty with his boathook.

Bettrys didn't go. She stood in the ferry-house door with her bag.

---

"I keep the landing," said Dorran.

Runa stood on the shingle at the foot of the jetty. The sun was up. The ice on the lake had gone from grey to white and the far side, the city, was a low pale smear of light along the water, a league off, with the hill above it and the hole in the hill's crown.

"Everyone's gone up," she said.

"I keep the landing," said Dorran. "Forty-one years. My father before me. Her father's ferry, then hers." He nodded at the lantern on its hook. "When the *Heron*'s due I light that. I've lit it every night since. In case."

"She can't come. It's ice."

"She'll break it. She broke it the last day. Stood in the bow with a pole and broke it all the way over. I never saw anything like it." He looked out at the ice. "She said she'd fetch the midwife back. Nessa doesn't say a thing and not do it."

Bettrys came down the step from the ferry-house door with her bag.

"I've a daughter in Lamp Street," she said to Runa. "Seventeen. On her own. I've not been away from her a night since she was born." She said it quite calmly, as if she were telling Runa how far apart the pains were. "I've stood on this jetty every morning for six days. If the ferry comes, I'm going to be on it." She held her bag with both hands. "I'll not be up a hill when it comes and miss it."

Runa looked from one to the other.

She thought about saying *Warden's order* again. She looked at their faces and didn't.

"It's on the slip," she said. "The *Heron*. Keel-up. I saw it this morning from the air. They've all been pulled up and turned over, the whole fleet, the day the tower went. The owl's in its cage, tied on the keel." She looked at Dorran. "She's not coming. She can't. She's on Candle Row with all the ferry-folk. She sent me."

Nessa hadn't sent her. Nearly, though. She thought of Nessa in the hut with her arms folded, saying *I want them told*.

Dorran stood very still on the end of the jetty.

"Keel-up," he said.

"Yes."

"With the owl."

"Yes."

He looked at the lantern on the hook a while. Then he looked at the ice.

"Well," he said. "I'll not light it tonight, then."

But he didn't move. He stood with his boathook on the end of the jetty looking across at the pale smear of the city, as if if he looked hard enough he would see a brown sail on it, and Bettrys stood at the foot of the jetty with her bag, and neither of them went.

And under the shingle, under Runa's feet, through Tatter, she felt it come.

---

The ice groaned.

It came up through her boots before it came through the air. A sound so low and long that it was more in her knees than her ears. Out in the middle of the lake, a mile off, where the island had lain, the white floor of the ice was lifting. A low hump, coming up out of it, wide as a town, with cracks running out from it in rings.

Dorran turned round.

Then the ice broke in the middle and something came up through it, and for a moment there was only fog, a great white fog boiling up off the lake as if somebody had poured water on a forge. And in the fog, something pale. Moving. A back. Then another, further out, rising and going over and going down. Pale as the ice, white and blue, and so long that she couldn't see where it began, coil after coil of it breaking up through the white floor and going under, until the whole middle of the lake was full of it.

The head came up last.

It came up out of the fog on a neck thicker than the toll-house, high, higher than the hill across the water, blunt and pale and crusted with frost, and she couldn't see any eyes in it. It hung there against the morning.

It opened its mouth.

No sound. Only cold. A wave of it, coming off the thing across the lake toward them, and she felt it coming before it came, and then it was on them, a wind out of the middle of Glasswater so cold that the spit froze on her lip, and the frost on the jetty grew under her eyes. On Bettrys's shawl. On the lantern.

Tatter's wings came open in it.

He didn't mean to. A kite's wings open to wind as a drover's hand closes on a dropped rope. This was wind. Off the lake, onto the shore, up the steep hill behind them, pouring up the pines and the scree to the shoulder where the village was. She felt it go under his wings and lift him, there on the shingle, an inch, two, so that his feet came off the stones.

And the lake rose.

---

She didn't decide anything.

She was on his back. She didn't remember getting there. The girth was still on, she'd never taken it off, and her knees were in the hollow and her hands were in the ruff, and the cold wind off the lake was pouring up the hillside behind the ferry-house, and he went up on it as he'd gone up the Stair's face a thousand times on the morning wind: straight up, without a wingbeat, so fast that the shingle fell away under them and the ferry-house roof went small.

She looked out over the lake from the height of the pines.

The whole lake was lifting. Round the coils, outward, in one long slow swell, as if the thing had taken all the room under the water and the water had nowhere to go but up. The broken ice rode up on it. Plates of it, turning over, grey and white. It came toward the far shore along its whole length, a grey wall the height of a house with the ice in its face, not fast. It didn't need to be fast.

Two figures on the jetty under her. One at the end with a boathook. One at the foot with a bag.

"Down," she said. "Tatter. Down to them."

*Up,* said Tatter. *Up is the only thing. Up.*

"Down."

*It's coming!*

"I know it's coming. Down."

She felt him look for it. In the high cold room. Feel round, for a step that wasn't there, and not find it, and put his foot down anyway.

He turned on the wind and went down across the face of the water.

---

Low along the shore, ahead of it. The swell on her left hand, so close she could hear it, a long grinding roar of ice on ice, and the cold off it like the open door of an ice-house. The shingle under her on the right. Tatter's left wing a yard off the water. A yard off the grey wall, coming.

She had the bone horn in her hand. She didn't remember taking it. She put it to her mouth and blew three, *trouble*, the pass-watch call, the one that brought men out of the toll-house in their shirts.

Dorran looked up.

He looked up at a kite coming down the shore at him a yard off a wall of water, screaming, with a girl on it blowing a horn, and he dropped the boathook.

"Up!" she was shouting. She didn't know if she was making words. "Up, up, it's coming, go up!"

Tatter went over the jetty so low his feet touched the lantern on its hook and set it swinging. Bettrys was standing at the foot of it with her bag, staring past them at the lake. At the grey wall. At the head over it, high in the fog, pale against the morning.

Runa saw her see it.

Bettrys turned round and ran.

She ran up the shingle with her bag in both arms and her shawl gone, and Dorran came off the jetty behind her, long and stooped, running as old men run, all elbows, and at the ferry-house he caught her arm and they went past it together and into the pines, up the goat-path, on their hands and knees in the frost, and Tatter went up over them on the cold wind and turned and came round and she saw them still climbing.

The water came in.

---

It came up the shingle bench all at once, the whole length of the shore, grey and white and full of ice. It went into the ferry-house door and out of its windows. It took the jetty. She saw it go: the posts leaning, all together, and the boards lifting, and the lantern on its hook going round and round on the end of the last post until the post went under and the lantern with it. It went into the twenty houses and through them. It went up into the pines. Up the goat-path, a little way, and stopped there, among the trunks, and hung, and began to go back.

On the path above it, an old man and a woman with a bag were sitting in the frost under a pine with their arms round each other, and the water was ten yards below their boots.

Runa took Tatter up.

She couldn't have stopped him. He went up the face of the hill on the lake wind in a long steady climb without being asked, over the pines and the scree, up to the shoulder, to the fold, and came down on the frozen turf among the sheep and the village and the grandmother's chair, and stood there with his wings half open and his whole body shaking so hard that she couldn't get off him, and then she was off him and on her knees in the frost with her arms round his neck, and he put his head against her chest.

*I did it,* he said. Puzzled. As if he were telling her about weather he'd never seen before. *I went down.*

"You did."

*It was wrong.*

"I know."

He was quiet. She felt him turn it over in the high cold room, the thing he'd done, and not know what it was, and keep it anyway.

*I'd do it again,* he said, as if he didn't think much of the idea.

---

Somebody put a blanket over her. Jenet. She stood over Runa with the baby inside her coat and a sheepfold blanket in her free hand, still furious, and dropped it on her and Tatter both without a word, and went back to the wall of the fold to look down at her house.

Her house was standing. Half of Stell was standing, roofless, with the water going out of the windows. The ferry-house wasn't. There was a gap on the shingle bench where it had been, and a stump where the jetty had been, and the lake going out.

Dorran and Bettrys came up over the lip of the shoulder an hour later, slowly, on their hands and knees, white with frost. Somebody gave Dorran a stick. Bettrys still had her bag.

She came and stood over Runa and looked down at her.

"Your nose is bleeding," she said.

It was. Runa hadn't noticed. Bettrys knelt down in the frost with her bag and opened it and took out a clean cloth, and held it to Runa's face with one hand and the back of her head with the other, firmly, as if Runa were a baby who'd come too fast.

"I've a daughter in Lamp Street," she said again, holding the cloth. "Seventeen. She'll have gone up with the rest. Won't she?"

Runa didn't know where Lamp Street was.

"I saw them all go up," she said, into the cloth. "The day the tower went. Everybody."

"Yes." Bettrys held it there. "Yes. She will have."

---

Across the lake, the water had gone into the city.

She could see it from the fold without the glass. The lower city, under a grey sheet of water and ice, with the roofs standing out of it. The white lamps burning under the water, some of them, still, a pale glow in the flood. And in the middle of the drowned shore, at the low end where the dyers' yards were, a long grey line across it that hadn't been there in the morning. A wall. Straight as a ruled line, stone-grey, with the water leaning on it on one side and the street behind it dry.

She didn't know what it was. She thought of the lion at the water's edge, and the thin white light in the dark shape's hand, and didn't know.

On the water all over the drowned streets, red-brown shapes were moving. Slowly. Back and forth between the high steps and the roofs. The herd.

She sat in the fold with the blanket round her and the cloth on her nose and watched them.

---

The thing went at midday.

It went south. Down the lake, in its own fog, slowly, coil after coil going over and under in the broken ice, with the long blunt head out in front, low now, near the water. Away from Stell, away from the city. Toward the lake's foot, where the hills came down on either side to a narrow place and the lake went over a lip of stone into the valley.

The whole lake went with it. She saw that. The water drawing back off both shores, off the drowned city and off Stell's shingle and the stumps of the jetty, going south in a long grey churn.

At the lake's foot it went over the lip.

The head first, down out of sight into the valley. Then the coils, over and over, pouring down into the gap in the fog like a snow-slide going down a gully. And then a sound came up the lake, a crack she felt in the fold's stones, and a whole grey flank of hill at the lake's foot slid down into the valley after it in a long fall of rock.

The lake went through the gap.

She heard it go. All the way from Stell. A roar, going on and on, as Glasswater poured out of itself through the broken lip and down the valley, south, toward the river. Toward the Wend. Toward everywhere downstream that had never heard of Stell or the Kite Stair or a reader with a bottle on a string.

Jenet stood at the wall of the fold with the baby and listened to it.

"Where's it going?" she said.

Nobody knew. Runa knew the valley went to the Wend, because the drovers said so. She didn't know what was on the Wend. Farms, she supposed. Ferries.

---

In the early afternoon Tatter went flat.

He'd been lying in the lee of the fold wall with his wings folded and his eyes half shut, too tired even to preen, and he went flat all at once, wings over his head, face in the turf, and every sheep in the fold bolted to the far wall.

She looked south.

Two shapes, low over the brown water in the valley below the broken lip, coming up it. North. Against the flood. A big red one, heavy in the wing, and a small green one a little ahead of it, quick, flicking about over the water like a goat-kid on a ledge. They came up the drowned valley so low that their shadows ran on the flood under them, and over the gap, and up the lake over the grey churn and the broken ice, and over the drowned city, and up. Over the broken hill.

Dragons. She'd never seen one. She'd heard the salt-merchants' stories at the toll-house, every summer, and not believed half of them.

She watched them go over the hill and out of sight.

*Big,* said Tatter, into the turf. *Hot. Gone over.* He didn't lift his head. *Is it all gone over now?*

"Nearly," said Runa.

Later there was smoke on the hill. Black, thick, going straight up in the frost-still air from behind the Library's long front. She watched it all afternoon. She didn't know what it was either.

---

The lamps died at dusk.

She was at the fold wall with half of Stell beside her, looking across the lake. Or across where the lake had been. It was mostly gone. A plain of mud and shingle and broken ice where the water had drawn back, a league of it, grey in the last light, with a channel of brown water still running down the middle of it to the gap. And the city beyond it, white. Every lamp in Vael still burning as it had for five days.

Then they went out.

One after another. From the top of the hill down. The Library's windows first, then the towers, then street by street, down the hill toward the shore, as if the toll-house boy were going round at bedtime with the snuffer, only faster than any boy. Down to the drowned lower city. Down to the shore. The last one, a single pale light by the water, near the slips.

Then nothing.

She'd never seen Vael dark. Nobody had. The colleges had lamps that burned without oil before anybody's grandmother. From the Stair at night it had always been there, below, a hand of cards spread on the black. Now there was only the black, and the shape of the hill against the stars, and a few small yellow points of tallow here and there, very small.

Tatter lifted his head.

She felt it go out of him. The fear stayed. It was the other thing that went: the thing he'd lived in since before she knew him: the roads in the air, the lines he flew, that every kite flew, that the College had charted for two hundred years. The pull and the lift and the current that ran under the wind. She felt it go out of the sky over the lake, all of it, at once, like water out of a mule-trough when the bung's pulled, and leave the sky flat.

*Gone,* said Tatter. He said it very quietly. *The roads are gone.*

"What's left?"

He was quiet. He looked at the sky over the dead lake as she had seen old drovers look at a valley where the snow had taken the road.

*Air,* he said.

---

They lit a fire in the fold, of wreck-wood the shepherds dragged up from the stumps of the jetty, and it wouldn't catch until a fisherman struck a flint over it for half an hour, swearing. Nobody had a word that would light it. Everybody tried. Bettrys tried, kneeling, and sat back on her heels and looked at her hands.

"That's always worked," she said. "Since I was a girl."

They sat round it in blankets with the sheep pressed against the far wall and the baby crying, and somebody passed round a pot of something hot, and Runa drank from it and found it was barley water, and was so hungry that she drank half of it before she remembered the weighing-beam, and then drank the rest.

When it was fully dark she went to the fold wall and looked across the lake.

There was a fire on the hill.

Up on the crown of it, where the Library was. One fire. Big, and red, and steady, on the black front of the hill where the Library's steps came down. It was the only light in Vael. She could see it from a league off across the dead lake. People were standing round it. She could see them, small, black against it, and something very large lying on the steps below it, and something larger standing with its head over it. And the fire going up into the frost-still air in a long red column, straight, with sparks in it.

Dorran came and stood beside her with his stick.

"Somebody's dead," he said.

"How do you know?"

"You don't build a fire like that for the living. Not on steps." He looked at it for a while. "That'll be somebody they thought a lot of."

She didn't ask what he meant. She stood at the wall with him and watched the fire on the hill burn, until the cold drove her back to Tatter, and she lay down in the lee of the fold against his side under Jenet's blanket, and he put a wing over her, which he had never done.

---

The air came down off the snow at first light, as it always did.

She felt it in her sleep and woke. The cold sliding down off the High Crown, over the far shore's pines and the shoulder and the fold, heavy and steady, on its way to where the lake had been. A plain wind. Nothing under it. No pull, no current. Only cold air, heavy, going downhill.

Tatter was standing at the lip of the shoulder with his wings open.

She went to him with the girth over her arm. Stell was asleep round the cold fire. Dorran asleep sitting up against the wall with his stick between his knees. Bettrys asleep with her bag for a pillow and one hand on it.

*Plain,* said Tatter, as she buckled him. He was feeling the air with his long primaries, spreading them, closing them, as Grete's fingers went over a fleece for the thin places. *It's only plain.*

"Will it hold you?"

He thought about that. She felt him think. Then she felt him do something she'd never felt a kite do: decide to find out.

He stepped off.

---

It held him.

It was worse than the old air. Thinner, somehow, and lumpier, with no road in it, so that he had to work every yard of it, his wings flexing, his ragged fork twisting and twisting to keep him straight. But it held. It poured down off the snow and he went down the hill on it, over the drowned pines and the wrecked houses of Stell and the stumps of the jetty, and out.

Over the lake bed.

A league of it, under her. Mud and shingle, flat and grey, with broken plates of ice stranded on it at angles and a brown channel down the middle going south to the gap, and all of it white with frost. A finger thick, she thought. White as the snow on the High Crown, from Stell's shingle to the city's. He went across it high, higher than she'd dared yesterday, because there was no hole in the air now and nothing to fall into, and the cold rose off the frost, and the sun was coming up behind the Fells.

The city came up ahead of her, dark. No lamps. Smoke from the hill, grey now, the fire gone to ash on the steps. The drowned lower city brown and wrecked, and the grey wall across its low end standing in the mud with nothing leaning on it any more.

And on the shore below the old fish-quay, on the frosted mud where the water had been, three figures going away up the shore to the west road. Two people, small, one of them bent over a stick. And something grey and huge walking between them.

Something red went up off the shore behind them, and something green with it, and a smaller red one after them, labouring, and they went south. Low and fast over the broken lip and down the drowned valley, the same way the water had gone, as if they meant to catch it.

Tatter didn't go flat. He tipped a wing at them and went on.

---

She saw the drawing as she came over the shore.

On the frosted mud, at the edge of the dead lake, where somebody had had a fire and the ash of it lay black on the white. Lines. Cut into the frost so that the dark mud showed through. A circle, wide as a threshing-floor. A wavering line going through it, like a river. And out from the circle, six long straight lines, each of them forty paces at least, going away from it in every direction across the frost, with a mark at the end of each one.

She took Tatter round it, once, slowly, low.

She didn't know what it was. It looked like nothing she had ever seen and like something every child has drawn on a slate. A sun, with its rays. A wheel off a cart.

The sun came over the Fells and lay along the frost, and the lines began to soften at their edges.

*Roads,* said Tatter, looking down at it, with his head on one side.

She didn't know how he knew. She didn't ask. She held on, and he went up off the shore on the plain cold air, and turned toward the pass, and below them the six lines lay out on the white mud in the first light with the sun coming to take them.
