---

On the ledges they say the hatching caves were warm before there was a Clan to sit in them. They say the first dragons found the place by putting their bellies on the moor and feeling for it, as you feel for the warm side of a loaf, and that the warm has never gone out of the floor since, not in the hardest winter, not in the year the tarn below went black with ash. Every dragon of the Clan was laid in an egg on that floor. Every one comes back to it. There is a saying at Varrhold that a dragon will forget its rider before it forgets the caves, and the riders laugh when they say it, and don't look at each other.

Wren didn't know the saying. She knew the floor.

She knew that if you lay on it with your cheek flat you could feel it humming, very low, too low to hear, like Bel's big kettle on the back of the fire before it thought about boiling. She knew which bowls were cold at the bottom and which were warm, and that the warmest were nearest the back, where the spring came up out of the rock and steamed and went off down its channel to the cave mouth and over the edge to the tarn. She knew that the water in the channel was warm enough to put your feet in until you'd counted fifty, and then it wasn't. She knew where the roof dripped. She had lived in the caves since the autumn with Gunna Tarne and Gunna's old dragon and the boy Leif, and the winterers at the back, and Old Grey, and she knew the caves better than she knew the hold four leagues down, where Papa was, and Aunt Bel, and Pip and Nan, and the dogs under the bench.

She knew the little red one best of all, and the little red one didn't know anything.

---

She'd come on the ninth day after a city Wren had never seen.

Wren had been at the back of the caves, with her feet in the channel, counting. She heard Kharrak land. You always heard Kharrak land. He came down on the rock outside the cave mouth as if he wanted the whole moor to know about it, and stones went rattling down the scree, and Gunna, who was mending a girth by the fire, said something about him that Wren wasn't supposed to hear and heard.

Wren took her feet out of the water at thirty-one and went to see.

There was Papa getting down. There was Lisska, riding on Kharrak's back between his shoulders, which she was saying in Papa's head was undignified, Wren could tell from Papa's face, and not getting down. And there was a third dragon on the rock behind them.

Small. Red, a dark red that went nearly black where the shadow was on it. No bigger than Mabbin. Standing a little apart from the other two, as a new heifer stands at the edge of a herd, as if she'd been put there.

Wren stopped in the mouth of the cave.

Behind her Old Grey came up out of the dark, slow, and stood at her shoulder. She felt him there. She didn't look round.

The little red one walked in past them both. She didn't look at Wren. She didn't look at Old Grey, or at the fire, or at Gunna getting up off her stool, or at the rows of stone bowls going back into the dark. She walked down between them on her own to the back of the cave, to the spring, and lay down beside the warm water with her chin on the stone and her eyes half shut. And stayed there.

"She's gone back in," said Gunna. Gunna had seen it before. She said it as she'd say a ewe had cast a lamb. "Some come out again. Some don't."

"I know," said Papa.

He came and stood behind Wren and put his hand on her head. His hand smelled of Kharrak, which was hot stone and smoke, and of a long way off. She didn't say anything. She stood in the warm air that came out of the hill and looked at the little red one by the spring, and stored it.

Papa stayed one night. He told Gunna things by the fire that Wren pretended to be asleep for. A roof. A drowned city. A man on some steps who didn't move. When Papa said the man on the steps, Gunna nodded as if she'd expected him.

In the morning he went down to the hold with the old one's name on a paper in his coat, and Lisska went with him, and the little red one didn't lift her head.

---

That was the autumn.

She lay by the spring all of it. She lay with her chin on the stone and her eyes half shut and the warm water going past her nose, and when Gunna put mutton under her nose she ate it, slowly, without looking at it, and when nobody put mutton under her nose she didn't eat. She didn't get up to drink. She drank lying down, from the channel, when her head happened to be near it. The winterers at the back, the old ones who didn't belong to anybody, lifted their heads now and then and looked at her and put them down again. Tamsin went and lay beside her once, the length of her, as Lisska lay against Papa's back on cold nights, and stayed until the morning. The little red one didn't move away. She didn't move toward.

Wren watched her.

She didn't go close. She watched from the fire, and from the bowl she sat in to do her letters, and from the channel where she put her feet, and from the far end of the caves when she went to bring the hens in. She watched as she used to watch Old Grey across the paddock gate at Upper Rush, in the winter when he first came home: not to see anything happen. To see what he looked like when nothing happened. So she'd know.

She knew what empty looked like. That was the thing nobody at the caves knew she knew, except Gunna, maybe, because Gunna knew most things. Empty looked like a beast that was standing in front of you and wasn't there. It looked like eyes that were open and had nobody behind them, like a window at the hold in the day, with the sun on the glass, so you saw the sky in it instead of the room. Old Grey's eyes had been like that all one winter. Wren had stood on the bottom bar of the paddock gate and talked to the sky in them every day until the snow went grey, and one day there'd been somebody in the room.

He was standing behind her now. He stood behind her wherever she went. Nobody told him to. Nobody led him. Aunt Bel wouldn't come up to the caves because of it, and Wren knew why, and nobody had told her that either.

---

Lisska came and went that autumn, with Papa's letters to the lion-woman tied on her leg. When she was at the caves she lay on the warm ledge by the fire, where she always lay, and at night she went down to the back and lay against the little red one and hummed.

It was the ledges' hum. Wren had heard it at Varrhold, the whole cliff of them doing it. Low, under everything, more in the floor than in the air. Lisska did it small, just for one. Wren lay in her blanket and felt it come up through the stone into her back.

"What does it mean?" she asked Papa once, when he came up. "The humming."

He asked Lisska, in his head. Wren watched him listen.

"She says it means *here,*" said Papa. "*You're here.*"

"Say it twice."

"*Here,*" said Papa. "*You're here.*"

"Does the little red one know she's here?"

Papa looked down the cave to where the green one lay against the red one in the dark.

"No," he said.

---

Leif taught her the rest of her letters when Ada was at the hold.

Leif was Gunna's tally-boy. He kept the count of everything at the caves: how much mutton came up from the hold and how much the winterers ate of it, how many hens, how many eggs, how many sacks of peat, how many days since Gunna's brother's boy at Skarrow had promised to send up the new girths and hadn't. He kept it on a board with a nail, in strokes. Four strokes, and then a fifth across the four, to make a bundle.

"Like a gate," said Wren, the first time she saw it.

Leif looked at it.

"It's a five," he said.

"It's a gate. Four posts and a bar."

"Gates have more posts than that."

"Not little ones," said Wren. "Not the one into the top field."

Leif had a young brown dragon, the lightest on the ledges, who snored, and big red hands, and he could write as straight as Ada, which Wren hadn't known boys could. He showed her the letters with a nail on a slate, because paper was dear, and she did them after him, over and over, in the warm by the fire, while his brown snored and Old Grey stood just outside the light with his head in and the little red one lay by the spring.

By the dark of the moon in the middle of winter she could write anything.

She wrote a letter to Papa. It took her all one afternoon, because she wanted the letters to be all the same size and in lines as straight as Ada's, and the slate was small. She scratched it with the nail, slowly, with her tongue between her teeth. She wrote about Old Grey. She wrote about the little red one, who'd stopped lying down.

That had happened in the deep of the winter, without anybody seeing it start. One morning she'd been lying by the spring, and the next morning she was standing. On all four feet. Head level. Wings folded. Eyes open, all the way open now, and gold, a dark gold like Kharrak's but darker, and nobody in them at all. She stood all day. She stood all night.

"Like a horse asleep," Gunna said. "Only she isn't asleep."

Wren wrote that the little red one stood at the back by the water and didn't look at anything. Then she sat for a while with the nail in her hand.

Then she wrote: I AM GOING TO SIT WITH HER.

She hadn't known she was going to write it until she wrote it. Then it was on the slate in capitals, and it was true.

She wrote COME FOR MIDWINTER at the bottom. Then she wrote it again under itself, in case. Leif wrapped the slate in a cloth and took it down to the hold at noon the next day on his brown, and said Papa had stood in the yard holding it and a man with a barrow had stopped to look at him.

"Did he read it himself?"

"He read it," said Leif. "Standing there."

"By himself?"

"I didn't help."

"Did he say yes?"

"He said yes," said Leif. "Twice. He said you'd want it twice."

---

She sat with the little red one the next morning.

She'd thought about where. You didn't sit right up close to a beast that was empty. She'd learned that on the gate. If you were right up close you were too big, and you filled up the place where they might look. You sat a little way off. An arm's length. Where they could find you if they wanted and not have to have you if they didn't.

There were loose bowls along the walls. Not the great hollows worn into the floor in rows, that you could lie down in; smaller ones, shallow, cut out of a single stone, that had come up out of their places in the floor in some winter nobody remembered and been pushed to the sides. Gunna said the Clan had used them once for carrying eggs to the warmest place and back. Wren dragged one down the cave to the spring. It made a noise on the floor like a cart with a stone in its wheel, and the winterers lifted their heads, and Gunna by the fire said, "What are you doing?" and Wren said, "Sitting," and kept dragging.

She turned it over with her whole weight. It went over with a bang that echoed up into the roof.

The little red one didn't look round.

Wren sat down on it. It was warm from the floor. She put her hands in her lap.

"I'm Wren," she said. "I'm Calder's. I'm Wren."

---

She didn't know what to say after that. She hadn't known with Old Grey either, at first. She'd said his name, and her name, and then she'd told him about the river, because the river was there.

So she told the little red one about the caves, because the caves were there.

About the floor, and the kettle humming in it. About the hens, who lived in the bowl nearest the mouth and got out every morning and had to be brought back, and the speckled one who laid in Gunna's boots. About the drip in the roof by the third row. About Tamsin, who was very old, three hundred and twelve, Gunna said, and slow, and whose wing had a black edge to it from the night at the Roosts, and who liked her nose rubbed above the nostrils and wouldn't say so. About Leif's brown, who snored.

She talked until her voice went thin. The little red one stood by the spring with her head level and looked at nothing, and the warm water went past her feet, and Old Grey stood behind Wren with his great head lowered over her bowl, breathing on her hair.

When she got up to go and help Gunna with the mutton her legs had gone to sleep.

"Well?" said Gunna.

"She didn't do anything."

"No," said Gunna. She gave Wren the knife. "Not today."

---

Papa came for midwinter.

She saw him from the cave mouth, a long way off down the white slope, walking. Ada behind him on Mabbin with a basket. Kharrak had come on ahead and was lying on the rock above the caves in the snow, red against all that white, and Wren had known from the way he was lying, with his chin on his forefeet and his eyes on the path, that Papa was walking up the long way, and why.

She came out of the dark past Old Grey and ran.

Down through the snow. It was up to her knees in the hollows and she fell twice and got up and ran. She heard Old Grey turn round behind her, slowly, and come after.

"Papa," she said, into his coat. His coat was cold and smelled of the moor. "Papa."

"I got your letter."

"Did you read it?"

"I read it," said Papa. "By myself. All of it."

She pulled back and looked at him. He looked the same. He looked a bit thinner. He looked as if he'd been standing in the cold waiting for her to say something.

"You read it by yourself?"

"Straight through."

"Say it twice."

"Straight through," said Papa. "Straight through."

Old Grey had come down the slope behind her and stopped a pace off. She felt him put his head over her shoulder. She put her hand up without looking and held his nose, which was soft and whiskery and warm, and Papa looked at Old Grey over her head and didn't say anything at all.

---

He saw the little red one that afternoon. Wren watched him see her. He stopped at the edge of the light from the cave mouth and stood there, and Gunna came and stood beside him and said the thing about the horse asleep, quietly, and Papa said something about a roof.

Wren went past them both with Old Grey and sat on her bowl.

She didn't look round. She knew Papa was there, at the edge of the light. She talked to the little red one about the hold, and the dogs, and the snow, and Lisska, who had gone west three nights ago with a letter Ada wouldn't help with, and the lion-woman, who wouldn't come in. She talked about Papa reading her letter straight through by himself. She talked about Old Grey, who'd been like this too, and wasn't now.

When she'd done she went back up the cave, and Papa was sitting on the edge of a hollow by the fire, and she sat down beside him and put her feet in the bowl.

"The roof," she said. "Tell me about the roof."

He looked at her.

"You said to Gunna. A roof."

So he told her. He told it plainer than he'd told Gunna, and left things out, she could hear the places where he left them out, like steps missing on a stair. A long grey roof in a city full of towers, with frost on it. A lake that had done something odd. The little red one standing on the very top of the roof with her four feet together, shaking all over, and not knowing where she was.

"And you went along to her?"

"On my hands and knees," said Papa. "Along the top. It was a long way down both sides."

"Were you frightened?"

"Yes."

"What did you say to her?"

Papa rubbed his face.

"I told her about barley," he said.

Wren stared at him.

"I couldn't think of anything else," said Papa. "She was shaking. I said it's a roof, it's all right, and then I didn't know what to say, so I told her about barley. I told her she'd like it. And then I told her she couldn't eat it." He looked at the fire. "I told a dragon on a roof in Vael she couldn't eat barley. In the frost. With the whole city underneath."

Wren laughed.

She laughed so much she had to hold on to his arm. She laughed until Gunna looked round from the fire, and Leif's brown stopped snoring, and Ada, who was unpacking the basket, said, "What's he said now?" and Papa said, "Barley," and Ada said, "Oh, Lord, not barley," as if she'd heard it all her life, which she had.

"What else?" said Wren, when she could.

"The top field. How it goes gold." He turned his hands over. "How the wind goes over it and it all goes one way. Like water."

"And what did she do?"

Papa was quiet a moment.

"She stopped shaking," he said. "And she lay down. Lisska came and lay on the other side of her, and she folded her legs up and lay down on the roof between us, and looked at the city."

Wren had stopped laughing.

She sat on the edge of the hollow with her feet in it and looked down the cave at the little red one standing by the spring. She thought about a roof, and frost, and a long way down both sides, and Papa on his hands and knees going along the top, talking about barley because he couldn't think of anything else. And the little red one lying down.

She stored it.

---

That night Ada sang the song by the fire, though she said Wren was too old for it, and Wren said, "Sing it twice," and was asleep before the second time. She woke once in the dark, warm, with Ada's shawl over her and Papa's knee under her cheek, and heard Papa and Ada talking low about six of something. Old Grey breathing beyond the firelight. The spring running away down its channel.

And at the back, the little red one, not making any sound.

---

After midwinter Papa went down to the hold, and the winter closed in again, and Wren sat.

She didn't sit every day. She wanted to; she'd written it on the slate; but there was the mutton, and the hens, and the peat to bring in from the stack under the overhang, and her letters, and the days when Gunna's arm was bad from the old burn and Wren had to do the girths. There were days when the wind came off the moor so hard it got into the caves even with the hangings across the mouth, and everybody sat in the one bowl by the fire with Tamsin's wing over them like a roof. There were days she forgot until it was dark, and then sat anyway, in the dark, on her bowl, an arm's length off, talking low so as not to wake Leif.

The little red one stood. Wren talked.

She talked about everything she could think of except the barley. She didn't know why she kept the barley back. She thought about it, lying in her blanket at night, and the nearest she could get was that it was Papa's. It was the thing Papa had said on the roof, and it had worked, once, and if she said it now, on an ordinary day with the hens getting out, it might get used up.

So she talked about the hens.

---

Lisska came home before the full moon, thin and tired, with a letter from the lion-woman on her leg that Papa took down to the hold to read with Ada. Lisska came up to the caves and lay on the warm ledge and said things twice back to Wren through Papa until even Papa was tired of it.

She went down to the back at night and hummed to the little red one. *Here. You're here.*

At the full moon Ada came up and Wren asked her to sing, and Ada said her throat was sore. She said it looking at the fire. Wren looked at her, and didn't ask again that night.

Then Papa came up to say he'd be gone a fortnight, south, with Gunna and Tamsin and six of the ledges, to a place called Blackwater, where there were three more.

"Three more what?"

Papa looked down the cave.

"Like her," he said.

Wren looked too.

"Will they be like her when they come?"

"I don't know," said Papa. "I don't know yet. I've to get them first."

Gunna went with him on Tamsin, slow and sure, with a pile of old sheets she'd been cutting into squares all week and wouldn't say what for. Leif stayed. Leif was cross about it for the whole fortnight. Lisska went west again at the end of it with another letter, from Blackwater, and didn't come back up to the caves first, and the warm ledge by the fire was empty, and nobody said things twice back to Wren.

---

They brought them up on the three-hundred-and-sixth day.

Leif saw them first, at dusk the day before, from the rock above the mouth, and shouted. Wren went up the scree and stood beside him in the wind. Three dots and two more, far off, coming down onto the edge of the moor, and stopping there for the night. In the morning they came on.

Old Brede's blue in front, low over the moor, slow. Two more of the ledges behind. And between them, flying low and heavy, because Brede had brought them a few leagues at a time all the way up the coast, like lame heifers walked home from the Cross, two young dragons. A black. A red, bigger than the little red one, nearly as big as the black.

They came down the slope to the caves in the late morning. Brede got down off his blue and said something to Gunna that Wren didn't hear, and Gunna, who'd come back two days before smelling of vinegar, with her face set, nodded and didn't answer.

Papa had said three. Wren counted them again, in case. Two.

The black and the red walked in past the hatching bowls.

They didn't look at anything. Not at the hens, not at the fire, not at the winterers lifting their old heads at the back. They walked down the cave between the rows, side by side, slow, and stopped by the spring, next to the little red one. And stood.

Three of them, in a row. By the water. Standing.

Wren stood at the edge of the light and looked at them for a while.

Then she went to the wall for the bowls.

---

She dragged them down one at a time.

They were heavy. She did it with her whole weight, leaning back, her heels scraping, the bowl coming after her in jerks with that noise like a cart with a stone in its wheel. The winterers lifted their heads. Brede, at the fire with a cup in his hand, half got up, and Gunna put her hand on his arm and he sat down again.

She set them by the spring, a little apart. An arm's length from each. She turned the two new ones over with a bang each, and the roof gave the bang back, and none of the three by the water looked round.

Then she went up the cave to the fire.

"I've made a rule," she said.

Gunna looked at her. Brede looked at her. Leif looked at her with his mouth open, because she'd said it so loud.

"An hour each," said Wren. "Morning and afternoon. The little red one first, because she was here first. Then the black. Then the red. Every day. And nobody's to come and sit with me, because then they'll have to look at more people, and that's too many." She thought. "Old Grey can. Because he's always there anyway. So it isn't more."

"That's the rule?" said Gunna.

"That's the rule."

Gunna took her pipe out of her mouth and looked at the three by the spring, and then at Wren, a long look, with the burned side of her face toward the fire and the other side in the dark.

"How will you know an hour?" she said.

Wren hadn't thought.

It was Leif who did it. He took a tallow candle out of the box by the peat, a long one, and a nail, and held the candle up against the burned stub of another to see how much went in an hour, which he said he knew from the lambing, and scratched six rings round it with the nail, all the same distance apart.

"One ring's an hour," he said. "Light it when you start. When it burns down to a ring, that's one."

"Six rings."

"Six hours. Three in the morning. Three in the afternoon." He gave it her. "One candle a day."

She took it. Then she took the slate off the shelf where she kept her letters, and the nail from him, and drew one stroke on the slate, down the side, small, by itself.

"What's that?" said Brede.

"Day one," said Wren.

---

She lit the candle on the end of the little red one's bowl and sat down.

She'd thought about what to say all the time she was dragging the bowls. She'd thought about it while the black and the red walked in. Three of them now. Three was a lot to say things to. You couldn't say the hens to three. You couldn't say the drip in the roof.

She sat on her bowl with her hands in her lap. The little red one stood a little higher than her head, a dark shape against the steam, her eyes open, gold, empty, the light of the candle in them and nobody else. There was a broken place over one eye where a horn should have been, a little stub of it, jagged, as if it had been knocked off on something. Wren knew it now as well as she knew the drip in the roof. There was a ring round her neck, too, a band all the way round, where the scales had gone dull. Where the collar had been. Like Old Grey's ring of white hair. On the rest of her the candlelight lay red, and on the ring it didn't lie at all.

"Papa says you'll like barley," said Wren.

The little red one stood by the spring.

"He told me," said Wren. "He said he told you on a roof. In a city. In the frost." She folded her hands. "So I'm telling you too, so you know he wasn't making it up."

The water went past.

"It's no use to you," said Wren. "You can't eat it. He said that too. But it goes gold at the end of the summer. Our top field. It goes all gold. And when the wind comes over it, it goes all one way." She looked at the little red one's face. "Like water. Like the tarn, when the wind's on it. Only gold."

Behind her Old Grey breathed out, long, through his nose, and the warm of it came down on her hair.

The candle burned down to the first ring. Wren went along to the black's bowl, and moved the candle, and sat down, and told the black about the barley.

---

On the second day she told them about the top field, all of it. Where it was. How you went up the lane from the yard, past the muck heap, past the place where the lane was always wet, to the gate with the bar that stuck. How the hill went up behind it to the sky. How in the spring Papa went up and down it behind the plough, and the rooks came after them, and in the summer it was green, and then it was yellow, and then for about a week at the end it was gold.

"Not yellow," she said to the red. "People say yellow. It's gold. You'll see."

The red stood by the spring. It was a darker red than the little red one, almost brown, and bigger, and it stood with its shoulder against the black's shoulder, touching, the only thing in the cave that touched anything. Wren didn't think they knew they were doing it.

Leif came down the cave with the mutton at noon and stood behind her bowl, a good way back.

"You've said barley forty times this morning," he said.

"Forty-one," said Wren, without turning round.

"Gunna says it's a long word for a dragon."

"It's two," said Wren. "*Bar. Ley.*" She considered. "Papa says it's the right one."

She put a second stroke on the slate that night, beside the first.

---

On the third day Ada came up with the bread.

It was the dark of the moon. Ada came up on Mabbin, slowly, with Leif walking at Mabbin's head, and sat by the fire with her shawl and her basket and watched Wren on her bowl at the back with the candle on the end of it. She didn't come down. Wren had told her the rule.

At night by the fire Wren said, "Sing it."

Ada looked at the fire.

"My throat's sore, pet," she said.

"It was sore at the full moon."

"It's sore again."

Wren looked at her. Ada didn't look back. She looked at the fire, with her hands in her lap, and her face was as still as the little red one's. Then she reached out without looking and pulled Wren's plait, gently, as she used to when Wren was small and wouldn't sleep.

"Say it twice," said Wren.

"My throat's sore," said Ada. "My throat's sore." And then, quieter, to the fire: "Your father's got it into his head that the song means something. And I've got it into mine he's right."

Wren didn't know what that meant. She stored it anyway. Ada went down to the hold in the morning on Mabbin, and Wren gave her a message for Pip about the speckled hen, and three strokes went on the slate in a row.

---

On the fourth day she was cross.

She didn't know why. She woke up cross, and the hens had got out, and the speckled one had laid in Gunna's boot again and Gunna had put her foot in it, and her bowl was cold on the top where she sat because the warm only came up through the bottom, and the candle wouldn't light. She sat with the little red one and told her about the barley in a voice that came out flat, like Aunt Bel's when she was counting spoons, and halfway through she stopped.

"You could look," she said.

The little red one stood.

"You could just look. Once. You don't have to do anything else." She heard her own voice go up. "Old Grey looked. He was much worse than you. He'd done something much worse than anything you've done, and he looked, and you haven't done anything, you just stand there, you just *stand* there, and you could *look.*"

Her voice went up into the roof and came back down.

Old Grey had lifted his head behind her. She felt it go up, away from her hair, and the cold come in where his breath had been.

Wren sat very still on her bowl.

Then she got up, and went round behind, and put her hand up flat on Old Grey's nose, where it was soft, and left it there.

"I didn't mean you," she said. "I didn't mean it about you."

After a while his head came down again.

She sat on the bowl. She said the barley again, properly, slowly, the whole of it, the top field and the gold and the wind and the water. She said it to the black and to the red. She didn't get up until the candle had gone down three rings.

That night she lay in her blanket by the fire and thought about what she'd said, and Aunt Bel at the hold with Pip and Nan, who wouldn't come up because of Old Grey. She thought she'd ask Papa about it. Then she thought she wouldn't.

Four strokes on the slate, all the same height, like posts.

---

On the fifth day she drew the bar across them.

She sat on the edge of her blanket in the morning before anybody was up, with the slate on her knees and the nail, and drew it slowly, from the top of the first post to the bottom of the fourth, a long slant across them. A gate.

She looked at it. One gate. Five days.

Then she looked down the cave in the grey light from the hangings. The three by the spring. The little red one, the black, the red. Standing in a row with the steam coming up round their legs. They'd been standing all night. They'd stand all day.

Gunna was awake. Gunna was always awake before anybody. She was sitting by the dead fire with Tamsin's long grey-green head in her lap, rubbing the old dragon's nose above the nostrils where she liked it, and she'd seen Wren draw the bar.

"A gate," said Gunna.

"Leif says it's a five."

"Leif would." Gunna looked at the three. "Your father said you sat with that grey one the whole of a winter."

"Not the whole. From after the snow came."

"How many days?"

Wren thought about it. Nobody had kept a tally, then. There hadn't been a Leif.

"Lots," she said.

"Lots," said Gunna. She nodded, as if that was a number. "And that's one gate."

"I know."

"I'm only saying it." Gunna's hand went on rubbing. "There's no knowing, with them. Some come out again. Some don't. Some come out after a gate and some after a hundred gates and some never, and nobody's ever found out why, and the ones who said they knew were lying." She looked at Wren with the side of her face that hadn't been burned. "You know that."

"I know."

"Good," said Gunna. "Light your candle, then."

---

On the sixth day it snowed, a late wet snow that came in at the cave mouth under the hangings and made the floor by the fire slippery, and Wren did the barley with a cold in her nose, and sneezed in the middle of the gold, and said, "Sorry," to the little red one, and went on.

On the seventh day the snow was going off the moor. You could hear it going. Water in every gully, and the stream in the channel running high and brown at the mouth where the meltwater came in off the slope, and the tarn below loud against its stones. Leif came in at noon with a fistful of something.

"First green," he said. "By the tarn. Under the heather."

He held it out to her. It was a bit of new grass, short and very bright, with the roots on and a crumb of black earth.

Wren took it down to the little red one's bowl and held it up, flat on her palm, an arm's length off.

"That's what it looks like before," she said. "Before it's barley. Barley's grass at the start. Papa says." She looked at it. "It's green for ages. You have to wait."

The little red one stood by the spring. The steam came up past the grass on Wren's palm and made it wet.

Wren put it on the end of the bowl, next to the candle, and left it there.

---

On the seventh night she lay awake.

She didn't mean to. She lay in her blanket by the fire with her eyes open and listened to the caves. Leif's brown snoring. Old Grey shifting his great feet on the stone just outside the light. Gunna breathing. The winterers at the back, so quiet you forgot them, and then one of them sighed, a long hot sigh that came all the way up the cave. The spring.

She thought about the roof.

She'd thought about it so often now it was like a place she'd been. Frost on the slates. A long way down both sides. The little red one at the very top, shaking all over. And Papa going along the ridge on his hands and knees, slowly, with the cold coming through his breeches, and stopping an arm's length off.

He hadn't touched her. He'd said that. He hadn't touched her, and he'd told her about barley, and she'd lain down.

But Lisska had been there.

Lisska had been there, on the other side, lying down, humming. *Here. You're here.* And there'd been the lake doing something odd, and the frost, and the whole city. Too many things. Maybe it wasn't the barley at all. Maybe it was Lisska, or the frost, or just that a roof was a frightening place to stand and lying down was safer. Maybe Papa had only thought it was the barley because it was what he'd said.

Wren lay and looked at the roof of the cave, which she couldn't see.

Then she thought: it doesn't matter. It's still what I've got.

She turned over with her back to the fire and went to sleep.

Seven strokes on the slate. One gate and two posts.

---

Papa came home on the eighth day.

She heard Kharrak, of course. He came over the edge of the moor in the middle of the afternoon and down on the rock outside so hard the hens went up in a panic and two of them got out under the hangings, and Gunna said the thing again that Wren wasn't supposed to hear. Then the hangings moved, and the light came in, and Papa's shape stood in it.

He didn't come in.

Wren was on the black's bowl, with the candle at the second ring of the afternoon. She heard him stop in the cave mouth. She heard Gunna get up and go to him, and say something low. She heard him not move.

She didn't turn round.

She knew what he was doing. He was standing in the light at the mouth where she couldn't see him unless she looked, because if he came and sat by her she'd talk to him instead. She'd want to tell him about the gate on the slate and the grass and the hens getting out and Ada's throat. She'd want to ask about the place he'd been, with the baskets, and whether the old one was better. She'd talk to him and not to them. He knew that. He'd thought about it, all the way home on Kharrak. She could tell from how quiet he was being.

She was glad. She was cross, too, a bit, because she wanted to run to him, and she couldn't, because it was her rule.

She looked at the black instead.

"He used to be like you," she said. Old Grey was standing behind her bowl with his head lowered over it, and she put her hand up and touched his jaw without looking, so the black would know who she meant. "He used to stand like that. Not looking. And then one day he looked. Papa says it was because of my voice." She thought about it. She'd thought about it a lot, lying awake. "I think it was because he got bored of not looking."

The black stood by the spring and looked at nothing.

At the cave mouth, very quietly, Papa laughed. Just once, through his nose. She heard it.

She didn't turn round.

---

He was still there at the end of the afternoon, when the candle reached the sixth ring and went out. He'd sat down in the mouth by then, on the rock, with his back against the wall and the light behind him. She went up the cave and he held his arms out without getting up and she went into them and put her face in his coat. It smelled of salt and Kharrak and a long way off.

"Eight," she said. "Eight days."

"I know," said Papa. "Gunna said."

"I've got a slate. With a gate on it."

"Show me."

She showed him. He looked at the gate and the three posts after it for a long time, as if it were a whole page.

"You've made a rule," he said.

"Did Gunna tell you?"

"She said you told everybody. The first day. Very loud."

"It's a good rule."

"It's a very good rule," said Papa.

He didn't ask her if anything had happened. She saw him not ask. He looked down the cave at the three by the spring, and then at her, and then he put the slate back in her hands and said Kharrak wanted feeding, and went out.

Eight strokes.

---

The ninth day was ordinary.

That was the thing she remembered afterwards, more than anything. How ordinary. The hens got out. The speckled one laid in the hearth ash and the egg was grey. Leif dropped a whole sack of peat on his own foot and said a word he'd learned from Brede. Papa was at the mouth all morning, on the rock, with Kharrak lying in the heather outside with his head on his forefeet, and Gunna took Papa a cup of something hot and they talked low, about the coast and a reef and the old one, and Wren sat at the back and did her rule and didn't listen.

She did the barley in the morning to all three. She'd got so she didn't need to think about it. It came out of her in the same order every time, like a song. The lane, the muck heap, the wet place, the gate with the bar that stuck. The hill going up to the sky. Green, and yellow, and then gold. The wind.

In the afternoon the light came in low.

It came in at the end of the winter like that, at that hour, under the edge of the hangings Gunna had tied back at the side, a long gold bar of it lying all the way down the cave between the rows of bowls. It didn't reach the back. It stopped a few paces short of the spring, so the three stood in the dark beyond it with the steam going up gold where it crossed the edge of the light.

Wren was on the first bowl. The candle was at the first ring of the afternoon. The little red one stood by the spring with her head level.

Wren had been talking for a while. She didn't remember starting. She was at the end of it, where the wind came.

"...and it goes all one way," she said. "Like water. Papa says. All one way."

The little red one lifted her head.

---

Wren saw it.

Not toward her. Not at first. Just up. Off the level, where it had been all winter. Up, as Mabbin's head came up in the paddock when there was a fox in the far hedge that nobody else had heard.

Then it turned.

Slowly. On her neck. Away from the nothing she'd been looking at since the autumn. Round. Toward the bowl. Toward Wren.

And she looked at her.

Wren didn't breathe. The little red one's eyes were dark gold, darker than Kharrak's, with the steam behind them and the edge of the light on one side of her face. They'd been open since the deep of the winter. They'd been open all the time. There'd been the sky in them, like the hold's windows in the day.

There was somebody in the room.

---

The little red one made a sound.

Small. High. A sort of chirr, in her throat, that started low and went up and turned over at the top, like Bel's kettle just before it starts to sing, when you can't tell yet if it's going to. Wren knew it. She'd heard it a hundred times, from the warm ledge by the fire at night, when Lisska was half asleep with her chin on her forefeet and didn't know anybody was listening.

She'd never heard any other dragon make it.

There was something in it. A turn. She heard it go over at the top. Something nearly a word. Like the wind in the hangings at night, that was sometimes nearly somebody saying your name.

Wren went very still on her bowl.

She didn't run. She'd run, with Old Grey. She'd been off the gate and up the lane shouting before he'd even finished turning his head, and she'd been sorry after, because she'd wanted to see what he did next and she'd missed it. She didn't run now. She didn't move her hands out of her lap. She didn't look round.

"She said something," she said. Not loud. "Papa. She said something."

"I heard," said Papa.

From the cave mouth, thirty paces off, in the light. His voice didn't come out properly. It came out like somebody else's.

Wren didn't touch her. She didn't go any closer. She sat on her bowl with her hands in her lap and the candle burning on the end of it, and the little red one's eyes on her, and she said it again. The same thing. The same words, slowly, so they'd be the same.

"It goes all one way," she said. "Like water."

The little red one made the sound again.

---

Nine strokes on the slate. She did the ninth one very carefully, that night, by the fire, with everybody watching her do it and nobody saying anything. A gate and four posts.

Leif looked at it over her shoulder.

"Nine," he said.

"I know it's nine."

"I'm only saying," said Leif. He was grinning so much his ears had gone red.

Papa was across the fire. He'd sat there all evening and eaten nothing, and every so often he looked down the cave into the dark at the back, and every time he looked Wren looked too. The little red one was standing by the spring. She was standing as she'd stood all winter. But her head wasn't level. It was up, a little, and turned, a little, toward the fire.

"Will she still be there in the morning?" said Wren. "Looking?"

Papa looked at Gunna. Gunna took her pipe out of her mouth.

"No knowing," said Gunna.

Wren slept with the slate under her blanket.

---

She was still there in the morning.

Not all of her. Wren could see that. She was there as a drover is there in the hold kitchen door, in out of the cold with his coat still on and the snow on his shoulders, not sure yet if he's stopping. Her eyes went from Wren to the candle to the spring and back to Wren. Then away, to nothing. Then back.

Wren did the rule. All three. The black and the red stood by the spring and didn't lift their heads, and Wren did their hours just the same, the barley and the gold and the wind, and when she was on their bowls she felt the little red one's eyes on the side of her face, coming and going.

In the afternoon she brought a strip of mutton down from the fire.

Nobody told her to. All winter Gunna had put the mutton down on the floor under the little red one's nose, and she'd eaten it without looking, when it was there. Wren sat on her bowl and held the strip out, flat on her palm, as Papa held out a crust to the greys over the gate. An arm's length off. She didn't put it any closer.

The little red one looked at it.

She looked at it for a long time. Wren's arm began to ache at the shoulder. The mutton was cold and greasy on her palm, and the steam made it wet. She didn't move.

Then the little red one put her head down, slowly, and took it.

With her lips. Very carefully. Not with her teeth at all. Wren felt the lips on her palm, warm and dry and rough, like Mabbin's taking an apple, and then the mutton was gone and her hand was empty.

She didn't move until it was gone. Then she put her hand back in her lap and held it there with her other hand.

"That's ten," she said. Not to anybody. "That's ten days."

---

On the eleventh day the little red one lay down.

Wren was at the fire at dusk. She'd done the six rings and the candle was out, and she'd come up the cave and sat down by Papa, and she was telling him about the speckled hen when Gunna said, "Look."

Down by the spring, in the dark, the little red one was folding her legs.

All winter she'd stood. All night, every night, like a horse asleep and not asleep. Now she folded her legs under her, slowly, front first, then back, as if she had to remember how, and lay down on the warm stone by the water, and put her chin on her forefeet. As Lisska lay. As she'd lain on the roof in the frost between Papa and Lisska, looking at the city.

She didn't shut her eyes. They were on Wren.

Wren got up and walked across the cave to fetch her blanket, though she didn't need it. The little red one's eyes went with her. Wren walked back. They came back. Wren went to the wall where the hens' bowl was and stood there, and the gold eyes were there in the dark by the spring, on her, the whole length of the cave.

"That one's come back," said Gunna.

She said it as she'd said the other thing, in the autumn. Plain, like news of a ewe. Then she put her pipe back in her mouth and looked at the fire, and Wren saw her hand on Tamsin's neck had gone tight.

---

Wren sat down at the fire in the cave mouth.

Papa was beside her. Leif across, with his brown. Gunna with Tamsin's head lifted off her lap and her hand still on it. Old Grey just outside the light, his head in. Kharrak in the heather outside, a darker shape in the dark, his eyes red.

At the back, by the spring, the little red one lay with her eyes open, on Wren.

Wren had been thinking about it all day. She hadn't known she was thinking about it. It had been there under the barley and the rule and the mutton, like the kettle in the floor. And now it came up.

"Ildra," she said.

Papa looked at her.

"Her name," said Wren. "It's Ildra."

"Why?" said Papa.

Wren thought about it. She looked at the fire and frowned. She didn't know why. She tried to find it, and it wasn't anywhere she could find it, and she decided she didn't mind.

"It's what she sounds like," she said. "When she makes the noise. The kettle noise. It goes *ildra.*" She looked up at him. "She said it. On the ninth day. I heard it."

Papa didn't say anything for a moment. She could see him trying to hear it again, in his head, the sound from the ninth day, and hearing only a kettle.

"Ildra," he said.

"Say it twice."

"Ildra," said Papa. "Ildra."

At the back of the cave, by the spring, in the dark, the little red one lifted her head.
