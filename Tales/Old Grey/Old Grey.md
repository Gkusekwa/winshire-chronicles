---

In the Marches they say a good grey knows the way home in the dark, and that a man who has a good grey never needs to be sober on a market day. They say it because it is true. A dray-beast learns a road the way water learns a hill, by going down it, and once he has learned it the road is part of him, the way his hooves are, and he can no more lose it than lose his feet.

Old Grey knew four roads. The lane down to the river. The lane up to the top field. The track over the hill to Harrow's End, where the mill was, and the smell of the mill came out to meet you a long way before you got there. And the long road south along the Wend to Cotter's Cross, which he had walked once a year since he was three, in harness with Mabbin, pulling Joss's wool.

He knew them by their weights. The river lane went down, and the weight of the cart came onto his breeching and leaned on his quarters all the way. The top-field lane went up, and the weight was in his shoulders. The Harrow's End track was level at the top, where the wind came, and smelled of thyme. The south road was long and flat and went on until there was nothing to do but walk.

He was old now. He knew that too, the way he knew the roads. The white had come into his muzzle, and his back had dropped a little behind the withers, and in the mornings when there was frost his near hind was stiff until he had walked it out. He didn't mind. He was the biggest beast at Upper Rush and had been since he was four, and there was nothing at Upper Rush, or on any of his four roads, that he had to be afraid of.

---

He had a left side and a right side.

His right side was the world. Hedges, gates, Joss walking at his head, the dog, weather. His left side was Mabbin.

They had been yoked together eleven years. She went on the left. When he stood in the paddock she stood on his left, a body's width off, and when he lay down in the long grass in the evening she lay down on his left, and if he woke in the night the first thing he knew was the warm of her there, and the smell of her, grey mare, oat-sweat, the tar on the gall on her shoulder that Joss painted every spring.

When the yearling had come, Sorrel, the yearling had gone on Mabbin's other side. So it went: Sorrel, Mabbin, him, and then the world.

---

His people he knew by smell first and by voice after.

Joss smelled of hoof-oil and onions and the pipe he carried in his top pocket and never lit, because Bel didn't like it, and which he took out and turned over in his hands when he was thinking. Joss's hands smelled of all the beasts at Upper Rush at once. When Joss came into the paddock in the mornings, Old Grey turned his head before the gate had opened, because the smell came first.

Joss called him *Grey*. That was his sound. Joss said it low, like setting down a full bucket. *Grey. Come on then, Grey.* And Old Grey came on, because the sound meant Joss, and Joss meant the halter, and the halter meant a road, and a road was what he was for.

Bel smelled of bread and lye soap. She said his sound sharper than Joss did, as if she were calling him in from somewhere, though he was never anywhere but in front of her.

The man from the other farm, the one who came by with the crust, smelled of barley dust and other people's dogs. He leaned on the gate and held the crust out flat on his palm and said nothing at all, and that was a thing Old Grey liked in him.

There were small ones. Pip, who smelled of the hayloft, and Nan, who smelled of milk. And the other small one, the girl from the far farm, who came with the crust-man sometimes and stood on the bottom bar of the gate. She smelled of milk too, and river mud. She said everything twice.

*Grey,* she said. *Old Grey. Old Grey,* as if once might not get there.

---

When he was a yearling a gate-hook had caught his left ear and split it.

He didn't remember the hook. He remembered the hands. Two men holding his head in the yard, the barley-dust one and Joss, and something going in and out of the ear, small and hot, over and over, and the smell of his own blood. He had rolled his eyes and stood for it because the hands were holding him and Joss was talking, low, the whole time, and when it was over and they let him go he had shaken his head and sprayed them both, and walked away to the grass, and behind him Joss had laughed until he sat down in the muck.

The ear had healed with a notch in the tip. He could feel the notch when the wind came from the left. It whistled. It was the only part of him that had ever been broken, and it had been broken by a gate, in a yard, at home, with Joss laughing.

That was what he knew about hurt. That it was small, and it was in one place, and then there was grass.

---

It was the end of summer, and the barley was in on the hill farms but not yet at Upper Rush, and the evenings were long and gold, with dust in them.

He was lying down with Mabbin on his left when the dog started.

Not the bark that meant a fox. Not the bark that meant the lane, a cart, the crust-man. A bark that went up at the end and broke. Then nothing. He knew the dog's sounds as he knew his own feet, and he had never heard the dog stop like that, in the middle.

Mabbin was up before him. He felt her go, the warm of her leaving his left side, and the cold that came into the place where she'd been. He got up. His near hind was stiff.

There were men in the yard.

He smelled them before he saw them. Many. A sour smell, of men who had walked a long way and been afraid a long time, and over it something else. Smoke. Not wood-smoke. Sweet. Sweet the way the inside of the hives was when Joss took the comb in autumn, but burnt, and with something under the sweetness that he had no road for at all.

The barn went up.

It went up with a sound like wind coming down a hill all at once. Light came out of it, red, and the heat, and the beasts in it screamed, Hollin's ox-beast and the cows and the dog in its cage on the cart. The light got into everything. It turned the yard into a place he didn't know.

A man came into the paddock with a rope.

He walked straight across the grass to them without hurrying, with a lantern held up in front of him, and he wasn't afraid at all. That was the thing Old Grey noticed. The others smelled of fear. This one smelled of nothing much. Leather. Lamp-oil. A little of the sweet smoke, old, gone into his coat. He walked up to Mabbin and put the rope over her head as Joss did, in one movement, and she let him, because a rope over the head meant a road.

Then he came to Old Grey.

Old Grey put his head up. Out of reach. He was the biggest beast at Upper Rush. A man without a halter had no business with him.

The man looked up at him in the red light. He said something, not loud. It wasn't Joss's sound. It wasn't any sound Old Grey knew. He said it again, the same, and lifted the lantern, and the sweet smell came off the lantern's warm tin, stronger, and went up Old Grey's nose and into his head, and his head came down.

He didn't mean it to. It came down the way it came down for the bit in the morning, when he was half asleep.

The rope went over.

---

They went out of the yard, Mabbin first, then him, and the man walking in front with the lantern, not hurrying, as a man leads stock to market. Behind them the barn fell in. Somewhere in the dark down the paddock, Sorrel was calling.

It was the yearling's call. High. Over and over. *Where. Where. Where.*

Mabbin answered.

She stopped dead in the lane and threw her head up against the rope and called back, the long low call a mare makes across a field, and the man in front stopped too and turned and looked at her. Then he went on, and the rope went taut, and she went on, because she had to. She kept calling. All the way down the lane. Sorrel's voice went thinner behind them and thinner, and then the sound of the fire covered it, and then the river covered the fire, and then there was only the lane and the lantern and the man and the road going down.

The river lane. He knew it. The weight came onto his breeching and leaned on his quarters, as it always did. He walked down it in the dark, roped to a man who smelled of nothing, with Mabbin calling on his left, and every step of it was a step he knew.

That was the worst thing, after. That the road was his. That his own feet took him.

---

They walked south a long time.

He knew the first of it. The Wend lane, and then the south road, the long flat one to Cotter's Cross. He thought for a while they were taking the wool. His body thought it. It settled into the south-road walk, the long one, where there's nothing to do but go on, and his breath came slow and even, and he waited for the smell of the wool-shed at the Cross and Joss's voice saying *There now, Grey*, and the bucket.

There wasn't a wool-shed. There wasn't Joss. They went through the Cross in the dark without stopping and on south, and the road went past the end of everything he knew, and after that it was only road.

There were other beasts. They came out of the dark and joined the line: a hound in a cage on a cart, an ox-beast he didn't know, three goats, a horse that kept trying to lie down. The men walked beside them. They talked low among themselves and didn't use any sounds he knew. When the light came up, grey, they were among trees he had never smelled, and the river on their right was wider than the Wend had ever been, and brown, and smelled of salt.

---

The town smelled of fish and tar and too many people.

He had never been in a place where the ground was all stone. It rang under his hooves. It went on and on, and there were walls on both sides so high that the sky was a strip, and the smell of the river came down the strip from the end, salt and rot and boats. People pushed past. A dog bit at his heel and he didn't kick it, because Joss had taught him not to kick dogs, and the dog went away.

They went into a yard full of pens. It smelled of every beast in the world. He stood in a pen with a rail at his chest and Mabbin in the next pen, close, on his left, where she belonged. She had stopped calling. She stood with her head down. The galls the rope had made on her neck were raw already.

He put his nose through the rails and found her neck, and blew on it, and she leaned on the rail toward him.

They were there two days. A man came with a bucket. Another man came and looked at their teeth and their feet and wrote something. On the second day the man who smelled of nothing came back with other men, in grey coats, and they stood at the rail of Old Grey's pen and talked, and one of them gave the man who smelled of nothing a small bag that clinked, and there was a paper.

Then they opened his pen.

He wouldn't go.

He knew what it was now. He didn't know the words for it, but he knew it, as he knew weather turning. They were taking him off Mabbin's left side. He planted his feet on the stone and put his head down and they couldn't move him. Five men on the rope, and they couldn't. He was the biggest beast at Upper Rush.

One of the grey men went away and came back with a thing that smoked.

The sweet smell. The hive-smell, burnt. They held it under his nose and he threw his head up away from it and they held it up there too, on a pole, and it came into his nose and down into his chest. And the strength went out of his feet the way it does when you lie down in the sun.

He went with them.

Behind him Mabbin called once. He couldn't turn his head to answer. He heard her, on his left side, getting further away, the long low call for a yokemate across a field. Then a wall came between.

---

The room was dark and low, and the sweet smoke was in it like water in a pond.

They brought him in and tied him short to a ring in the wall. There was straw on the floor, old and trodden, and the straw stank of other beasts' fear. Hounds. Something with claws. A beast he had no smell for at all, big. All of them gone now, and the fear left behind in the straw.

A brazier stood in the corner with the sweet stuff on it, smoking. Every breath he took was the smoke.

He stood. He was tired. The smoke was in him now, all of him, and it made him heavy, a good heavy, like the end of a long day in harness when the collar comes off and the stall is warm. He wanted to lie down. His knees wanted to go.

There were two men. A young one, who smelled of sweat and of being afraid, and who stayed by the brazier and fed it. And an older one, small, who didn't smell of being afraid at all. The older one came and stood in front of him and began to talk.

It wasn't talk. It was the same few sounds, over and over. Low, steady, slow. Like rain on a roof. Like the mill-wheel at Harrow's End when you stood close, going round and round so you stopped hearing it and only felt it in your feet.

Then, still talking, the old one put his hand on Old Grey's head.

Flat. Between the eyes, just under the forelock. Where Joss put his, in the mornings, when he came in with the halter.

Old Grey leaned into it. He couldn't help it. It was where the hand went. It meant Joss, and the halter, and a road.

And something came into him through the hand.

---

It is hard to say what a beast's self is, and the old songs don't try. The Keepers of the Wyldmarch say every beast has a stall inside it where it lives, and that a bond is when the beast opens the door and lets you in to stand beside it.

This wasn't a door. Nobody opened it.

It came in through the hand like cold water into a boot. It filled the place where Old Grey was. He felt it fill. It didn't push him; there was no pushing. It was only that there was more of it than there was of him, and it kept coming, and there was nowhere for him to be.

So he went out.

He didn't mean to. A beast in a stall that's filling with water doesn't choose to go out of it. He was standing in himself, and then he was standing beside himself, a little way off, in the corner, as a smaller beast stands when a bigger one comes into the pen, and the stall where he had always been was full of somebody else.

There was nothing in it that smelled of the old man, nothing of leather or lamp-smoke or the small dry body standing there. Only a wanting. A shape. Something put on, like a collar.

The old one stopped talking. The smoke went on.

"There," said the old one. Old Grey heard that from where he stood, outside himself. A sound with no meaning in it. "There. You're chosen now."

By the brazier the young man had turned his face away.

---

The grey stood.

They put a collar on him. It was iron, heavy, and cold, and it had marks on the outside that the grey didn't see because he had no way of seeing his own neck. There were marks on the inside too. The old man put those there himself with a small hammer, tapping, while the grey stood with his head low.

When the collar was on, the old man took his hand away.

The grey stood.

---

There was a while when nothing happened that he kept.

There were roads. He walked them. They didn't go into him, the way roads had. His feet took them, and that was all. He couldn't have walked any of them back.

There were other bound beasts, in a line. Hounds, two greyharts, a horse. They didn't smell of fear any more. They didn't smell of much. When the line stopped they stood, and when the line moved they moved, and none of them put a nose to another or blew or leaned. Each one stood a body's width off the next and there was nothing on its left side and nothing on its right.

There were camps. Tents, grey canvas, and the smell of soup and smoke and many people, and children, and at the back of the camps a fenced place on a slope, and the beast lines. The grey stood in the lines.

There was a handler. The older man, the small one, who didn't smell of being afraid. He put his hand on the grey's neck in the mornings, and the grey moved, and when he took his hand away, the collar held it, and the grey went on moving the way the hand had wanted. The hand was a weight. Not a weight like a cart's, which you lean into and it leans back, and the leaning is something you do together. A weight like snow on a roof. It sat on him and didn't lean back.

He didn't feel the snow. He didn't feel the cold. He didn't feel the harness rub, or the place on his near hind that had been stiff in the mornings. A man struck him with a stick once, for standing in the wrong place, and he didn't feel that either. It happened somewhere a little way off, to a big grey beast he was standing beside.

He pulled a sled. Up from the water, to the tents, and back. The sled had barrels on it. The water-hole was cut in the ice of a beck, and the beck went down to a river, a wide flat white one, frozen. Up and back. Up and back. The weight went on his shoulders on the way up and on his breeching on the way down, and his body leaned into it as a body does, and he wasn't in his body.

He stood where the old man put him.

Outside the stall, in the corner of the pen of himself, something small stood and watched the grey, and didn't know what it was watching.

---

The smell came in the night.

He was in the lines. He was standing. There was straw under his feet and the camp was asleep, and the beck ice was creaking in the cold, and the handler was asleep in the straw at the end of the lines with his coat over him.

The smell came across the snow from the dark.

Hoof-oil. Onions. A pipe that was never lit.

The small thing in the corner of the pen lifted its head.

It didn't know why. It had no road for why. But it lifted its head, as he had lifted it every morning in the paddock before the gate opened, because the smell came first. And it came a little way across the floor of the pen toward the stall that wasn't its own any more, and stood at the door.

A man came out of the dark between the beast lines. Big, slow. He had something in one hand that shone, and over his shoulder a halter. Old Grey knew the halter. The smell of it. The good one, from the nail inside the barn door, the one that came down on market days.

The man came close. He put his free hand up and laid it flat on the grey's face, between the eyes, under the forelock.

"Grey," he said. Low, as he always said it. "Grey. There you are. There you are, then."

The small thing at the door of the stall leaned. Toward the hand. Toward the sound. It had a sound. It had always had a sound.

Then the handler woke.

He woke in the straw at the end of the line without any sound at all, and sat up, and saw, and he didn't shout. He put out his hand toward the grey. He didn't even need to touch him. The collar was there.

The snow came down on the roof.

All of it at once. All the weight that had been sitting there, and more, pushed down, through the collar, into the stall, so hard and so fast that the small thing at the door was thrown back across the pen into its corner and lay there.

And the grey came up.

He didn't feel it. He felt nothing. Somewhere a little way off a great grey beast reared up against the stars, huge, higher than a barn door, with the iron shoes on its forefeet catching the light from a lantern on the snow. And a man stood under it with a halter over his shoulder and his hand still up where the beast's face had been, and didn't move, and didn't move.

And the forefeet came down.

---

The small thing lay in the corner of the pen a long time.

There was a smell that went on after. Onions, and hoof-oil, and something under them, iron and hot. The smell was in the straw. The grey stood in it. They didn't move him off it until the morning.

In the morning men came and lifted something off the snow and put it on a cart, and laid a blanket over it, and took the halter that had been lying beside it, and a knife, and put those on the cart too. The cart went away up the river, the way the beck went down, north.

The grey pulled the sled.

The small thing in the corner of the pen didn't lift its head again for a long time. It had lifted its head once and come to the door, because a smell came first and a sound came after, and this was what had come of it.

---

The night the fire came there was no moon.

He was in the lines. He was standing. There were more tents than before, and more beasts in the fenced place on the slope, and a new pen beside the lines with beasts in it who weren't bound yet: two young wolves, a spotted marsh-cat, a yearling greyhart. They smelled of fear still. They paced. The grey didn't.

Then every beast in the camp that could still be afraid was afraid all at once.

The wolves went flat in their pen. The greyhart threw itself at the rails. Up and down the beast lines the bound ones stood, as they always stood, but the horses at the far end of the camp screamed and screamed, and the people in the tents woke and came out and looked up.

Something came over the hill against the stars.

The grey didn't look up. His head was low. But the smell came down on the camp before anything else did, and it was a smell he had no road for and every road for at once. Hot stone. Iron in a forge. The air before lightning. And under it, bigger than any beast he had ever stood near, so big that the smell had a size, beast. Beast.

Then fire.

It came down on the tents at the back of the camp, the binding tent and the store wagons, in a long roar like the barn at Upper Rush, only longer, and red, and the snow round it went to water in one breath. Men ran. Women ran with children. Nobody came at the beast lines. The thing in the sky didn't put its fire there. It went along the back of the camp, the handlers' tents, the long low tent with the smoke-holes, and left the people's tents and the lines alone, as if it had been told to.

Someone was coming across the snow toward the lines.

The grey couldn't see him, with his head low. He smelled him.

Barley dust. Other people's dogs. Smoke, a great deal of it, and hot stone, and the forge smell, as if the man had come out of the fire. But barley. Under everything. Barley dust, and a hand that held a crust out flat.

In the corner of the pen of himself, the small thing lay where it had been thrown, and didn't lift its head. It had come to the door once.

The handler was beside the grey. He had a lantern on the snow at his feet, and his hand was on the grey's neck, and the weight was in the hand, ready.

The man with the barley dust walked up the lines. He had something in his hands. A halter. The good one. The smell of it. Onions and hoof-oil and the nail inside the barn door.

"Stop there," said the handler.

The barley-dust man stopped.

The handler talked to him. The grey didn't keep it. The weight on the roof grew heavier, slowly, and settled, the way snow settles before it slides. The grey's body set itself. His weight came back onto his quarters, and his forefeet got light on the snow.

The barley-dust man took a step.

The roof came down.

The grey went up, against the fire, enormous, as he had gone up once before against the stars. Somewhere a little way off. To somebody else.

And then the ground jumped.

Something came down on the snow between the handler and the grey. A foot. A single foot, bronze, clawed, as broad as a cart and as heavy as a hill. The beck ice cracked across with a sound like a tree splitting. Snow went up and came down on all of them. The smell of hot stone and iron was so close it was everything.

The handler's hand came off the grey's neck.

He ran.

---

Old Grey came down.

Nobody brought him down. There was nobody holding him up any more. That was all. The weight that had been on the roof was gone, all at once, and with nothing holding him up he came down, slowly, like a sheaf leaning over in the field when the band's cut. His forefeet touched the snow. His head went low and stayed there.

The stall was empty.

He knew that much, from his corner. The somebody-else that had been standing where he used to stand had gone out of it when the hand went. It was empty. Its door was open. He could have gone back.

He didn't.

He didn't know how. He had been outside it a long time, in the corner, and the corner was where he was now, and the way back across the floor was a road, and he had no road. He lay in the corner of the pen of himself and looked at the empty stall and didn't move.

Outside, his body stood in the snow with its head low and its eyes empty, and the barley-dust man stood in front of it with the halter in his hands and the fire behind him, and waited.

Something else came down on the slope behind the barley-dust man. Small, quick, green. It smelled of wind.

The barley-dust man put the halter over Old Grey's head.

The smell of it. The good one. Joss's.

His hands shook, buckling it. Old Grey felt them shake, a little way off. Then the man took the lead and stepped back, and the rope went taut, and Old Grey went with it.

One step. Two.

The way a beast goes with anybody who picks up the rope. There was nobody in the stall to say no.

---

They went north up the river lane, four leagues, in the dark.

He knew it. Not with himself. His feet knew it. It was the river lane, the long one, and it went up, and the weight was in his shoulders all the way, and his body leaned into it as it had leaned a thousand times, and walked.

The barley-dust man walked at his head and didn't speak. Above them something flew, high, quick, green, and sometimes made a small sound like a question.

A league along the lane a bell began to ring.

It was far off, across the ice, on the other bank. Fast. On and on. The kind that gets men out of their beds. It came over the frozen river and up the lane and went on behind them as they walked, ringing, ringing, and in the corner of the pen of himself something heard it, and didn't lift its head.

---

They came up the lane at Upper Rush at dawn.

The smell of home came first. It came down the lane to meet him a long way before the gate: the dung-heap, the barn that wasn't there, the new barn going up in its place, raw timber, and peat-smoke, and bread.

Bread. Lye soap.

A woman stood at the top of the lane with a lantern that had gone out.

And Mabbin.

She was at the paddock gate. He smelled her before anything. Grey mare. Oat-sweat. The tar on the gall on her shoulder. She put her head over the gate and called, the long low call a mare makes across a field for her yokemate. It went down the lane and over him and on.

In the corner of the pen of himself the small thing heard it and shut its eyes.

He didn't lift his head.

The bread-woman came down the lane. She stopped in front of him. She put her hand up, and laid it on his face, flat, between the eyes, under the forelock.

He stood for it.

She said something to the barley-dust man. Then she took the lead out of his hand and walked down the lane to the paddock, and opened the gate, and Old Grey went in after her, because the rope went taut. She took the halter off. She went out and shut the gate.

He stood in the snow in his own paddock.

Mabbin came across the snow and stood on his left side, a body's width off, where she belonged, and put her nose against his neck, and left it there.

He let her.

---

The winter went on a long time. He didn't keep most of it.

He stood in the paddock. When the snow was deep, the bread-woman brought him into the byre at night and he stood in the byre. In the morning she let him out and he stood in the paddock. When she put a bucket in front of him he drank from it, and when she put hay in the rack he ate, slowly, as you eat when you aren't hungry and don't know you aren't. When she put her hand on his face, between the eyes, he stood for it.

Mabbin stood on his left. Every day. All day. She didn't graze much. She stood. When he lay down she lay down, and at night she lay with her back against his side, the whole length of it, as she had never done in the summers when there was no need.

The young mare stood on her other side. So it went: Sorrel, Mabbin, him, and then the world.

He didn't know any of it. Or he knew it somewhere a little way off, in the corner of the pen of himself, like rain heard in sleep.

There was a hill above the farm, and something new on it, a mound of earth with the snow on it, and the bread-woman went up there every day and stood. The smell of onions and hoof-oil had gone from Upper Rush. It had been on the halter, and the halter hung on the nail inside the new barn door, and the smell got fainter there every day, and then it was only leather.

---

The girl came within the week.

She came down from the house by herself in the last of the light, without the barley-dust man. Milk and river mud. She climbed onto the bottom bar of the paddock gate and put her arms over the top bar and her chin on her arms, and looked at him.

"Old Grey," she said. "Old Grey."

He was standing near the gate, as it happened. His head was low. He didn't move it.

"I'm Wren," she said. "Wren. I'm Calder's. You know me. You know me. I came with the crust." She thought about it. "With Papa. With the crust."

He stood.

She stayed a long time, talking. About the river, and the ice, and a lion-woman across the river, and a green thing in the sky who saw everything and talked to her papa in his head and said things twice. She liked that. She said that one twice herself. When the bread-woman called her in for her supper she climbed down and said, "I'll come tomorrow. Tomorrow," and went.

She came the next day. And the next. Every day, in all weathers. She stood on the bottom bar of the gate with her chin on the top bar and talked to him about everything, and said his name over and over, because, she told him, once might not get there.

After a while he was always standing near the gate when she came.

He didn't know why. His body did it. It was something about the end of the day, the light going, and the cold coming up, and the bread-woman calling the hens in. The body went and stood by the gate. That was all. A beast learns a road by going down it.

---

It was very small, what came back first.

Barley.

The bread-woman had been to the mill, and there was barley in the feed, and the smell of it came up out of the bucket into his nose in the byre in the early dark. And in the corner of the pen of himself, the small thing that had shut its eyes opened them.

It didn't lift its head. It only opened its eyes. Barley. Barley dust. A hand held out flat with a crust on it. A gate, and a man leaning on it, saying nothing.

It lay there in the corner with its eyes open and smelled the barley, a long time, until the bucket was empty.

---

Then the girl said his name.

It was a day like the others. The snow was going grey at the edges, and the light was longer, and the ice on the river had been loud in the night. She was on the bottom bar, talking. About the green thing, and a collar somebody had struck off somebody across the river, and the river going out. And in the middle of all of it she said, "Old Grey." And stopped. And then again, because once might not get there: "Old Grey."

The small thing in the corner of the pen lifted its head.

Not far. A hand's breadth. Toward the sound.

And outside, in the paddock in the late light, the great grey head turned a hand's breadth on its neck toward the gate, toward the girl on the bottom bar, and stopped.

The girl went quite still.

Then she was gone. Off the gate, up the lane, running, shouting, her voice going up the lane ahead of her to the house and the hill and the sky, saying it over and over to anybody who'd listen, so that everyone at Upper Rush knew before anybody had asked: he turned his head. He turned his head. He turned his head.

---

The barley-dust man came down from the hill that evening and stood at the gate and looked at him a long time.

He looked into Old Grey's eyes. Old Grey knew that look. Joss had looked at him like that, the spring the near hind had swelled, looking for where the hurt was.

The barley-dust man didn't find anything. Old Grey's eyes were empty still. The small thing in the corner had lifted its head, but it hadn't left the corner. It was a long way from the corner to the eyes.

The bread-woman came down too. She stood at the gate beside the girl and looked at Old Grey. Then she put her hand on the girl's head, on her hair, and left it there.

---

The barley-dust man went away after that and didn't come down again for a long time. The girl stood at the gate every day. She told Old Grey the green thing was on the crag above the farm every night, watching, and that it told her papa everything, and her papa told her. She pointed. He didn't look.

The days got longer. The snow went off the paddock in patches and then all at once. Water ran in every rut. The river roared.

One evening, when the light was long and gold and the paddock smelled of mud and new grass, the bread-woman came down to the gate. She stood and looked at the three of them. Sorrel. Mabbin. Old Grey.

"Grey," she said.

His ear moved.

Only that. The ear, the left one, with the notch, turning toward her voice as it turned for a gate-latch.

She stood and looked at it. Then she put her hand on Mabbin's neck, and on his, one each. She said something to the barley-dust man, who had come down the lane behind her. He said something back. She laughed, a short hard sound, not happy, and took her hands away.

---

They went down the river lane in the dark.

The barley-dust man led. Mabbin first, then Sorrel, then Old Grey, roped head to tail. His feet knew it. The weight came onto his breeching and leaned on his quarters all the way down. He followed the rope. He followed anything.

The bread-woman walked behind with a handcart and the two small ones, and the old woman who smelled of herbs and singing, and the girl. The girl kept running up the line to walk beside Old Grey, and being called back, and coming again. She talked to him in a whisper the whole way, because the barley-dust man said they had to be quiet. Her whisper said everything twice. It was six leagues. The sky was going grey when the lane came out on the flat by the water.

At the bottom of the lane there was the river, and a jetty, and a light on the far side.

The river was loud. He had never been on it. He had walked beside it all his life, along the lane, along the south road. He had never once been on it. It smelled of mud and cold and snowmelt, and it was moving, the whole width of it, faster than he could walk.

A boat came over on a rope.

There was a woman in it. She smelled of tar and river and bread, a different bread. She brought the boat in against the jetty and stood in it with her hand on the rope and talked fast and warm to everybody at once. The people went over first, all of them together, the bread-woman and the small ones and the old woman and the girl, and the girl stood at the rail and waved, up, at the sky. Then the boat came back for Mabbin, who stood at the edge of the planks and blew and wouldn't, until the barley-dust man talked her down. Then Sorrel, who had seen her mother go.

Then Old Grey.

The barley-dust man led him down onto the jetty. The boards rang hollow under him. The boat was lower than the jetty and it moved. He didn't look at it. He didn't look at the water. He followed the rope down off the boards and into the boat as if it were a road, and stood in the middle of it where the barley-dust man put him.

The tar-woman looked at him a long time. At his head. At his eyes. She said a short sound to the barley-dust man, and he said a shorter one back.

It moved under him. It was the strangest thing he had ever stood on. The floor of it went down at one end when he stepped and came up when he stood, and the river was right there, loud, on both sides, cold coming up off it. The woman with the tar smell said something fast and warm. The rope went out across the water and the boat went out along it.

Halfway across, the current took it.

The boat swung. It went round on the rope and leaned and the water came up to the edge on his right side, loud, and everything that had been level was not level.

Old Grey braced his legs.

He didn't mean to. No one told him to. Nobody's hand was on him and there was no weight on any roof. His body did it, as it had done it on the river lane in the mud and on the Harrow's End track in the ice and in the yard the day the gate-hook caught his ear: spread the feet, drop the weight, lean against what's leaning on you. He set himself in the middle of the boat like a post driven into a field.

The boat steadied.

It went on across.

In the corner of the pen of himself, the small thing felt it. The weight, leaning on him. Him, leaning back. The leaning was something done together.

It was the first thing in a long time that had leaned back.

---

On the other side there was stone. A jetty under a wall, and the wall going up a long way, and a great many people, and the smell of a place he didn't know.

He walked off the boat onto the stones as if they were a road. They were a road. His feet said so.

The bread-woman took his rope. The girl came running down the jetty and put her arms round his foreleg, as much of it as she could reach, and held on.

He stood for it.

She smelled of milk and river mud. Her face was pressed against his leg, above the knee, and she was saying something into the hair there, over and over, and the small thing in the corner of the pen had its head up now, and was listening.

---

After that they were in a camp again.

He didn't know it was a camp. He knew it smelled of many people and smoke and soup, and that the paddock had gone, and that he stood where the bread-woman tied him, among strangers, with Mabbin on his left and Sorrel on her other side. There were walls above. Big ones, grey, with a bell on them. When the bell rang the people in the camp looked up.

The bread-woman came and went. The girl wasn't there. She had gone through a gate in the wall with the old woman who smelled of herbs and singing. The girl had told him about that too, before she went. She said she'd be on the other side of the wall, and she'd come back, and come back.

He stood in the camp a few days. He didn't keep them.

Then one morning the smell changed.

It came down the river from the south. Many men. Fear and iron and the sour sweat of men who have walked a long way, and over it, under it, through it, the sweet smell. The hive-smell, burnt. Honey-smoke, a great deal of it, coming up the road with the men, on the wind, into the camp.

And beasts. Beasts that didn't smell of anything at all.

In the corner of the pen of himself, the small thing went flat.

It knew that smell. It had been in a room full of it once, and something had come in through a hand like cold water into a boot, and it had gone out of itself to make room. It pressed itself into the corner and shut its eyes and didn't move.

Outside, the camp came apart.

---

There was noise. Everything at once. The bell on the wall, fast, on and on, the kind that gets men out of their beds. People screaming and running and carrying things and dropping them. Smoke. Not honey-smoke: wood, thatch, the tents going up at the far end. Iron on iron somewhere off down the road, and men shouting, and under the men a sound he knew from the beast lines. Hounds. Bound hounds. Coming up the road, silent, many of them, the sound of their feet and nothing else.

The bread-woman was at his head.

She had all three of them on ropes. Mabbin was wild, up on her hind legs, screaming, and the bread-woman was hanging off her halter with both hands and being lifted off her feet. Sorrel went where her mother went. Old Grey stood between them with his head low.

The bread-woman pulled. They went.

Up the road toward the wall. Through all of it. The fire and the people and the noise, the carts overturned, a dog running, a child sitting in the road. Old Grey walked through it as if it were a road. It was a road. The weight was on his shoulders because it went up. His feet took it. Nothing in it reached him. Things happened a little way off, to a big grey beast he was walking beside.

There was a woman in front of them. Not the bread-woman. Another. She smelled of iron and sweat and a great cat. She took Sorrel's halter. She shouted something at the bread-woman and the bread-woman shouted something back, and then the gate was there, a great black hole in the wall with people pouring through it, and they went at it.

Mabbin went through on her hind legs. Sorrel went after her mother.

Old Grey went last, with the bread-woman at his head now, through the dark of the gateway, hooves loud on the stone under the arch, the wall going over him, the bell right above him so loud it was in his teeth.

And out the other side.

---

It was a yard. Stone, under his feet, and walls all round, and people everywhere, and in the middle of it a long low building with its doors open and people going in.

The bread-woman stopped. She was breathing hard. The rope went slack between her hand and his halter.

There was a voice.

He heard it through everything. Through the bell, and the people, and the iron down the road, and the screaming, and the smell of honey-smoke on the wind outside the wall. Thin. High. A girl's voice. A thin girl with a great deal of hair, standing on the stones by the long building with her arms out.

"Old Grey," it said.

And then again, because once might not get there.

"Old Grey."

---

The small thing in the corner of the pen lifted its head.

It had lifted its head once before for a voice, a hand's breadth, and stopped. It had lifted its head once before for a smell, and come to the door, and this was what had come of it.

It got up.

It didn't know how. Its legs didn't know the way. There was no road across the floor of the pen from the corner to the stall, it had never walked it, and the stall was dark and the door was open, and the honey-smoke was on the wind.

It took a step.

---

Outside, in Greyhold's yard, with the bell going and the gate behind him and nobody at his head, Old Grey lifted his near forefoot off the stones.

It was the stiff side. It had been stiff every morning of the winter. He felt it. Somewhere not very far off at all, he felt it.

He put it down again a stride nearer the voice.

Then he stood, as he had stood before, his head low, his eyes on nothing.

Behind him the bread-woman looked at the rope in her hand, slack, with nobody pulling it, as if somebody had put it there.
