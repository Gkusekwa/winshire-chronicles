---

At Greyhold they said the cat had been there before the school was built. Grieve said that was nonsense; it had been there since he came, and it had been old then. Neither of them had asked the cat.

It was grey, and the size of a calf, and it slept. That was what people saw. In summer it slept in the only shade in the yard, and in spring on the wall top where the sun came first, and at night in the Long Barn, in a different stall every night, curled in the straw against some beast's flank. When anyone looked at it, it was asleep, or near enough. They looked, and went away thinking they knew what it was for.

It let them. It had never seen the use in being looked at.

---

It knew the hill by its warm places.

There were the ordinary ones. The stones of the wall top, when the sun had been on them an hour. The bake-house wall at dawn. The straw where a big beast had lain all night and just got up. The infirmary's warming-stones, which the old women there woke with a sound and which stayed warm till morning without a fire. The white lamps in their iron brackets along every wall, which had never once been lit and were always lit, and were warm to sit under, a little, like a hand held near a face.

And there was the warm under.

It came up through the whole hill. Everywhere, faint, so that the stones of Greyhold were never as cold as stones ought to be, even in the hard frost of the winter when the river stopped. It was strongest at the north end, under the tower, where the narrow stair went down to the paper man's room. There it came up through the steps like the heat through the side of a beast asleep. In the winter, on three nights when it turned over, the cat had lain on that stair and felt it in its belly, and not slept at all.

It hadn't lain there since. Some warm places are not for lying on.

---

It knew the beasts by their warmth too.

Every beast had its own. The hounds ran hot and quick and close to the skin. The greyharts were warm deep down and cool at the edges, like a pond in summer. The martens in their wire cages were small sparks. The two dray-beasts that pulled the wains were warm in a slow, even, endless way, like a hayrick that has been heating since harvest, and they had only the one warmth each.

The others had two.

That was the thing people didn't see, with their eyes always on faces. A bonded beast had its own warmth, and inside it, running out of it, another. A line. It went out of the beast and away across the yard, or up the wall, or out of the gate and down the River Road, and at the end of it, always, was a person. A boy on the wall with a sword. A girl at drill. A grey woman asleep in the knights' lodging with her boots on. The line ran out of the hound in its stall to the boy on the wall, and back, and the hound lay with its chin on its paws and was not alone in itself, and never would be.

Two warms. One life. The cat did not have the sounds for that, but it knew it as it knew the sun.

And it knew this. The night before a bad day, the line went thin.

Not cold. Thin. Drawn out and fine, as milk goes thin in a dish left in the rain. A beast whose person was going to fall off the wall stair tomorrow, or take a spear, or lie down on a bed and not get up, had a line in it running fine as a hair. The beast didn't know. People didn't know. The cat knew, as a beast knows weather turning, and when it found a thin line in the barn at night it went into that stall and lay down against that beast and stayed.

It had never wanted a reason for that. A cat goes to the warm place. This was the other thing a cat goes to.

People had noticed, over the years, the ones who looked. The fork man had noticed. The fork man watched where it slept and said a sound to the midden girl, and then the next day the old greyhart's man fell off the stair in the rain. The fork man had liked that. He'd said the same sound for days.

There had been many. The old ox-beast with the white face, whose line had gone fine as cobweb one winter night and then gone altogether, and the little old man at the other end of it had not been long after. The brindled hound's girl, who had no line left in her at all, only the place where one had been, torn; that was the coldest thing the cat had ever lain beside, and it had lain on the foot of her bed to the end because there was nothing else to lie beside. There had been more of them than there were stalls in the barn.

It had always been one at a time.

---

On the hundred and forty-third day, as people counted days, which the cat did not, the thin was everywhere.

The cat felt it come up the hill in the afternoon like river fog. Not in one stall. In all of them. In the hounds and the martens and the hawks and the greyharts, in the hound in the yard that a girl was holding by the scruff, in the falcon on the post by the north tower. Every line on the hill had gone fine at once, and the cat stood in the middle of the Long Barn with its tail low and didn't know which way to go.

It had never had that. It sat down in the aisle and washed one paw, slowly, to think.

The barn was loud. It had been loud since the day before, since the gate. There were people in it who didn't belong to it: people from the camp by the river and the town along the bank, who smelled of smoke and wet bundles and being afraid. They sat against the walls of the empty stalls with children in their laps. Three big greys from the farms stood near the door, a mare and her grown daughter and a great old gelding with white in his muzzle who stood where he was put and didn't move his head. The old woman who belonged with them smelled of herbs and singing. A square woman smelled of bread and lye. Two small ones slept against her.

The cat went past them all and they didn't see it. People with children in their laps don't.

---

At dusk the greyharts went.

The cat heard the hooves in the yard and went to the barn door. Riders. Every rider on the hill who could sit a beast, in mail, with the small grey captain at the front on her grey horse, and the greyharts led out of the west end with their great white heads up and their eyes rolling, and the hounds at heel, and the falcons up. They went out through the river gate onto the road and turned north, upriver, fast. The lines went with them, thin, all of them. The cat watched them go out of the gate into the dusk and down the road and away until it couldn't feel them any more.

The barn was half empty after. It had never been half empty.

The cat went down the water-gate stair.

---

It didn't often go that way. The water-gate stair went down inside the river wall, steep and wet, in a long slant, to a low arch at the bottom with iron bars across it, and on the other side of the bars was the river. The steps were cold. There was no warm in them at all, this close to the water. But the cat had felt something below that it didn't have a name for, and it went down to look.

There was a boat at the bars.

A flat boat, a ferry, with a woman standing in it and a pole in her hands, holding it against the current. She was small. Her hands were big. She smelled of tar, and river, and eel smoke, and bread, a different bread from the barn's, and over all of it, faint, of a girl: milk and river mud. She was shouting.

The same sound. Over and over, up through the bars at the sentry on the steps above the arch. A boy, a student, with a spear. He shouted something back. She shouted the sound again, louder, and banged the pole on the bars so they rang.

The cat sat on the third step from the bottom and looked at her.

She had no line in her. She was a one-warm, like the drays, like the fork man. But there was something in her the cat hadn't felt in a one-warm before. Not thin. People didn't go thin; they had nothing to go thin. A damp. A cold coming up in her, very far off, like the cold off the river in the evening before anyone can see the mist. Not tomorrow. Not for some days. But coming.

The cat looked at her through the bars, and she didn't see it. She was looking up the stair, past it.

The paper man came down.

Slowly, with his stick, one step at a time, past the boy with the spear, past the cat on the third step, without seeing either. He smelled of old paper and ink, and faintly of wine. He went right down to the bars and put his face to them, and the river woman put her face to them from the other side, and she talked low and fast, the same few sounds, and he listened.

Then he turned round and went back up the stair. Fast, for him. His stick knocked on every step. He didn't look at the boy. He didn't look at the cat. He went up past it into the dark of the stair, toward the yard and the north tower, and the river woman stood in her boat and watched him go.

Then she pushed off from the bars and the boat went away downriver, into the dusk, toward the long low house at the foot of the jetty, with a lamp in its window that someone had lit by hand.

The cat stayed on the step a while, in the cold. Then it went up.

---

It went to the boar.

Out of all of them it went to the boar, because the boar's line had gone finest. The cat could feel it from the barn door, at the far end of the west end, in the biggest stall, behind the place where the iron-banded door had been before the boar broke it in the winter. A line so fine you could hardly tell it was there, running out of that great black bulk and away across the yard to the knights' lodging, where the boar's woman was putting on her mail.

The boar was awake. It lay at the back of its stall with its great tusked head on the straw and its small red eyes open, looking at nothing. It looked at the cat when the cat came in, and didn't move, and the cat went past its tusks and lay down between its forefeet, where it had lain once before, and put its back against the boar's chest.

The boar breathed. It was like lying against a bellows. Each breath went in a long way and came out hot and smelling of acorns and blood.

The cat lay there through the first hour of the night, and the second.

It could feel the rest of them, all down the barn and out in the yard and up on the walls. All of them gone fine. It could feel the hounds left at the east end, and the hawks, and the falcon and the falcon's boy, somewhere up by the north tower, whose line was the finest of all except the boar's. The cat lay against the boar and felt them all and couldn't go to any of them. It had chosen. A cat is in one place.

So it lay against the boar, and the boar breathed, and outside in the dark the hill waited.

---

The bell began at the third hour.

It was the other bell, fast, on and on, the one that got everybody up. The barn woke all at once. Children crying. People getting to their feet against the stall walls, and the fork man's voice, flat, saying sounds.

The boar got up.

It got up all at once, as it did everything, and the cat went out from between its forefeet and sat in the straw at the side of the stall and let it. The boar stood with its head up, toward the river, toward the gate. Its bristles stood. The line in it was so fine now it was a line drawn in frost.

The boar's woman came.

She came down the aisle with a sword at her side and a lantern in her hand, broad and square and grey, in a riding coat with mail under it, and she didn't run. She came to the stall and put her hand flat on the boar's shoulder, and said a sound, low, and the boar turned its great head to her for the first time in many nights and put its snout against her coat.

She said the sound again.

Then they went out. The two of them. Up the aisle, past the children, past the greys, out of the barn door into the noise. The cat sat in the straw of the empty stall and watched them go, the boar's black back and the woman's grey head beside it going out into the firelight.

It didn't follow. There was nothing to follow for. The cat goes beside them in the night. In the morning they go out, and what happens happens.

It sat in the warm the boar had left in the straw and waited.

It wasn't long. The bell rang and rang. There was a great noise from the river gate, iron and shouting and the gate itself, the great oak leaves of it, swinging. Then, far off across the yard, in the gateway, both of the warms the cat had lain beside went out.

Together. The boar's and the woman's. One and then, in the same breath, the other, as two candles go out in one draught from a door.

The cat sat in the straw.

Then the bell stopped, in the middle of a stroke, as if someone had put a hand on it, and after that there was only the noise.

---

The yard filled with men.

The cat went up onto the beam over the barn door, where it could see. Men in grey coats were coming in through the river gate, a great many of them, with spears and torches, and they smelled of the road and of sweat and of the sweet burnt smoke that the cat had smelled before only on the bound beasts that came up the river road. Bound beasts came with them. Hounds, silent, in a pack, at the heel of a man with his arm up. The cat looked at them from the beam and its fur went up all down its back.

They had no warm of their own. None. They were warm as a stone is warm after the sun has been on it, from outside, and inside them where a beast's own warmth should be there was a small dark place, a long way in, and nothing in it. Their eyes were empty. They went where the man's arm went.

The cat had seen them before, on the road, and on the day of the gate, and never so many. It didn't like to look at them. It looked anyway.

Knights came out of the lodging and off the walls, the few who were left, and the yard became the kind of place where everything is happening and nothing can be seen. Fire caught somewhere. The light jumped. The people in the barn were dragging things into the doorway below the cat's beam: carts, and barrels, and the boar's broken iron-banded door, and the fork man stood up on a cart behind it all with a pitchfork in his hands, bald and square, saying sounds at the people as if they were beasts to be got in out of the rain. And they got in.

At the gap at the end, where the carts didn't meet, someone put the old grey gelding.

He stood in it. He didn't do anything else. His head was low and his eyes were empty and he stood in the gap like a wall. The cat looked down at him from the beam. He had no line. He'd never had one; he was a one-warm, like the wain-drays. But a long way in, where the bound hounds had nothing, he had something. A small warm, in a corner of himself, very small, lying down with its face to the wall.

He had had his bad day already, the cat thought. A long time ago. And was still in it.

A man in grey came at the gap with a spear and stopped in front of him, a few paces off, and didn't come any closer.

---

At the east end of the barn, among the small ones, an old yellow hound bitch had begun to whine.

The cat felt her from the beam. She was old, grey round the muzzle, with a bad hip, and she belonged to a knight with a grey beard who was on the river wall now in the dark with a sword. Her line went out of the barn and over the yard and up the wall stair to him, and it was so fine the cat could hardly find it. She lay in her stall with her chin on her paws and whined, high and thin, without stopping, and nobody went to her. People had children to hold.

The cat came down off the beam.

It went along the aisle, past the empty stalls of the west end and the stall where the ox had been, past the greys' old woman sitting on a bucket with her stick across her knees. In the brindled hound's stall, the old one by the north wall that nobody had put anything in since autumn, two small ones were asleep in the straw where somebody had laid them. The cat went past them. It went into the yellow hound's stall and lay down against her back.

She stopped whining.

She turned her head and looked at the cat, and then put her head down again on her paws, and the cat lay against her with its spine along hers. She was hot. Hounds always were. Her bad hip trembled.

Two cages along, in the dark, a hawk's line had gone fine too. A young one. The cat could feel it. It couldn't lie beside both.

So it lay beside the hound.

It felt the knight on the wall. Through her, along the line. Not him. The far end of the line, where he was. Heat and noise and cold air and the stone under his boots and, for a breath, something very bright.

Then the line went.

It came back down the line into the hound all at once, out of the dark, like a door slamming at the far end of a long room so the cold comes down the room and hits you, and the hound lifted her head off her paws and looked at the barn wall, toward the river, with her ears up. As if she'd heard her name.

Then she put her head down.

The cat lay against her. The heat went out of her slowly, as it goes out of a stone at night. The cat didn't move until it was all gone.

Two cages along, the hawk had gone out in the same breath. With nobody.

The cat lay in the straw against the cooling hound and listened to the yard. There was a great deal of it. Men shouting in the dark at the north end, at the tower, where the paper man's stair went down: a lot of men, going that way, and the falcon's boy, and the falcon screaming. The falcon's boy's line ran very fine and very bright, and his falcon's back to him, and they were holding the stair. The cat could feel them doing it. A long while. Longer than the cat would have thought.

At the bottom of all that, deep, under the tower, under the stair, the warm under had begun to move.

---

Then the big one came in through the gate.

The cat felt it before it heard anything. The hill felt it. The stones of the yard, under the flags, under the straw, under the cat, went tight, as a beast's skin goes tight when you put your hand on it, and the cat was on its feet in the hound's stall with its back up before it knew it had moved.

Then the noise in the yard stopped.

All of it. The shouting and the iron and the screaming, everything, as if every man in the yard had drawn a breath at once and was holding it. The cat went up the aisle and onto the beam again, fast, and looked.

A beast had come in through the river gate.

It was bigger than the boar. It was bigger than anything that had ever been in the yard, bigger than the greyharts, taller at the shoulder than the barn door, and grey, and it stood in the middle of the yard in the firelight with its great head low and its shoulders up, and every man in grey and every knight and every boy with a spear had gone back from it, all round, and left a space. It smelled of the river. Of the island, which the cat had smelled on the wind from upriver all its life without ever going there, cold stone and old trees and the reeds. And of something else, under that, that the cat's nose went into and couldn't find the bottom of.

And it had a line.

New. The cat had never felt one so new. Raw at the edges like a cut. It went out of the big one's warmth, which was enormous, which was like standing in front of an oven with the door open, and up, to a person on its back: a girl, flat on its neck, with burned hands in its fur. The cat knew her. She smelled of the midden. The girl who'd come to the barn with a fork every morning and been let in by the brindled hound.

The midden girl and the big one. One line, between them, thick as a rope.

And thin. Already. Even that new, even that thick. Somewhere ahead of it, not now but before the night was out, a cold was coming down that line so great the cat flattened its ears to feel it.

The big one turned his head toward the barn.

The cat lay flat on the beam and didn't move.

The stones by the well got up.

They lifted, one against another, grey on grey, and came up out of the yard into a shape, a back and a shoulder and a head, as big as the big one, with the dust pouring off it, and stood between the barn and the men by the well who'd been gathering there with their spears. The men by the well stopped gathering.

The cat looked at the stones standing up in the yard. It had lain on those stones in the sun. It had never known they could do that.

Then the big one went away north across the yard, toward the tower, toward the falcon screaming, and something green came down out of the dark over the walls with its wings spread, screaming louder, and the men in grey by the gate dropped what they had and ran out of it into the night.

---

From the north end of the yard, the falcon's line went out.

The cat felt it like a stair that isn't there when the foot comes down. A bright small hard warm, going. And then, a breath later, at the other end of what had been the line, the falcon's boy. Not all at once. Slower. A long cold coming down into him from the far side of himself, as the cold had come down into the hound. The cat could feel him lie down in it.

Then he was gone too.

The stones by the well lay down, one on another, into a heap by the well, and were stones.

The cat stayed on the beam.

Below it, at the gap, the old grey gelding stood where he'd been put. The man with the spear in front of him had gone. In the dark behind the gelding, the greys' old woman had got up off her bucket, and the bread woman had both her small ones in her arms out of the hound's old stall, and was holding them very tight. The fork man stood on his cart with the pitchfork and looked across the yard toward the north tower, and his face did nothing.

Under the hill the warm under was turning over. The cat could feel it in the beam. In its claws. Slowly. Like something very big in a very small space finding that the space had got smaller.

The cat got down off the beam and went out over the barricade, over the carts, past the gelding's legs, and across the yard.

Nobody stopped it. Nobody looked. There were dead men on the flags and fires burning in the corners of the yard, and the white lamps on the walls were going on and off, on and off, all round the walls, as if the hill were breathing on them. The cat went through all of it with its tail low and its ears back and went to the north tower.

---

The green one was on the stair.

She was three steps down, as far as she could get, a dragon in a stairwell meant for people, with her hind feet in the broken flags at the top and her forefeet on the steps and her wings spread out over the whole stairwell like a roof. Green, and young, the cat thought, though it had never seen a dragon close before. Hot. Hotter than the boar. She was shaking. On one of her horns a scrap of something was tied, and it smelled of milk and river mud.

Under her wings, on the steps, lay the falcon's boy, on his back, with his head on the step above, and the falcon beside him on the step with his hand on her.

The midden girl and the big one were gone. Down. The cat could feel them, far down already, under the tower, under the stair, going down through the place where the black door had been. It could feel the hole they'd made. A breath came up out of it, warm and cold together, and the old paper smell.

The paper man was down there too, at the bottom of the stair by the door. The cat could smell him. Paper, and ink, and blood. He wasn't anything else any more.

The green one looked round at the cat.

She had a line. It went out of her upriver, out under the water, a long way, further than any line the cat had ever felt. It went to a man somewhere off there in the dark, near water, among stones, and the line was thin. Fine as a hair. She didn't know. Her head kept turning that way, upriver, and back, as if she'd heard something and couldn't find it.

The cat went down past her forefoot, onto the step, and lay down against the inside of her foreleg, under her wing, beside the falcon's boy and the falcon.

The green one went still.

She looked down at the cat. Her head came down, close, a great green head with eyes like lamps, and her breath went over the cat, hot, smelling of the sky and of meat. She made a small sound, high, at the back of her throat, a sound with a lift at the end of it. The cat didn't know what it meant. It didn't look like the sort of sound that needed an answer.

The cat put its chin on its paws.

After a while the green one's head went back up. She didn't move her foreleg. She kept her wings out over the stair, and she kept looking upriver, and the cat lay against her and felt her shake, and then shake less.

---

They lay there into the deep middle of the night.

The hill got worse. The white lamps on the walls stopped going on and off and came on and stayed on, all of them, too bright, so bright the yard had no dark corners left in it and every stone had a shadow as sharp as a knife. The ward-letters over the stair were burning. The cat could feel the warm under coming up the stair now, out of the hole, not faint, not a warmth you lay on, but hot, and moving, and getting closer to the top of the stone, as water climbs a trough left under a spout.

The cat's fur stood up all along its back and stayed up.

Far down the stair, below the paper man, below everything, at the bottom of the hole, the big one's line went bright and hard. Something was happening down there. The cat felt the midden girl's end of it pull tight, and pull, and then a cold came down that line, the cold the cat had felt coming in the yard, and it was worse than the cat had thought it would be. Worse than the hound. Worse than the boy on the step. It came into the big one from somewhere under him, out of the rock, out of the warm under itself, and the big one took it, and the girl took it through him, and the cat, three steps from the top of the stair under a dragon's wing, felt it go by and pressed itself flat on the stone.

The green one got up.

She didn't decide it. She got up all at once, as the boar had. Her foreleg went out from against the cat and her wings came in, and she put her head down the stairwell toward the cold, and went down it. Past the falcon's boy. Past the cat. Down the stair, into the hole, the way the midden girl had gone, folding herself small into it, scraping, down and round and out of sight.

The cat felt her go all the way down. It felt her get to the bottom, and find the big one, and lie down beside him.

That was right. The cat knew it was right. The big one was too big to lie beside, for a cat, and too far down.

Then the hill dropped.

---

Not the hill. Something a long way off, upriver, under the water, going down. The cat felt it through every stone of Greyhold at once, through the step under its belly and the wall at its back and the flags of the yard: a great dropping, as if a piece of the world had been a floor with nothing under it and somebody had taken the floor away. The lamps on the walls went white as noon. The stair shook. Dust came down the stairwell in a sheet.

And under the hill, at the bottom of everything, the warm under woke.

The cat had felt it turn over in the winter. It had felt it turning all night. Now a thing that had been asleep was awake, all at once, all through, and the warm under was not warm any more. It was something the cat had no way to feel. Too big. A heat with no bottom and no beast in it. As if the bound hounds' small dark places had all been put together into one, and the one was the size of the hill, and it had been lying under the cat all its life.

The cat lay on the step beside the falcon's boy and the falcon, with its ears flat and its fur up and its claws in the cracks of the stone, and did not go anywhere. There was nowhere to go that wasn't on top of it.

---

The night went on.

It went on a long time after that, and the cat didn't keep much of it. People came to the top of the stair and looked down at the falcon's boy and went away. Someone put a cloak over him and the falcon. Someone tried to lift the cat off him, and the cat looked at them, and they stopped trying.

In the small hours the chains began to go.

The cat hadn't known there were chains. It knew now. Each one went with a sound it felt rather than heard, a long falling thunder deep in the rock, under the river, under the hill, going on and on, like the bucket chain running down the well in the yard when someone lets go of the handle. One. A while. Another. The hill shook with each one and settled. Somewhere in between, very far off and very deep and going away, the cat felt the big one's warmth and the green one's, together, moving. Then more lines with them, the green one's man, and two other great hot dragon-warms, and all of them going away through the rock upriver, as if the rock were letting them through.

The last chain went. After it there was a quiet so long the cat lifted its head.

Then the sun came up.

---

The cat felt it come up through the stone. It had never felt the sun come up through stone before.

The hill heaved. All of it, once, a great slow shrug, as a horse shrugs off flies. On the wall to the east, the old grey inner wall that the knights faced every noon to make their sounds, something split. The cat heard it. A crack going up through the stones from the bottom to the top, slow, grinding, so loud and so long that every bird on the river went up at once.

And the warm under went.

Up. Out of the hill, out of the river, up, and away. The cat felt it go out of every stone at once, as the heat goes out of a stall when the door is opened in winter. It went away toward the sunrise. North and east, over the far bank, over the hills, further and further, and the cat lay on the step and felt it go until there was nothing to feel.

All round the walls of Greyhold the white lamps went out.

All of them, together, in one breath. The cat had never seen them out. Nobody on the hill had. They went dark in their iron brackets and stayed dark, and the yard was grey in the dawn and nothing more.

The step under the cat was cold.

The cat lay on it for a while and didn't believe it.

Then it got up and went down the stair. It hadn't meant to. Past the cloak over the falcon's boy, down, to the bottom, to the paper man's door, which was open, and past the paper man's long dark room with its brown books all over the floor, to the far end, where the three steps went down, and the hole that had been the black door.

The paper man sat at the end of the bottom step against the wall, with his stick across his knees. The cat sat at the top of the three steps and looked down past him into the hole.

Cold came up out of it. River cold, and stone cold. That was all.

No. Not all. Very far down, under the cold, under where the warm under had been, there was something else. Small. Shut. Like a fist closed round something. It didn't breathe in and it didn't breathe out. It wasn't warm.

The cat looked at it for some time, from the top of the three steps.

It didn't lie on cold things.

It went back up into the yard.

---

The noon bell didn't ring.

The cat sat on the wall top above the landing, where the sun was, and waited for it, because it had waited for it every noon for longer than the fork man had been on the hill. At noon the bell rang, and the people came out into the yard and stood in their lines facing the old grey wall and said the same sounds all together, the students yawning, the knights with their eyes on nothing.

At noon nobody came out. Nobody stood in lines. There was a split in the old grey wall from the bottom to the top that a child could have put her arm into.

The sun went over and down. The cat stayed on the wall top. The stones were warm only where the sun had been on them, and nowhere else, and when the sun moved off a stone it went cold behind.

---

They gathered the dead all that day. The cat watched from the wall. The falcon's boy was carried up the stair on a door, with the falcon on his chest. The paper man came up last, and nobody seemed sure where to put him. The old yellow hound was carried out of the barn and laid by her knight in a row along the chapel wall, and the young hawk by its girl. The boar lay where it had fallen, across the gateway, with the boar's woman against its shoulder, because it was too big for anybody to move.

There was no thin anywhere on the hill.

The cat went down to the barn at dusk and went along every stall and felt every beast that was left in it, every hound and marten and the two old wain-drays and the hawk that had lived. Their lines were all still there, the ones that were left. Bruised. Pulled. But not thin. Whatever had been coming had come. Nobody on the hill was going to have a bad day tomorrow that they hadn't had already today.

The cat had never found the barn like that. It walked up the aisle to the end and back, and there was no stall to lie down in.

---

After dark the farm people went down the water-gate stair.

The cat watched them from the barn door. The bread woman first, with the smallest one on her hip, crying in a tired, steady way, and a lantern. The old woman with the stick, with the boy by the wrist. Then the mare, and her daughter. Then the grey gelding.

Nobody led him. His rope hung from his headstall and trailed on the flags. He came out of the barn last, slowly, and went across the yard after them toward the head of the water-gate stair, and he went down it into the dark on his own, his great feet finding each step.

The cat watched him go. The small warm in his corner, a long way in. Still lying down. But with its face, now, turned a little from the wall.

He was going toward the milk-and-river-mud girl. The cat could smell her down there at the bottom of the stair, at the long low house, with the river woman. He went toward her as a beast goes down to water.

---

The next day they put the dead in the ground on the hill west of the walls.

The cat lay on the west wall in the thin spring sun and watched them dig. Fifty-three holes in a morning, in rows. The beasts went in with their people, side by side, one grave, because one warm had gone out of them both in one breath and it would have been wrong to put them apart. It took eleven men and a dray-team on a chain to drag the boar up from the gateway, and its grave was twice as big as any other. The falcon went in on the falcon's boy's chest, under his chin, where she'd always sat. The old yellow hound went in against her knight's legs. The small beasts went in, and the hounds.

The fork man stood at the top of the hill and said sounds, one after another, all morning, in his flat voice, and every time he said one people looked at a hole.

At the foot of the hill, apart, at a long stone's throw from the nearest grave, the midden girl stood with the big one.

He lay behind her on the grass with his head on his paws, watching the graves fill. The line between them was there, thick, sore. The great cold that had come down it in the night had come, and gone through, and left the line standing, as a fence stands after a flood with the water gone through it. They hadn't gone thin again. They were only tired.

The cat didn't go down.

---

After that the days went on, and the hill was cold, and the cat went round it.

It lay on the wall top above the landing in the mornings. It lay in the barn at night, not in any stall in particular, wherever there was straw. The paper man's stair was cold and it didn't go there. On the second day the midden girl went down it with the big one, into the dark, and came up hours after, smelling of river water and of the paper man's room, with a black book against her chest. On the third day men set a ladder against the gatehouse and hung a dead man in chains over the river gate, by the charter cut in the stone, and the cat smelled him from the wall, and nobody's line on the whole hill went thin for him, not even a little. If anyone was going to be sorry about that man, they were a long way off.

On the fourth day a thin man came and shouted at the landing with a brass horn. On the fourth evening, on the shingle below the landing, an old bronze dragon opened a torn wing and tried it and lay down again.

The cat watched it all from the wall top.

There was no thin on the hill. But below it, below the river wall, down at the landing, in the long low house with the turf roof and the lamp lit by hand, there was.

The cat felt it gather. Day by day. In the milk-and-river-mud girl first, a damp, like a sky coming over. In the bread woman. In the old woman with the stick. And most in the river woman, the small one with the big hands. The cold the cat had felt in her at the water-gate bars, far off, was nearer every day. It came up off the river toward her house like the evening mist, which lies along the bank a little higher every hour.

The cat lay on the wall top above it and watched it rise.

It had no business at the landing. The landing was off the hill. There was no barn there and no beasts with lines, only three farm greys, and the big one, whose grief had come and was his own. The cat lay on the wall and told itself that, in the way of cats, by washing a paw.

---

On the sixth morning, at first light, everyone at the landing came out of the long house.

The cat sat on the wall top and watched. The river woman got her ferry off its slip and into the water. The old woman stood on the jetty with the boy by the hand and the smallest one on her hip, and the bread woman stood in the door of the house and came no further. A young man was there, the midden girl's brother by his smell, with an old man propped in a chair at the top of the jetty steps under a blanket. The milk-and-river-mud girl stood on the end of the jetty with her arms folded. The grey gelding stood behind her.

The midden girl was at the foot of the landing, with the big one.

A man went along all of them. He smelled of barley and dragons. The cat had never smelled him before but it knew him at once: he was the far end of the green one's line, the man who had been near water among the stones. He went to the midden girl last and they stood and said something short to each other, and then he went down to the water.

The old bronze dragon went into the river.

She went in slowly, testing the stones, and then the shelf went out from under her and the river took her. A red one went in beside her, under her chest, against the current. The green one flew low over them both, crying. The river woman hauled her ferry out along the rope beside them with the barley man in it, and he put his hand out and laid it on the old one's neck, and kept it there. The old one held her torn wing up out of the water the whole way. The cat could see it shaking from the wall.

It took them the best part of an hour to get across. On the far side they came out on the stones and stood. Then they went away up the bank, slowly, and were gone.

Halfway across, coming back alone on the rope, the river woman stopped hauling.

She stood in the ferry in the middle of the river with her big hands on the rope and looked back at the landing. At the girl on the end of the jetty. At the midden girl, sitting down now against the big one's side at the foot of the landing, with a long bright thing across her knees, drawing a stone down it and not looking up.

The cat felt the cold in her come up another hand's breadth.

Then she hauled again, and came in.

---

At noon the midden girl and her brother went off along the bank toward the town with a handcart, and the big one went with them. The landing was quiet. The old man in the chair had been carried in. The green one had gone. The smoke from the house's chimney went straight up in the still air.

The cat got up off the wall top.

It went down through the yard to the head of the water-gate stair. It went down the stair, all the way, past the place where it had sat on the third step and watched the river woman shout. The bars at the bottom stood open now. The cat went out through them onto the stones of the landing, into the light.

The river was loud. It smelled of mud and snowmelt.

The cat walked along the foot of the landing, past the jetty, past the ferry drawn up again on its slip with the water running out of it. The milk-and-river-mud girl was on the end of the jetty with her legs over the water and the grey gelding standing behind her. The gelding's head didn't move. The girl looked at the cat walking past along the stones, a long level look, and the cat looked back, and went on.

The long low house had its door shut. There was a step in front of it. One stone, broad and worn hollow in the middle by feet.

The cat sat down on it.

It was warm.

The sun was round the other side of the house, and nothing was warm from under any more, on this side of the river. It was warm from inside. From a fire on a hearth on the other side of the door, that somebody had built that morning out of driftwood and peat, and a lamp on a table with a brass foot that somebody had lit with a spill. The warm came out under the door and through the stone and up into the cat, a small warm, a made one, a warm a person had made with her hands.

The cat sat on it with its tail round its feet and looked at the river.

---

After a while the door opened behind it.

The river woman stood in her doorway with her sleeves rolled up and a cloth in her hands, and looked down at the cat on her step.

The cat didn't turn round. It could feel her there behind it. The warm of the kitchen came out round her into the afternoon, and the smell of eel and smoke and wet wool, and the cold in her, very near now. Tomorrow. Or the day after. Coming up off the river toward her door.

She said a sound. She said it again, differently, as if the first one hadn't been the right one.

The cat didn't move.

She went back in. She left the door open. The warm came out of it onto the step.

When she came out again she had a dish in her hand, with eel on it, and she bent down with a grunt and put it on the step beside the cat and stood up again with her hand in the small of her back.

The cat looked at the eel. Nobody had ever seen it eat. It didn't see why a woman with a dish should be the first.

The river woman looked at it not eating. She made a short sound, low in her throat, that might have been a laugh. Then she wiped her hands on the cloth and went back into her kitchen, and left the door open, and the warm came out.

The cat sat on the river woman's step, in the warm from her door, with its tail round its feet, and kept her.
