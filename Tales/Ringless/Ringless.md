They tell it differently in every inn.

In the Marches they say she went over Greyhold's wall one winter with a blunt sword and a hammer, and came back in the spring out of the river on a lion's back, with the knights' own chain round its neck that she'd cut off it with her own hands. On the Candle Downs they say the lion is the size of a hay-wain and pulls iron out of the ground like carrots, and there are two of it, because one lion couldn't do what that one does. On the Pass road they will tell you she's the old king's lost granddaughter, and the lion was left for her on the Greyhold steps in a basket. By the autumn there were men on the Iron Road who'd swear there were seven lions, and that she'd never once been afraid of anything, and that she sang to them.

Tobin heard most of them. He'd been at the start of it. He could have told them a better one than any of theirs, if anybody had asked him, which nobody did.

She mucked out the Long Barn. She counted things under her breath. She had a horse called Turnip with a turnip tied to his bridle. She couldn't sing.

---

Millbrook was burning, and he was holding the bottom of it.

That was the joke, if there was one. The rising had come down off the hill to hold the town for the one realm, and Vale had stood on the wain with his arms out and said no Millbrook house would burn, and now the whole of the jetty road was on fire from top to bottom and Tobin was holding the foot of it against nobody. The knights were somewhere up in the smoke, on the far side of the fire. The fire was holding the street for everybody.

Carts. Barrels. A boat turned on its side across the slip, somebody's boat, Fennick's eel-boat maybe, with the tar going soft on its bottom in the heat. A dozen of them behind it with staves and billhooks and a pitchfork. His own stave was a hay-rake's handle with the rake knocked off. His eyes ran. Everybody's eyes ran. They'd stopped wiping them.

An hour ago there'd been a hundred of them at the top of the street, saying the words. Saying them all together, low, over and over, the words Reede had taught them in the tithe barn, that weren't any tongue Tobin knew and turned a knight's horse-word aside like a hand turning a cup. He'd felt them work. He'd been saying them and felt them go out of him and lie down across the street like a bar on a door, and a knight's horse had gone up on its hind legs at the bottom of the hill with the man's hand flat on its neck, shouting, and the horse hadn't heard him. Tobin had laughed. He'd been laughing, saying the words. Then the roofs over the barricades had caught, and the words had come apart in everybody's mouths into coughing, and the hundred had come apart with them, and here he was. A dozen. The foot of the street. The river at his back.

"They'll not come down through that," said Kemble, beside him. Kemble mended nets on the jetty. Had done. The jetty was alight at the far end. "Not with horses. Not through that."

"Then what are we holding it for?"

Kemble looked at him.

"I'm asking," said Tobin. "I'm only asking. Nobody tells me anything. I'm asking what we're holding."

"Ludd said hold the slip."

"Ludd's up the hill."

Nobody said anything to that. Dunnock, the Saltreach boatman, spat over the boat into the street. A thatch came down somewhere above them with a long soft roar, and the sparks went over their heads on the river wind like starlings, and settled on the water behind them and went out.

---

He'd seen the camp at dawn.

Before the knights came. Before anything. He'd been on the jetty road with Kemble at first light, rolling barrels, and somebody had said *look*, and pointed down the river past the last houses to the water-meadows, where the tents had been all spring. The Saltreach people's tents. The ones who'd come over on the ferry in the thaw with their carts and their children because the companies were burning the far bank.

There was smoke over the water-meadows. Low, brown, lying along the grass.

"That's them," someone had said. "That's the tents."

And someone else had laughed. Not a big laugh. A short one, the kind you'd give a joke you'd heard already.

He'd walked down after, when the barrels were done, on his own, to look. He didn't know what he'd thought he'd see. The tents were gone. The poles were standing, a lot of them, black, in rows, with nothing on them. There was a kettle on a tripod over a heap of grey ash, still upright, and when he touched the tripod it was warm. Nobody. Not a body, not a dog. They'd got out, whoever they were. Gone up the road to Greyhold in the dark with what they could carry, and left the kettle.

Nobody had said about the camp. Not in the tithe barn. Not Ludd. Not Reede. *Millbrook rises, holds the town, the knights get caught between the town and the Ruin.* That was what they'd said. That was all they'd said.

He'd stood there with his hand on the warm tripod and thought, *Somebody knew.* Somebody had sent somebody down the back channel in the dark with a pot of fire. That was a thing somebody had decided. And they hadn't said, because they'd known what people like Tobin would say.

He'd gone back up the road and rolled more barrels. What else was he going to do?

---

She came along the slip in the light of the burning street.

He knew her before he saw her face. He'd have known her in the dark. Two years of watching her walk across the yard at Greyhold to the posts, to the barn, to the bell, as if every walk was a patrol and she'd been told to report on it after. She came along the slip at the water's edge with a door carried between her and somebody else, and a man lying on the door, and a sword in her belt. A real one. She'd got a real one at last.

The other end of the door was Tam.

The man on the door was Odo Marl. Tobin knew his arms. Everybody in Millbrook knew Odo's arms.

He looked round from the barricade and saw the three of them, and his face did something he couldn't feel it do. He looked at Tam. At Odo, lying on the door with his teeth set and his legs wrong. At her, with a sword, coming along the slip toward a dozen men with billhooks.

Dunnock had seen her too. Dunnock lifted his billhook.

"*No.*" Tobin got his stave out across the man's chest before he'd decided to. "No. Let them by."

"She's crown…"

"She's not anybody's." He didn't look at Dunnock. He looked at her. "Let them by."

They let them by. Dunnock stood back with his billhook down and his face saying he'd remember it.

She came along past the end of the barricade, past the boat on its side, with Tam at the foot of the door, and Tobin stood at the end of it with his stave and watched her come. She didn't look frightened. She didn't look anything. When she was level with him she stopped.

"I didn't know about the camp," he said.

He hadn't meant to say it first. It came up out of him like the smoke.

"The tents by the meadows. The ones from over the river." He swallowed. His throat was raw with it. "Nobody said about the camp. Reede didn't. Nobody. Millbrook rises, holds the town, the knights get caught, that's what they said. Nobody said anybody was going down the back channel at dawn to burn the camp." He looked at Odo on the door. At the legs. "Nobody said about any of it."

"No," she said. "They don't."

That was all. *They don't.* As if she'd known it for years. Perhaps she had. She'd always known things before him and not said.

He stood there.

"Go on," he said. "Get them out."

She went on past him, toward the punts below the slip. He watched her go. He thought: *you've told her you didn't know. Fine. That's you clean. That's you a boy who didn't know.* And he thought of Ludd in the tithe barn four nights ago, on an upturned trough with the lantern beside him, telling the ring-captains in a low voice what Tobin wasn't supposed to have heard, because Tobin had been in the next stall with the horses and nobody ever noticed where Tobin was unless he was talking.

*Callis has had the seal since the summer. Reede gave it him. The river gate, the night after Millbrook. Third hour.*

Callis. Who'd ridden ahead of him at Ferriby on a good horse with a hound at his stirrup, while Tobin sat a cob at the back. Who'd looked at Tobin for two years as you'd look at a gate you'd already shut.

"Maren."

She stopped. She didn't turn round. She'd done that at the hedge, too.

"The friend," he said. Low. Fast, before he could think better of it. "With the seal. The one whose paper cleared the river for the barges, and whose men rode it. The one who's to let them in." He stopped. He breathed. The smoke went into him and he nearly coughed and didn't. "It's Callis."

She turned round then.

He told her the rest. Since the summer, since before Upper Rush; Reede; the inner circle and the better names; the frost fair; her. Tomorrow night, the river gate, the third hour. Under the school. He heard himself telling it, very fast, as if he were reading it off a bill.

"I don't know what's down there," he said. His voice went. He let it. "Nobody's told me. I'm only telling you."

"Why?"

Because you came to the hedge, he thought. Because you stood at the back in your father's cloak with your hood up and listened to all of it and still went home. Because of a kettle on a tripod.

"Because you came to the hedge," he said. "And because of the camp." He turned back to the barricade. "Go on. Get Tam home."

He didn't watch the punt go. He heard it. The pole going in and coming out of the water, *chock*, *chock*, under the bank, going up toward the ferry house in the dark. Tam poling. He knew Tam's stroke. Then the fire got louder and he couldn't hear it.

Dunnock was looking at him.

"What did you tell her?"

"Where to find a boat."

"Took you long enough."

"I talk," said Tobin. "Everybody says. Ask anyone." He put his stave back on the boat's keel and looked up the burning street at nothing. "Hold the slip."

They held it till the jetty road had burned down to a street of red ribs, and nothing came down it, and then they went away through the back lanes in the dark with their eyes streaming, out past the last of the gardens and up across the fields to Fennick's.

---

On the next night Ludd picked the men for the boats.

He did it at dusk, on Fennick's top field, under the long thorn hedge where the lanterns had hung three weeks ago in a line like beads. There weren't any lanterns now. There were a hundred or so of them, sitting in the wet grass, black to the elbows, the ones who hadn't gone home. Ludd walked along the line and touched men on the shoulder. *You. You. You.* Big men. Men who'd been soldiers, some of them, or said they had. Men who could keep their mouths shut in a boat.

He came along the line to Tobin and looked at him, and went past.

"Not you," he said, over his shoulder. "You talk."

They went down the hill in the last of the light, the ones he'd touched, to the back channel, where the punts and the eel-boats had been pulled up out of the fire. Tobin watched them go. He'd thought he'd be picked. Then he'd been glad he wasn't. Then he'd sat in the wet grass and hated being glad, and been glad anyway.

He didn't know if she'd got the word into Greyhold. He didn't know if anybody had believed her. He didn't know if Callis was standing at the river gate at this moment with his arm in a sling and the seal in his good hand, or in the cells under the gate with Rowan looking at him. He didn't know anything. He sat under the hedge and pulled the grass out of the ground a blade at a time.

---

Greyhold's bells began a little after the third hour.

He heard them come across the fields from two leagues off, thin, like a fork on a pan. Ringing and ringing. The whole field sat up. Men stood. Somebody said, "That's it, that's them, they're in," and somebody else said, "Or they're not, and that's the alarm," and they argued about it under their breath in the dark, as if that made any difference at two leagues.

Tobin stood at the hedge with his hand on the thorn and listened.

*Get in*, he thought. *Get in, get in, get in.* And then, *don't*. Both. At once. As if he were two people in one coat.

The bells stopped.

All at once. In the middle of a stroke. He heard the last one start and not finish.

Nobody said anything. There wasn't anything to say. Whoever had the bell-tower now, it wasn't the bell-ringer.

---

At midnight the ground moved.

He was lying down by then, in the lee of the hedge, with his coat over his head, not asleep. He felt it come up through the grass under his back. A long low shudder, deep down, as if the whole hill had been struck from underneath with a beam. The thorn hedge shook. All of it, along its whole length, every twig, with a sound like rain.

He sat up.

Then from upriver, a long way, a noise. He'd never heard anything like it and he couldn't have said what it was like afterwards when people asked him, so he said a barn, which wasn't right. A barn coming down, a long way off, and going on coming down. Going on and on, as if there were a barn under the barn, and another under that, all the way down.

Men were standing up all over the field.

"The Ruin," somebody said. "That's from the Ruin."

Tobin stood up with them and looked upriver into the dark, where the island was, five leagues off. There was nothing to see. There was a light over that way, low, in the sky. A dull red. Then nothing.

"What's the Ruin got to do with it?" said Kemble. "The gate's at Greyhold. The gate's at Greyhold, the boats went to Greyhold, what's the Ruin got to do with…"

"Nobody knows," said Tobin. "Nobody's told anybody. Didn't you hear? That's the plan. That's the whole plan. Nobody tells anybody, and then the ground falls in."

Somebody told him to shut up. He shut up.

---

The light came up grey over the Saltreach hills, and he was on the top of the field at the hedge when it came out of the river.

He saw the water first. Away up the valley, past Greyhold, past Redwatch's tower, where the island should have been. There was no island. There was a whiteness on the river. Steam, he thought, or spray, a great deal of it, going up and up, and under it the river was the wrong shape: it went into a hole. He could see it go in. Both channels of the Wend, the near one and the far one, bending in toward the middle and going down into something.

Then the thing came up out of the hole.

He didn't understand the size of it. He watched it come up a while before he understood it, because there was nothing near it to measure it by except the river, and then he saw Redwatch's tower beside it, the tower he'd ridden past a dozen times on a cob at the back of a patrol, and the tower was a peg in the bank next to it. The thing was grey. Grey as the cliff under Greyhold. It came up out of the river with the water going off it in sheets, white, and it had a head, and a mane like a ridge of rock, and it was a lion. It was a lion the size of a hill.

It opened wings.

He heard himself make a noise. Everyone on the hill was making noises.

The wings went out over the river from bank to bank, slowly, as if they'd been folded since before anybody was born. A good while after, a wind came down the valley across the fields and went through the thorn hedge beside him, and the grass of the top field lay over in one long wave.

It went up.

North-east. Over the Saltreach bank, over Harrow's End, over the dark of the Fellwood and the hills behind it, slowly and then faster, with the sun coming up under it and catching it on the belly, until it was a mountain in the sky, and a hill, and a bird. Then nothing.

Along Greyhold's wall, two leagues off, there'd been lights all night. The wall-lamps. They'd been dark half the winter, guttering, and at midnight they'd come on all at once, white and hard and too bright, and he'd thought, *they've mended it, whatever it was.* Now, as the speck went out of the sky over the Saltreach hills, they went out. All of them, all along the wall, at once.

He looked down at Fennick's farm below the hill, where a lamp hung on a bracket over the dairy door, one of the crown's licensed ones that Fennick had paid for and grumbled about. It had been dark since midwinter, and in the night it had come on by itself, white, and frightened the dog. It was dark.

The field was very quiet.

"Was that it?" said Kemble.

Tobin didn't answer.

"Was that what was under the hill?" said Kemble. "Is that…" He was a grown man with grey in his beard and his voice went up like a boy's. "Is that the one realm? Is that what it looks like? It's gone. It's gone off. Where's it gone?"

*Every one of you chosen*, Vale had said, on the wain, with his arms out. *The one realm, come again.* Tobin looked at his hands. At the ring on his finger, black with the smoke from the jetty road. Nothing had chosen him in the night. Nothing had come down out of the sky and looked at him. The beast had come up out of the river and gone, and it hadn't looked at anybody.

"I don't know," he said. "Nobody's told me."

---

Nobody stopped them on the road.

That was the strange thing, after. The rising came down off Fennick's hill in twos and threes over the next days and went home, those who had homes, and the knights didn't come for them. The knights were burying people. You could see them from the River Road, on the west slope under Greyhold's wall, a long line of spades going up and down in the thin spring sun, knights and grooms and Millbrook people all together, and nobody asking who'd been on which side of a barricade. There was too much digging for that.

Tobin went up on the fourth day, with half of Millbrook, because the word had gone round that they'd hung the leader on the gate.

He kept his hood up. He walked in the middle of a crowd of women with baskets and nobody looked at him. He'd been gone two months. He'd been thinner when he left and he was thinner now and he'd stopped shaving, mostly because there was nothing to shave with, and the beard wasn't much of a beard but it was something. Nobody at Greyhold had ever looked at the back of the unchosen line long enough to know his face anyway. He'd told Maren that once. She'd said *I know your face*, and he'd said *you're the back of the line too, that doesn't count.*

The body was under the charter.

They'd hung it by the arms, in ordinary dray-chain, from a bracket over the gate, and the arms were stretched up and the head hung forward. There was a board round its neck. Black letters, painted, big enough to read from the far bank. THE RESTORATION'S LEADER. Over it, cut deep in the gatehouse wall where it had been since before anybody's grandfather, the line about the bonded and the unbonded serving as equals. He'd walked under it every day for two years. He'd hated it every day for two years. Somebody had a sense of humour, putting the board just there. Or nobody had, and that was worse.

He looked at the face.

It was younger than he'd thought. The hair was short and dark and the face was long and pale and the mouth was a little open. He'd seen that face laugh out loud on a hay-wain in the torchlight with seven hundred people roaring back at it. He'd seen it jump down into the mud and take an old woman's hand in both of its own and call her *mother*. He'd seen it say *I forgive Calder Fenn*, and its voice had broken.

It wasn't laughing now.

Beside him a Millbrook woman with a basket said, "That's never him."

"It's him," said someone.

"It's never. My sister's man saw him on the Way this morning, alive as you. Walking south. All over mud, like he'd come up out of the ground."

"That's the other one."

"What other one?"

"There's two. Didn't you know? There was always two. One for the hedges and one for the knife." The man said it with relish, as if he'd known for years. "That's how he was in two places. Everybody knew."

Tobin looked at the face under the charter.

The hedges, he thought. *This* was the hedges. The warm one. The one who'd forgiven the farmer. The one who'd said every one of you chosen and meant it, or looked as if he meant it, which on a wain at night came to the same. And the other one, the one for the knife, the still one, whoever he was, the one Tobin had never seen, had come up out of the ground and walked south down the Way all over mud.

And left this one here.

---

There were fresh graves on the west slope, a long row of them, and a woman by the gate was naming the dead to anyone who'd stand still.

"Captain Thorne," she said. "At the gate. With her boar. Warden Hale, the old archivist. Him in the archive, by the door, with a sword-cut in him, an old man like that. The Haverly boy on the north stair. Him and his bird."

Tobin had been turning away. He stopped.

"Haverly," he said.

"Lucan Haverly. The young knight. Held the stair an hour, they say, him and his falcon, so nobody could follow them down. Then the falcon got a bolt and he got a spear." She looked at him. "You knew him?"

He thought of Haverly at the practice posts, doing something quick and clever with his feet that made the Darrow girl laugh. Haverly on the bench at Pell's lesson, with Kesh on his fist, not looking at Tobin at the other end. Haverly in his new grey cloak at the front of the patrol to Ferriby, and Tobin on a cob at the back, thinking, *that's what a beast does for you. That's the cloak and the horse and the front.*

He'd called Haverly things. Mostly behind his back. Some to his face.

"No," said Tobin. "Not really."

He went back down the hill.

At the bottom of it, on the River Road, a carter from Redwatch was telling a crowd how the knight's girl, Ashdown, the one they'd thrown out, had come up out of the river at dawn on a lion. Not the big one. A lion of her own, grey, the size of a horse. She'd been under the ground all night with it, and come up, and the knights had come down to the reeds with their great chain to take it and looked at her and gone home.

"She broke a knight's arm, that one," said a woman. "On the island. In the spring. With a practice sword."

"It was blunt," said Tobin.

They looked at him.

"The sword." His mouth was doing it without him. "It was blunt. It was mine. I left it her. The grip's wound with blue cord, I did it myself, the school's leather makes your hand sweat." He stopped. "She broke Callis's arm with my sword."

The woman looked at him a while.

"Well," she said. "You'll not be getting it back."

Ludd sent him down the river in the spring.

It wasn't a reward. Tobin understood that. The knights hadn't come for anybody yet, but they'd come in the end, and the ones who'd been seen at the barricades were better somewhere else, and a boy who'd been at Greyhold two years and walked out of it was the kind of thing a knight remembers. And there was work down the river. Ludd said so in the tithe barn, with a bill in his hand that had come over the river from the Cross in a fisherman's punt.

"When the silver comes up from the coast," he said, "they're coming over. The companies. Three thousand. Into Hearthmere, where there's not a ward or a word left standing. The one realm doesn't stop at the river, lad." He tapped the bill. "They'll want men on this side who know the fords and the Downs. Who'll show them which farms are ours."

"Three thousand what?" said Tobin.

"Men."

"What kind of men?"

Ludd gave him the look from the top field. *You talk.*

"Our kind," he said.

---

The ring-house was a shepherd's hut on the down above the fords.

The drovers called them the Ashford fords, because the road that came up out of them went west to Ashford, two days' walk, and nobody went anywhere else from them. Long shallow reaches of the Wend, brown water and shingle, with the current going over the stones in a long low noise you stopped hearing after a day. The Brackenwood behind, north, dark on its ridge. The Candle Downs going away west, fold on fold, chalk and grass and hedges, with a chapel tower in every fold.

There were six of them in the hut. Tobin, Kemble, two brothers from a farm near the Brackenwood who never said anything, an old hedge-preacher who'd been fined at Ashford three times and was proud of it, and a woman called Moll Prentice who'd walked down from Hallowmere with a ring on her finger and a bad cough and Reede's book in her apron. They had a fire, and a sack of meal, and bacon from a farm a day and a half west under Wether Down, where a man called Ned Pollard wore a ring and had told them, the first week, that whatever he had was the one realm's.

"When they come," Pollard said, "they can have my barn. I mean it. Let the knights come and look at it then."

He had a wife, and a daughter of about twenty who didn't like any of them, and a boy of fourteen called Hal who followed Tobin round the yard asking about Greyhold. What the beasts were like close to. What it felt like, the trial day, standing in the yard waiting to be looked at. Whether it was true there was a lion under the school.

"Not under it now," said Tobin.

"Was it you that let it out?"

"No," said Tobin. "Nobody let it out. It let itself out. Nobody had any say in it at all. That's the one thing everyone keeps getting wrong."

Hal didn't understand that. Tobin didn't blame him.

---

The bills came over in a fish-basket.

A Saltreach boy brought them across the fords every new moon on a pony, with a layer of salt herring on top, and they took the herring and passed the bills out along the Downs. Reede's press at the Cross. Big black letters, the six hands in a ring at the top. THE CROWN'S LAMPS WERE A CRIME. THE CROWN BLED A BEAST FOR THREE HUNDRED YEARS AND CALLED IT THE GODS' GIFT. LOOK AT YOUR LAMPS NOW.

He nailed them up on chapel doors. It wasn't hard to make anybody look at their lamps now. Every lamp on the Downs was dark. The licensed ones over the inn doors, the warming-stones in the chapel floors, the words on the well-hoods: nothing. He watched a farm wife at a well near Ashford say the sweet-word over her bucket a dozen times, louder and louder, and then throw the bucket down the well after it.

The bills were true. That was the trouble. Every word on them was true, and it didn't help anybody at all.

---

The first lion story came over in the fish-basket too, by mouth, with the herring.

"She went through the wall," said the Saltreach boy, sitting on the hut step with a mug of their small beer. "At Greyhold. The knights took the farmer's kin, the dragon farmer's, his mother and his sister and the little ones, and shut them up in a granary in the inner ward with a guard on the door. And in the night the lion walked up to the old wall and the wall opened in front of it like a door. Like it was a door. Stone. And she walked in and walked them out and her mother rowed them over the river in the dark." He drank. "Walked through the wall."

"She did not walk through a wall," said Tobin.

"Did."

"She didn't. She'd want to know who was going to pay for it after. She'd want it written down." He was laughing. "Was there a report? There'll have been a report. *The wall was opened on the eighth night by Student Ashdown with the beast's leave.*"

The boy looked at him as if he were mad.

"You know her?" said Kemble.

"I sat next to her at porridge for two years," said Tobin. "She ate it in the same order every day. Edges first."

That night, lying in the hut with the noise of the fords coming up through the floor, he thought about the wall opening. The old grey stone. He'd leaned on it every noon at Greyhold, facing it, saying the words with the rest. *I will keep the watch below.* He'd said it two years without asking what was below, because nobody asked. And it had been that. That thing, the size of a hill. Under his feet. And the wall had been for keeping it in.

And now a lion had opened the wall like a door, for a farmer's family. For a granary.

That one stayed with him. It wasn't the biggest story he'd hear. But he lay there and saw it, the old grey stones moving apart in the dark like two men stepping out of each other's way, and couldn't stop.

---

The silver came up from the coast at the beginning of the summer, and the companies came over.

They came at night, the short nights, when everybody in Hearthmere was looking the other way. Everybody was looking east that month. The crown's knights had gone over into the Wyldmarch after the beast with the great chain on six wains, and the stories came back across the Wend every week: the chain, the forest, the knights on the grass plains, a ring of stones. Nobody was watching the fords.

The six of them stood on the Hearthmere bank with lanterns.

That was all they had to do. Stand at the edges of the shallows with a light, so the men coming over knew where the ford went and where the deep channel was. Tobin stood on a shingle spit in the river to his knees with his lantern held up, and the water pulled at his legs, and out of the dark on the Saltreach side came the companies.

He'd thought there'd be songs. Because of the hedge, maybe, and seven hundred people going down the hill singing the river lullaby. There weren't any songs. There was the sound of water round a great many legs, and men swearing at the cold, and the clink of crossbows and pikes being carried high, and a horse screaming somewhere when it lost its footing. They came over in a long dark line that didn't end, man after man, wet to the waist, past his lantern. Coats with colours he could just see. A black ship on grey. A red hand. A green boar. They didn't look at him. One of them took his lantern off him without asking, to light his pipe at it, and gave it back.

"Welcome," said Tobin. To the next one. To any of them. "Welcome to Hearthmere. To the one realm. Welcome."

"Where's the nearest granary?" said the man.

---

He showed them.

He'd been told to. He knew the Downs by then, a whole spring of nailing bills to chapel doors. He knew which farms were ours. He walked at the head of the black-ship company for a day and a half west along the drove-roads in the summer heat with the chalk dust coming up white round his boots, and the captain of the black-ship company, a long sunburned man from Gullhaven with a voice like a cart going over cobbles, rode beside him on a horse that had been somebody else's until that morning and asked him questions.

How many in the household. How many beasts. How many hands. Was there a dog.

"Pollard's ours," Tobin said. "He'll give you whatever he has. He said so. He said you can have his barn."

"Good," said the captain. "That saves time."

---

Pollard came out to the gate to meet them.

He'd seen them coming down the side of Wether Down, a long line of them on the drove-road, two hundred, and he came out to the gate in his best coat with his ring on his hand, and his wife behind him in the yard, and Hal on the barn step. He held up his hand with the ring on it. Tobin saw him do it. He held it up like a man holding up a lamp.

"You're welcome," said Pollard. "You're all welcome. Whatever I've got."

"Grand," said the captain.

They took the grain. That was expected. Two barns of it, the old year's and what was left of the last, and they loaded it onto Pollard's own wains with Pollard's own forks while Pollard stood and watched and said "That's right, that's right, the far bay's the good barley." They took the bacon off the hooks in the kitchen. They took the hens. They took the two horses and the cart. Pollard said, "That's right," and then he stopped saying it.

They took the plough-oxen.

Two red oxen, old, with brass on the tips of their horns, standing in the home paddock. Pollard went white when the first man went into the paddock with a rope.

"Not the team," he said. "Captain. You can't. Not the team. They're the plough. I'll not get a crop in without the…"

"They'll eat," said the captain.

"I'll not get a crop."

"Then you'll not need a plough."

The man with the rope had the near ox by the horns. Pollard went toward the paddock gate and two men stepped in front of him and he stopped. His wife had her hand on his arm. She didn't say anything. She was looking at the barn, at the little door under the hoist-beam, and Tobin looked where she was looking and saw a company man coming out of the little door with a sack on his shoulder, and another behind him, and another. Seed-corn. Tobin knew without being told. The seed for the autumn, kept back, kept dry, up in the loft where nobody would think to look. Except somebody had.

"That's the seed," said Pollard's wife. Very quietly. To nobody. "That's next year."

---

It was Hal who did it.

Tobin saw him go and didn't understand what he'd seen until afterwards, when he went over it, which he did most nights after. The boy came down off the barn step with a hay-fork. Not fast. Walking. Across the yard toward the man with the near ox on a rope, with the fork held low in both hands, as you'd carry it to a hay-cart, and his face perfectly calm.

"Put him back," said Hal. "That's my dad's."

The man with the ox turned round and laughed.

Somebody at the gate shot him.

Not the man with the ox. The boy. A crossbowman by the gatepost, leaning on it, who'd been eating an apple off Pollard's tree. Tobin heard the bolt go. A short flat sound like a door shutting in the wind. Hal sat down in the yard. He sat down, with the fork across his knees, as if he'd decided to have a rest, and looked down at his shirt.

Then he lay down.

Pollard made a noise Tobin had never heard a man make. He went across the yard and the two men in front of him didn't stop him this time. He got down in the muck beside his boy and got his arms under him.

"Oh, for the love of…" said the captain. He turned his horse. "Who did that? Gedge? Was that you?"

"Had a fork," said the crossbowman.

"He's a child."

"Had a fork, Captain."

The captain looked at Hal on the ground with his father over him. He looked a while. Then he looked at the sun.

"Get the team on the road," he said. "And fire the barns when you're done. I'll not leave a bushel for the knights."

---

They fired the barns.

Tobin stood at the gate. He didn't remember going there. He was standing at the gatepost where the crossbowman had been, and the apple core was on the ground at his feet with the bite marks in it, and the barns were going up behind the house in two tall columns of white smoke with fire at the roots.

Pollard was still in the yard with the boy.

Pollard's wife had come to the gate. She was standing in front of Tobin, near enough to touch. She wasn't crying. She was looking at his hand. At the ring on it. Plain iron, on the third finger of his right hand, where Ludd had put it on midwinter's eve in Fennick's tithe barn and said *now you're one of us*. She looked at it as you'd look at a stain.

Then she looked at Pollard's hand, on the ground, on the boy's shirt. Tobin looked too.

Pollard's ring was gone. Somebody had had it off him. Some time in the last hour, in the yard, among the sacks and the hens, while he was saying *that's right, that's right*. Iron's iron. Somebody would get a copper for it at the Cross.

"You said friends," said Pollard's wife. "You sat in my kitchen and ate my bacon and said friends."

He opened his mouth.

He'd talked his whole life. He'd talked at Greyhold, on the bench, in the yard, on patrol, at the back of every line, loud, about everything. He'd talked to drovers at roadblocks. He'd talked on the top field under the hedge, and at the barricade, and to the companies coming over the river in the dark: *welcome, welcome to the one realm*. He opened his mouth and there was nothing in it at all.

She went back across the yard to her husband.

The black-ship company went off down the drove-road toward the next farm with the red oxen walking in the middle of it and the brass on their horns catching the sun. The captain looked round for Tobin from his horse, to show them the way.

Tobin was gone.

---

He walked back to the fords through the night.

A day and a half's walk, and he did it in a night and a morning, without stopping, along the drove-roads in the dark with the chalk showing white under his feet. He didn't think. He didn't let himself. He walked, and when he started to think he walked faster, until his chest hurt and he was half running, like the last lap of the yard at Greyhold with the instructor shouting.

The hut on the down was empty. Kemble and the others had gone west with the red-hand company. There was a cold fire and the meal sack and a heap of Reede's bills on the bench.

He went down past it to the river.

It was early. The fords were grey and empty. The water went over the shingle with its long low noise, the same noise it had made all spring, as if nobody had come over it in the night with crossbows. He walked out into it. To his knees. To his thighs. Out past the shingle spit where he'd stood with the lantern, to the edge of the deep channel, where the brown water went black and fast.

He took the ring off.

It didn't want to come. His finger had swollen in the heat. He had to twist it, and it scraped the skin off his knuckle going over, and there was a band under it round his finger, white, where the sun hadn't been since the spring. He looked at the band. He looked at the ring in his palm. It was a little thing. Plain iron. Odo Marl might have made it, or Tam. He'd been so pleased with it. He'd stopped wearing it on a string under his shirt because he was pleased with it, and wanted it seen.

He threw it in the deep channel.

It didn't make much of a splash. He didn't see where it went. The river had it and that was all.

He stood in the water with his hand open until he couldn't feel his legs. Then he came back out onto the shingle with his boots full and his breeches black to the hip, and sat down on the bank, and couldn't think of a single place in the six kingdoms to go.

---

He went west. Because it wasn't east.

All that summer the Downs burned. The companies went north through them village by village, stripping, and behind them the smoke stood up off the chalk in columns you could see from three ridges away. Where the smoke was, he went the other way. He slept in hedges. He ate what he could get. He worked a day here and a day there for bread at farms the companies hadn't reached yet, mending hurdles, carrying water, saying nothing about where he'd come from. He found he could say nothing now. It was new. He wasn't sure he liked it.

He heard the second story in a chapel at a place called Coombe Ashley, in high summer, with the floor full of knights.

There'd been a fight in the village street. The knights had come down off the ridge at dawn on the red-hand company while it was loading a granary, a few dozen of them, greyharts and horses and hounds, and it had been steel, all steel, as in the old songs, because there was nothing else on the Downs that worked any more. The company had gone off west with half its wains. The knights had kept the village and their dead and their wounded, and the wounded were laid out in rows on the chapel floor on straw, on the cold warming-stones that didn't warm.

Tobin carried water. Nobody asked who he was. A boy in a torn shirt with a bucket was nobody.

A groom was holding a knight's head up so he could drink. A Greyhold groom. Tobin knew him: Ewan, from the Long Barn, who'd mucked out beside Maren every morning for a year and said maybe ten words to her. Ewan didn't know him. Ewan looked straight at him over the wounded man's head and saw a bucket.

"Were you at the Ring?" said the wounded man. To Ewan. He was feverish and wanted to talk. "You were. I saw you. With the wains."

"I was there."

"Tell it, then. Tell it me again. I can't remember it right."

---

Ewan told it.

He told it to the man on the straw, but he told it loud enough, because he wanted to be heard. Tobin knew that. He'd done it himself.

"There was this ring of stones," Ewan said. "On the plains. In the Wyldmarch. A league of grey ground round it with nothing growing, dead as Hallowmere, and the thing lying in the middle of the stones with its head on its paws, the big one, the one from the river. We had the chain. Midsummer night. Isvaren's men up on the stones with their lamps, making a net of light over its back, white, you never saw anything like it. And us under it with the chain in loops, running, pinning it down to stakes. And then somebody's burning honey-smoke upwind, the whole sky's sweet with it, and nobody knows who." He wiped the man's mouth. "And then she comes."

"Ashdown."

"Ashdown. With the lion. Up the stones after Isvaren's men and knocks them off into the beck, one, two, and their lights go out. And the lion goes for the stakes." Ewan's voice dropped. "Pulls them. Out of the ground. Iron stakes as long as a man and driven a yard down, and he pulls them up like carrots out of sand. One after the other. And on the other line of stakes, the same. Another lion. The same lion. Two of him. I saw them both. Both at once, a hundred yards apart, pulling stakes."

"There's never two."

"There were two. I'm telling you. I've not drunk since midsummer and I'm telling you."

The man on the straw laughed and coughed.

"And the big one gets up," said Ewan. "And the stones fall in. All of them. Into the ground. And the captain's under the chain with her leg broke, and who pulls her out? Ashdown does. The lion lifts the chain off her with his head and Ashdown pulls her out by the arms." He was quiet a moment. "Then a week after, there's a lake, out in the forest, where the clans meet. And something comes up out of the forest. A stag as big as a hill, they said, with antlers on it like a whole wood in winter, and the trees getting up out of the ground and walking along with it. And every beast in that forest running for the lake. And she held a lane against it. Her and the lion. And there were two of him again, in two lanes, half a morning." He laid the man's head down. "The clans were saying it when we left. Two lions. They had a word for her."

"What word?"

Ewan shrugged. "Couldn't say it. Clan word."

---

Rowan came into the chapel at noon.

Tobin was at the back with his bucket and saw her come. Small. Neat. Grey hair. On foot, which surprised him, with a stick, and her left leg strapped out straight from hip to ankle in leather and slats so that she walked like a man on a peg. She went along the rows of straw and stopped at every man and said something to every one. Nobody stood up. She told them not to.

He'd stood in her yard at noon every day for two years. She'd read the council's order to them in the snow, the day Davin was taken, in her quiet voice that carried to the back without being raised. *That is the order. It is not mine. I don't make it.*

He put his head down over his bucket and went out of the side door before she got to the back of the chapel. He didn't think she'd know him. He didn't want to find out.

Outside in the street there was a dead horse, and a woman sweeping glass from a broken window with her back to it, and a hound lying in the shade of the chapel wall with its chin on its paws, waiting for somebody who was inside on the straw or wasn't. He sat down against the wall at the other end from the hound.

Two lions, he thought. Two of him. In two places at once.

The man in two places. That had been Vale. The legend of Vale, the hedge-legend, every alehouse on the river: *he spoke at Wendmouth and the Marches the same night*. Tobin had believed it. He'd loved believing it. And it had been two brothers in two coats, one for the hedges and one for the knife, and one of them had been hung on Greyhold's gate.

And now there was Ashdown, and two lions. And a knight's groom who'd mucked out beside her for a year and never said ten words to her telling it on a chapel floor as if it were scripture.

The thing was, Ewan wasn't lying. Tobin had heard enough lies in a year to know the taste of them. Ewan had seen two lions.

*When they throw you out,* he'd said to her. At the gate of the field, under the thorn hedge, with the lanterns going out behind him. *When they throw you out of the gate with nothing, come and find us. We'll still be here.*

They'd thrown her out. He'd been right about that. He'd been right about the one thing and wrong about everything else. She'd gone out of the gate with nothing and come back with a lion, and pulled Rowan out from under a chain, and gone on to a lake to stand in front of a forest. And where was *us*? *Us* was a captain from Gullhaven asking how many dogs. *Us* was an apple core by a gatepost.

The hound at the far end of the wall lifted its head and looked at him, and put it down again.

"Fine," said Tobin, out loud. "Fine. I'll come and find you."

The hound didn't think much of that. Neither did he. He got up anyway.

---

He didn't know where she was. That was the first thing.

Nobody knew where she was. That was the point of her, it seemed. By the time a story about her came over the river she'd moved on, and the story got bigger without her, like a fire with the pot taken off. All the rest of the summer he followed the stories round the Downs, and they went everywhere.

She was at a lake in the Wyldmarch. She was in the Thornwood. She'd gone home to Greyhold. She'd been taken by Isvaren. She'd been seen on the King's Road at Ashford on a lion the size of a barn, which she hadn't, because Ashford was the companies' now and the companies would have sold her to the Tide Council by the pound. He met a man at a well who'd met a man who'd seen the lion's footprint, and it was the size of a cart-wheel. He met a woman who said she was the old king's granddaughter.

"She's a ferrywoman's daughter from below Greyhold," said Tobin. "Her mother does the river crossing. Her brother's a smith."

"That's what they'd want you to think," said the woman.

He worked the harvest that was left. Where the companies hadn't been yet, the grey wheat was in, and he walked behind the reapers with the gleaners and took the bread they paid in and ate it in the hedge-bottoms at night with his boots off. His boots were going. He'd had them at Greyhold, school boots, and they'd done the yard and the patrols and the barricade and the fords and a whole summer of chalk, and the left one was opening at the toe like a mouth.

At the end of the summer a carter came south down the Downs from the Pass road with salt and news.

"Lion at Vael," he said. "At the gate of Vael, up under the High Crown. Came down over the mountains with a herd of tuskers walking with it like dogs. And a woman on it. The College has her now." He spat. "They'll register her. They register everything up there. They'll have her in a book by kind and weight and what she's worth."

So Tobin went north.

Up the Downs, toward the Pass road, a ridge at a time. Twelve days to Vael from Hallowmere, the carter said, over the Iron Fells, and the Pass open till the snow. He had no money and his boot was open and he didn't know a soul in Isvaren and wouldn't have been let into Vael through the front gate if he'd had all three. He went anyway. It was better than standing still.

He got to the last chalk ridge before the King's Road at the north edge of the Downs, and from the top of it he could see, away north across the valley of the Mere, the line of the Iron Fells against the sky. And they were white.

The end of summer, and the Fells were white to the foot.

A drover on the ridge with a flock of thin ewes told him. Snow on the Pass a month early. Shut. Nobody over till the spring. "Never seen it," the drover said. "Not at the back end of summer. My father never saw it. Like the whole north's gone cold at once."

Tobin sat on the ridge with his open boot and looked at the white Fells till the sun was off the ridge. The ewes went past him down the hill.

"Fine," he said again, to nobody. He was saying that a lot. He got up and went back.

---

He went back to the Marches because of the letters.

It came to him on the way down the Downs, in a hedge, at night, when he couldn't sleep for the cold. All summer he'd been following stories. Stories went everywhere and came from nowhere and got bigger as they went. But there was one place in the six kingdoms where stories stopped being stories.

Liss Ashdown's ferry house. Below Greyhold. With the red door.

She'd write to her mother. Maren would. She was the kind who wrote reports. Somewhere, every month, wherever the lion was, she'd be sitting down with a stub of pencil writing *Mother. I am at such a place. The lion is well.* And it'd go down the roads and the rivers and end up on the table in the ferry house kitchen, and Liss would read it out, because Liss told everybody everything, and by night every boatman on the landing would know.

He came back down off the Downs in the first of the autumn and found the Marches under water.

---

The flood had been and gone.

He hadn't known. Nobody on the Downs had known. A wall of brown water had come down the Wend out of the north, out of the mountains, the boatmen said, out of a lake that had broken its shore, and gone through the Marches in a night and a day, and the river was back in its banks now but the Marches weren't. The water-meadows were mud and wrack. The Eelweir on the far bank was gone, houses and all; you could see the stumps. There were drowned sheep in the hedges. The late barley was flat in the fields and black, and the whole valley smelled of rot and river.

And on the grass under Greyhold's river wall, along the landing, there were people. Hundreds of them. Carts and blankets and hand-barrows, and fires, and children. The Saltreach people who'd come over in the spring, the ones from the camp, the ones the crown had ordered back over the river and nobody had ferried back. They'd been there all summer. The flood had come up round them and gone down again and they were still there, waiting, because there was nowhere else.

Tobin walked in among them with his hood up, and sat down by somebody's fire, and nobody asked him anything.

---

He was there through the start of the autumn.

He did what they did. Queued at the river for water, because the wells were sour. At the end, took the Clan's grain, when it began to come down the Way in wagons and over on the barge, a measure a head, from hard men in grey cloaks who looked at him as if they knew exactly what he'd been. Helped a man from Upper Rush rebuild a hurdle shelter. Kept his hood up, always, when the knights came down from the gate. There weren't many knights now. Rowan had taken most of them to the Downs. The ones left on the wall were old, or hurt, or students. He saw a boy he knew by sight up on the river wall, a student with a marten on his shoulder, and turned his back.

From the end of the landing he could see Millbrook, two leagues downriver.

Half of it. The jetty road had new thatch on it, yellow, in a row among the black, and new timber in the gaps, raw and pale. The Kettle had a roof again. Smoke from chimneys. The forge at the top of the street had smoke too, a thin grey line going straight up on still mornings. Odo, he thought. Or Tam.

He didn't go down. He didn't go anywhere near it. He sat at the end of the landing in the mornings and looked at the yellow thatch, and thought about the night it had burned with him holding the bottom of the street, and then he got up and queued for water.

He didn't go to the ferry house either.

He could see it. From the landing, a little way along the bank, with its stone jetty and its red door, and the ferry on its rope going over and back, and a woman on the jetty with a coil of new rope over her shoulder, shouting at the barge-hands. Every day. He could have walked there in a quarter-hour. He didn't. He could think of the first thing she'd say, and the second, and every one of them was true.

---

The Vael story came down the river with the upper valley's people, who'd come down ahead of the water.

"There were two dragons," said a woman from somewhere up the Wend, at the fire, with a baby that wouldn't stop. "A red and a green, flying ahead of the flood, landing in every town the day before and telling us to get up the hill. A man on the red one. If it weren't for them I'd be in the river." She shifted the baby. "And they said it came out of a lake at Vael. And a lion stood in the street there and held the water back with a wall. Raised a wall out of the street, out of the cobbles, high as a house, and held it."

"With the woman," somebody said.

"With the woman. And then half the city drowned anyway, but not the half behind the wall."

Tobin, at the edge of the fire, laughed.

They looked at him.

"Sorry," he said. "Sorry. It's only, I keep missing her. Everywhere I go, she's just been." He rubbed his face. "She raised a wall, and I went up the Downs to see, and it snowed."

Nobody knew what he was talking about. The baby went on crying.

That night he lay under somebody's cart and thought about a wall coming up out of a street. A lion that walked through walls in the spring and made them in the autumn. And Maren beside it with her hand on it, he supposed, telling it where.

*Raised a wall.* As if it were nothing. As if it were a hurdle.

He'd wanted, at the hedge, all of them to be chosen. Every potboy and every ferry-hand. A beast at every side. He'd wanted it so much he'd put a ring on for it. And she'd had one beast. One. She hadn't been chosen at the trial or on any day after. She'd gone out to the island all winter on her own to a lion nobody wanted, and sat by it, and gone on sitting by it.

He turned over under the cart and the cart creaked.

---

The letter came in the back end of the autumn.

He knew because the landing knew. It went along the fires before dark like wind along a hedge. One of Liss's barge-hands had it from the kitchen, and told it at the first fire, and it came along the line a fire at a time, getting a little bigger at each one, and when it got to Tobin it was this.

"She's in Kessmoor," said the man from Upper Rush. "Ashdown's girl. The letter's from there. Some place in the iron country, the Scald, where the town burned. The big one's lying down on it, the one from the river. And the Kessmoor men have built a cage round it, a great iron cage with water, and they're going to shut it." He stirred the fire. "The iron place, the hand said. That's what she called it. That's where she is."

"How long ago?"

"What?"

"The letter. When did she write it? How long's it been coming?"

The man looked at him. "How should I know? A month. Less. Ask Mistress Ashdown."

Tobin sat a while.

Then he got up, and went along the landing in the dark to the end of it, where the River Road went up the hill past Greyhold's shut gate. Under the charter there was nothing hanging now. They'd taken the body down some time in the summer. There was only a pale stain on the stone where the board had hung and the rain had run round it.

He went past it, west, on the River Road, toward the King's Road and Hallowmere and whatever was the other side.

He didn't go to the ferry house to say. He thought about it. He stood at the turning a moment and looked back along the bank at the red door, with a lamp in the window. An oil lamp. Yellow. He thought about knocking on the red door and asking a woman whose town he'd helped burn which road her daughter had gone by.

He went on.

---

From the Marches to the Scald was a hundred leagues and more by the roads, and all of it was dead.

He'd known Hearthmere was dead. He'd lived in it since the spring. But on the Downs it had been a thing you noticed at a well or a chapel door, a lamp that didn't light, a word that didn't take. On the King's Road west it was the whole country. He walked it day after day and the country said nothing. The inns every six leagues had dark lamps over their doors and people in their yards drawing water from the river in buckets. The milestones went by every league, grey, with their numbers, and nobody had cleaned them since the spring. The gravel gave out and the road was paved, and then he was in the valley of the Mere, and he could see the capital along its north shore going up in terraces from the water. Hallowmere. Where the lamps had burned in the better streets since the charter, and every street looked down on the lake.

He came along the south shore at dusk and sat on the bank and waited for the lights.

They didn't come. The whole city went dark as the sun went, terrace by terrace, down to the water, and stayed dark. Here and there a yellow point. Oil. A candle. A city the size of all of the Marches put together, sitting on its hill in the dark like a cold hearth, and the Mere in front of it showing the sky, and nothing else.

He didn't go in. He went round it in the night by the lanes, and in the morning he was on the Iron Road.

---

The left sole came off two days past the Mere.

He felt it go. It had been flapping for a week, and he'd been walking on the toe of it, and on a stony bit of the road it caught and tore back to the heel and he was standing on his own stocking on the cold stones. He sat down at the roadside and looked at it.

There was a meal-sack in the ditch. Somebody had dropped it off a cart, or thrown it. He unpicked the string from its mouth with his teeth and tied the sole back on, round and round, under the instep and over, and knotted it, and stood up. It held. It held for half a day, and then he did it again.

The Iron Road went north and west over the moors. There wasn't much on it. Carts, once a day or less, ordinary carts with ordinary horses, going east toward Hallowmere. One was loaded with chain. A whole wain of it, new, black, the links as long as his forearm, under a tarpaulin that had blown back at one corner. Two men in crown-blue riding beside it, and a clerk on the box with a ledger.

"What's it for?" said Tobin, at the roadside, as it went by.

"The crown's work," said the clerk, without looking down.

Tobin stood and watched the chain go east. More chain. For the crown. In a country where the old chain had just come up out of the river in pieces on the legs of a thing the size of a hill.

"Of course it is," he said. "Of course it is. What else would it be for?"

---

At the border there was a hut.

A brick hut by a grey stone in the heather, with Kessmoor's wheel painted on its door and a green lamp on a post outside it that wasn't burning. A clerk inside at a desk, young, with a cap and a cold, and a book open in front of him. He looked up at Tobin in the doorway in his rags and his tied boot as if he'd seen worse, which he probably had.

"Beasts?" said the clerk.

"What?"

"Beasts. To declare. Any beast of power to be papered at the guild-house. And any gauge for the Bleed, or any instrument." He sniffed. "Any bond, any working, any spoken word. It's the Assembly's order."

Tobin looked down at himself. He held his arms out from his sides so the clerk could see all of him. The coat. The string.

"Nobody chose me," he said.

The clerk looked at him a while. Then he wrote something in the book.

"Pass," he said.

---

Kessmoor was dead too, near the Scald. He'd thought it wouldn't be. The carters had said the iron country had its own lights, green ones, that weren't magic. But the moor going west from the border was grey and brown and flat, with nothing on it but heather gone to wire and a few sheep that had died where they stood, and the sky over it was a colour he'd never seen a sky go. Yellow-grey. Low down. Like a bruise going off.

He walked into it for two days.

On the second evening, at dusk, he was sitting at the side of the road eating the last of the Clan's bread when something went over him.

Low. From behind him, from the east. He heard it before he saw it, a sound of wings, heavy, labouring, and looked up, and it was right over him. Green. Small, for what it was. Long and thin, with its wings going hard and its sides going in and out, and its head out in front, low over the moor, heading west along the road in the last of the light.

A dragon.

He sat with the bread in his hand and watched it go. It didn't look at him. It went away west over the moor, labouring, getting smaller, as if it had come a very long way and had a long way still to go and wanted it over.

The dragon farmer had three. Everybody on the river had known it by the end of the winter: a red one, a big old bronze, and a little green one.

The farmer's green one. Going west. Along his road, to where his road went.

"Thank you," said Tobin, to the sky, with his mouth full. "That's very helpful. That's the first straight answer I've had all year."

---

He saw the beast the next day, at noon, from three leagues out.

He'd come over a long low rise in the morning and seen the cordon ahead of him, far off, on the flat. A ring. An earth bank, long and low and grey, going away to both sides, with towers on it here and there, and over it something that smoked. White smoke, lying along the ground in rings. And inside the rings, in the middle, a long way off, under the yellow-grey sky, something dark, lying down.

It was the size of a hill. He knew the size by now. He'd seen it come up out of the river.

He stopped in the road. It was a long way off and it was lying with its head on its paws and it wasn't looking at him, and his legs stopped anyway.

Then it stood up.

He watched it. All of it, from three leagues, with his string-tied boot in the dust of the Iron Road and his hand up over his eyes. It stood, slowly, forelegs first, and then the hind, and stood there with its wings half out and its head up, and turned its head. Round. The whole ring, slowly, as if it were looking at everything in the world one thing at a time. When its head came round toward him he stopped breathing.

It went past.

Then it put its head down to the ground, between its forepaws, and a sound came across the moor to him a little after. A crack. Then a long grinding roar. Then a noise like a great house full of windows, all its windows, breaking one after another and going on breaking. White went up round it, a cloud, like steam, like a whole sky of moths, and he couldn't see it, and then the cloud thinned and its head was down in the ground to the shoulders.

He didn't see what it brought up. It was too far. He saw it lift its head out of the ground with something in its jaws, small, and turn its neck back over its shoulder, and put its head down to its own back. Then its wings went out.

It went up.

West. Away from him. Low, over the far side of the ring, so low that the smoke over the rings boiled up white where it went over, and on out over the moor toward the edge of the sky. He watched it go till it was a speck in the yellow-grey, as he had at Fennick's in the spring, when it had been a mountain and a hill and a bird and nothing.

Twice now. Twice he'd watched it go, from a long way off, with his feet in the road.

He looked at the ring on the flat, with the smoke lying on it.

*She's there,* he thought. *She's there and it's just gone. Of course it has. Of course I've missed it. I've missed everything all year by a day.*

He started walking again. Faster.

---

He came to the cordon at dusk.

The road ran straight across the flat at it and through the bank by a gate, and along the road, the last league, there were lamps. Green ones, on iron posts. They'd been dark when he first saw them. The light was going off the moor now, and they'd been lit, and they were green, sick and soft. Then they weren't.

They went white. All of them, all along the road ahead of him, at once. Hard white, like the wall-lamps at Greyhold on the night the island went. One of them cracked in its glass with a small sharp sound like a knuckle.

And the ground under his tied boot went warm.

He felt it come up into him. Up through the sole, the bad one, into his foot. Warm, and then a sort of buzzing, and then something in his head going light and loose, like the first cup at the Kettle on a fair day when you'd had nothing to eat. He stopped in the road and put his hand out to a lamp-post and it was hot.

The smoke over the rings inside the bank had gone thick. White, boiling up.

And over the bank, from inside, from somewhere along to the left, came a sound. A man laughing. High. Going on too long.

Then another.

Then somebody singing.

---

The gate in the bank was open and there was nobody on it.

A brick gatehouse with an iron rail along the front of it, at the height of a man's hip, the kind you'd tie a horse to. Nobody at it. A cap on the ground. A stool kicked over. Everybody had gone along the bank toward the laughing; he could hear them, shouting, a long way along to the left in the dusk. On the rail, hung over it in a coil, was a rope.

He looked at the rope.

He'd spent two years at Greyhold being told where to go and what to carry. Fetch that. Hold this. Muck out. Stand at the back. Rope, when you heard laughing like that in a ditch, was obvious. Anybody could have told you. Nobody had to.

He took it off the rail and put it over his shoulder.

He went in through the gate.

Up onto the top of the bank. And it opened out under him in the white lamp-light and the smoke: the ring, the rings, a ditch along the outside of the bank and another inside it, and inside that water, smoking, cold-smoking, white, and iron towers going away in the fog, and over everything a high thin ringing, on and on, that he couldn't place, like the harness-bells on the ponies at Millbrook fair, too many of them and a long way off. Away in the middle, where the beast had been, nothing. A broken place, with the white coming up out of it.

And along the bank, along the lip of the inner ditch, a woman. Standing. Looking down. With her hair tied back, and a sword at her hip, and boots wrapped up in sacking to the ankle.

And beside her, standing on the lip with its head down over the ditch, a lion.

Grey. As grey as Greyhold's stone. With a mane like a broken wall. He'd heard it was the size of a barn, the size of a hay-wain, the size of a horse. None of them had been anywhere near. It stood on the bank like a piece of the bank that had decided to be something else, and the woman beside it came up to somewhere about its elbow, and it turned its great head, slowly, and looked at him coming.

She didn't turn round. She was looking into the ditch.

Tobin hitched the rope up on his shoulder and walked along the bank toward her through the ringing, with his boot coming untied again, to say the first thing that came into his head.
