On the ledges they say a dragon is hatched twice: once out of the egg, in the warm, and once on the Choosing Ledge, in the cold, when it stops in front of somebody and stays. The first hatching it doesn't choose. The second it does. Most of them only ever have the one.

Skell remembered the warm before he remembered anything.

It was under him. That was the first thing, before light or sound or the smell of the others. A floor that was warm as nothing else in the world would ever be warm again, from below, from very deep, steadily, as if the stone itself were breathing slow under the place where he lay. He had lain in it a long time in the egg. He didn't remember the egg. He remembered coming out of it, wet and furious and cold all down one side where the air touched him, and putting himself back down at once on the warm with his eyes still shut, flat, all four feet out, as close to the floor as he could get.

That was the caves above Coldtarn, on the eastern rim, where the Clan keeps its eggs because nowhere else on the Scarfell runs warm enough to hatch them. A low cave, and then another behind it, and others behind that going back into the hill, and in the second cave a spring that came up out of the floor and ran away in a channel cut by men, and steamed. His egg had lain in a bowl of the floor near the spring. The bowls were worn smooth. Dragons had lain in them since before there were people to cut channels.

Out at the front of the caves there was the mouth, and the light, and past the light there was the drop.

He knew where the drop was before he could walk. He couldn't have said how. He'd lie on the warm with his eyes shut and know it: the floor going on, and on, and then the floor stopping, out there in the light, and nothing, a great deal of nothing, all the way down to the black water. The tarn. It lay below the caves' mouth, round and black and still, and it never froze, the keepers said, though he didn't know then what the keepers said. He only knew it was there. The drop on one side of him, always. The warm under him. And the others.

---

There were eleven of them that summer, in the first cave. Blacks and reds and a bronze, and two blues, and Skell was the smaller of the blues. They lay on the warm in a heap and crawled over each other and bit each other's tails, and they talked, all the time, all at once, without words, because a hatchling hasn't any. Kin-talk. You hear it in your head from any dragon you can see, and in the first cave you could see all of them, so it was never quiet. *Here. Mine. Here. Warm. Mine. Off. Here.* It was like lying in the middle of a lot of small fires.

At the back of the caves, in the dark beyond the spring, there were old ones. Unbonded, grey at the edges, who came up from the Roosts in the autumn to lie in the warm all winter, and didn't move, and hardly ate. One of them hummed.

That was the other first thing. Not a word. A sound in the head, and in the chest at the same time, low, going on, that the old one gave out across the cave in the evenings when the young ones had fought themselves to a standstill. The mother-hum. Every dragon of the Clan knows it, the ledges say, and none of them remembers learning it. When it came, the heap went still. Eleven small fires burning down at once.

Skell lay at the bottom of the heap, on the warm, and listened to it, and knew where the drop was.

---

The keepers came in the mornings with the barrows.

Two men and a woman, mostly, sometimes a boy. They smelled of mutton and lye and the hill, and of the old ones' dung, which they shovelled. They came in at the mouth with the light behind them and the barrows bumping on the stone, and they whistled.

The whistle meant meat. That was all Skell knew of men for a long time: they came in out of the light, from the side where the drop was, and made sounds with their mouths, and one of the sounds meant meat. The rest was noise. They talked to each other over the barrows, and to the hatchlings, and to the old ones at the back, a great deal, in a low cheerful steady noise like water in the channel, and none of it meant anything. One of the sounds they made at him more than at the others. *Skell.* They made it when they pushed him back from the barrow with the flat of a shovel because he was too small to be at the front and too stubborn to stay at the back. It didn't mean him. It meant the shovel.

In the autumn they brought the hatchlings down.

They came with wicker baskets, big ones, lashed on the back of an old brown dragon who lay down in the cave mouth with a long-suffering look, and they lifted the hatchlings one at a time into the baskets, and the hatchlings screamed. Skell screamed. They put a lid on him. He felt the old brown get up under him and walk to the edge and he knew, with everything he had, where the drop was, and then they were in it.

He didn't remember the flight. He remembered the basket's wicker against his nose, and the cold coming in through it, and the smell of the old brown, hot stone and burnt hair. And the warm going away under him. Going and going. Then gone.

---

The pens were cold.

Stone, and straw on the stone, and no warm under it at all. A row of caves on the lower ledges under the Roosts, with oak bars across their mouths, black and heavy, with gates in them, and the light coming in through the bars across the straw in stripes. Two to a pen, the keepers said, or three when they were small. Skell had the other blue, who was bigger than him and lay on the side of the pen nearest the bars, where the light was, and a red, who was bigger than both of them and lay where it liked.

He lay at the back.

Not because they made him. Because the back of the pen was rock, and the rock went up into the cliff and down into the hill, and there was no drop behind it. He could lie with his back to it and know: nothing behind me. In front, the bars. Past the bars, the ledge, and past the ledge, the drop to the yard, and the yard full of men and fires and noise. And above, the Roosts, the whole cliff of them, and the bonded and the old ones sitting outside their caves in the evenings with their wings folded, looking down. You could hear them. Kin-talk, slow and deep and a long way up, like the old ones in the caves, only more of them. They talked about weather. They talked about sheep.

The keepers came with the barrows and the whistle. In the spring they brought the young ones out on the lower ledges and taught them to open their wings and go off, and come back. Skell went off last every time. Every time he stood on the lip with the others gone ahead of him and the drop going down in front of him, and his whole body knew where it was, and then he went anyway, because the keeper was behind him with the flat of the shovel, and his wings opened by themselves, as they'd been made to.

He was good at it, once he was off. That surprised everyone. The keeper with the shovel laughed and made the sound at him, *Skell*, and it meant, for once, something that wasn't the shovel.

---

He was a yearling at his first choosing and didn't stop in front of anybody. He didn't look at them. He walked up and down the long ledge in the firelight with the others and kept to the cliff side, away from the lip, and smelled them as he went. Young men and young women of the Clan, in a line with their backs to the rock. They smelled of soap and fear and their mothers' cooking. Some of them were shaking. One of them was sick. Nobody stopped in front of anybody that year. The Clan went home grumbling, and the keepers put the young ones back in the pens.

He was a two-year-old at his second, and he chose.

He hadn't meant to. That was what he'd say, after, to Tor, when Tor asked: *I didn't mean to.* And Tor would laugh, and say that was a fine thing to tell a man.

It was this. The line was long that year, more than twenty of them, and the ledge was crowded, and the young dragons came out of the pens in a rush and spread along it, and Skell kept to the cliff side as he always did. And at the near end of the line, by the head of the stair, where the line ran out and the ledge ran on a little way to its lip, a boy was standing with his heels on the edge.

Not in the line. Behind it. He'd been pushed out of it by the crowding, or he'd stepped out of it to see. He was long and thin and had a great deal of nose, and he wasn't looking at the dragons at all. He was looking down. Over his own shoulder, down, into the dark below the lip, as you'd look at something you'd dropped. His heels were on the edge. Half of each heel was over nothing.

Skell's whole body knew where the drop was.

He went down the ledge, past the line, past all of them, and stood between the boy and the lip. Right up against him. His shoulder against the boy's chest, pushing, so that the boy had to step back off the edge onto the ledge or fall over.

The boy stepped back.

Skell stayed where he was. Between. With the drop behind him now instead of behind the boy, which he didn't like at all, and the boy in front of him, staring.

The whole ledge had gone quiet. The yard had. He didn't notice. He was waiting for the boy to go and stand somewhere sensible so that he could get away from the edge, and the boy didn't, and didn't, and stood there with his mouth a little open and looked at him, and Skell looked back, and stayed.

And something tied.

It was at the back of his head, at the root of his skull where the first plates are. He felt it go round. Once, and again, and pull tight. A knot. And from the knot, a line, going out of him, across the little space between them, into the boy. Into the middle of him. And tied there too.

The boy said, "Skell?"

It was the keepers' sound. The shovel sound. The boy had heard it, somewhere, from the keepers, as everyone had.

It meant him.

He knew it as he knew the drop. Not the sound: what it was for. It was for him. It came along the line from the boy, into the knot, into his head, and it meant *you, that one, the blue one, you*. And under it, along the line, there was more. The boy was frightened. The boy was so pleased he was nearly sick. The boy's knees had gone. All of it, coming along the line into Skell, warm, and nothing in the world had ever been warm like that except the floor of the caves.

He said the first thing he ever said to anybody.

*You're too near the edge.*

The boy stared at him. Then he began to laugh. He laughed so hard he had to sit down on the ledge, in front of the whole Clan, with his long legs out and his face in his hands, and Skell stood over him, between him and the drop, and didn't understand at all, and stayed.

---

His name was Tor. Tor Brecc. He was seventeen.

Skell learned it that night, and learned a great many other words after it, all at once, because everything Tor said meant something now. Everything. Tor talked, and it came along the line and opened in Skell's head like a hand opening, and there was a thing in it. *Ledge. Cloak. Tomorrow. Granda. Mutton, again. Bloody hell.* He didn't know most of the things. He knew the words for them, which was not the same, and he carried them about with him like stones he'd picked up and didn't know what to do with.

Other people's words didn't open. That was the strange thing, the thing that took longest. Everyone else still made the keepers' noise. Tor's mother made it, in the yard, with her hand over her mouth. The keepers made it. Tor's grandfather made it, an old man with a stick and a white beard and the same nose, who came down the cliff the next morning on his old black dragon's neck and looked at Skell a long time and said something to Tor.

Noise. Water in a channel.

Then Tor said, "He says you've a lot to learn about edges."

So that was how it went. He heard the world by halves. Tor's half, opened. Everybody else's, shut. He learned to wait for Tor to say a thing again, or answer it, so he'd know what it had been. Sometimes Tor didn't, and the thing stayed shut, and Skell turned it over and over and couldn't get into it. Sometimes Tor did, and it wasn't what the person had said at all, and Skell could tell from the feeling on the line that Tor was lying, and that Tor knew he could tell, and was doing it anyway because it was funnier.

That night they slept in a cave on the ledges, one below Morrow's, with a fire in its mouth. Skell lay at the back, against the rock. Tor lay by the fire. And in the night Skell got up and went and lay down behind him, with his back to the fire and the drop, which he hated, so that he could put his chin over Tor's shoulder from behind and know where he was.

Tor woke. He reached up without looking and scratched under Skell's jaw. The soft place, where the plates don't meet and the skin is thin, that nobody had ever touched.

In the morning there was a mark on the top of Tor's right shoulder where Skell's chin had been. Blue. Like a bruise, only blue, and shaped like nothing. Tor showed it to everybody.

---

*What's a boat?*

They were on the ledge outside the cave, in the first summer, with the fire going and the wind coming up the glen. Tor was mending a strap. His grandfather had been and gone, on Morrow, and had said a thing, and Tor had laughed and said it back to Skell to keep: *A bonded pair is a boat and its rope.*

"A boat." Tor thought about it. "You've not seen one."

*No.*

"I've seen two. On the Wend, when I was small, from the Way. A boat's a thing you sit in on water. It floats. Like a stick does, only you're in it."

Skell turned it over. He had the word. He had the thing about the stick. He didn't have the water, the Wend; he'd never seen a river bigger than the beck. So he put it on the only water he knew. The black water under the caves. Coldtarn. Round and still and deep. A thing on it, floating, with somebody sitting in it.

*And the rope.*

"The rope ties it to the shore. So it stays where you want it. If the rope goes," Tor pulled the strap through, "the water takes it. Out. Off. You can't swim after it. It goes where the water goes."

Skell saw it. The black tarn, and the thing on it, and the rope, and the rope going, and the thing drifting out across the black, slowly, toward the middle.

*Which one's me?*

"What?"

*The boat or the rope.*

Tor laughed. "I asked him that. When I was ten. I asked him which was him and which was Morrow."

*What did he say?*

"He said whichever one's in trouble." Tor bit off the thread. "He said, mostly, it's the one asking."

The old chief died the next autumn, after Skell's first midsummer on the ledges, and when they burned him every dragon on the Roosts cried for him, Skell with them, the long many-voiced cry that comes up out of you without your asking and goes down at the end. Down, for the dead. He didn't know the old chief. He cried anyway. You can't not, the ledges say. It's in you like the hum.

They burned the chief's younger son not long after, the warm one, and cried again.

Then it was the winter the ledges didn't fly.

Skell didn't understand it. Tor explained it, and the explaining came along the line hot and sour, like something gone off. There was a war in the Clan. Two sides, the red thread and the grey, the chief's son and the chief's brother. And there was a law older than either of them, that no bonded rider fights kin. So the bonded didn't fight. Eleven pairs, on the ledges: the old ones, mostly, and Leif with his young brown, and Tor. They stayed in the Roosts. They hunted the high glens and came home and sat outside their caves and didn't fly for either side, and the war went on below them in the dark, steadings burning down the glen, and the smoke coming up.

Once a week a long sour man came up the stair from the yard, limping, and stood on the ledge and made noise at them.

"He says we're cowards," Tor said.

He said it as if it were nothing. It came along the line as if it were a stone in his mouth.

There had been three others, before. Three bonded pairs who'd broken the law and flown, in the autumn, before the war was properly started, a great bronze and a red and a green, and their riders were dead now, at a mill on the Vale floor, all three in one night, and the three dragons had gone down a barley field to a farmer and he'd taken them. Tor told it. It went round the Roosts all that winter in kin-talk and in noise both, and Skell didn't understand that either. A man with three ropes. He tried to see it and couldn't. Three boats on one shore.

He lay behind Tor at night with his chin over Tor's shoulder and felt him lie awake.

---

In the spring the old man with the stick went down to a ford on Morrow's neck, to make the two sides talk, and the ledges went with him.

All eleven. They flew down out of the Scarfell in a line, Morrow first, and came down on a slope above a brown loud beck in spring flood, by a burned shieling, and folded their wings, and sat in a row facing the water. Keepers of the truce, Tor said, as their grandfathers' grandfathers had been. Skell sat at the end of the row because Tor did. On his left, an old grey. On his right, nothing, the slope going down.

He didn't like it. Men on both banks with horses, and the smell of horses, which he didn't care for, and of iron. And a man in the middle of the water. A man standing in the beck with the water at his knees, talking, a long time, to both banks, and the noise of it coming up the slope, shut, shut, all of it. Behind the man, on a ridge, two dragons: a red, a big bronze. Their kin-voices came across the slope low and wary. The red's was like a fire with the door shut.

Tor didn't open any of the man in the water for him. Tor was listening too hard.

Then the gorse on both banks was full of men with crossbows, and the red came off the ridge roaring, and the old bronze went over the water so low the man in it went down on his knees, and everything was noise.

And Morrow got up.

Skell felt her go. Not on the line; she wasn't his. In the kin-talk, the old dry voice he'd heard every evening since the choosing, from the ledge above, going up all at once, hard. She went down the slope with the old man standing on her back with his stick, shouting. Skell didn't need it opened. He could feel what it was from Tor. Tor had stood up. Tor was standing beside him with every part of him pulling down the slope after the black dragon and the old man on her, pulling so hard the line between them shook, and he didn't go.

None of them went. The law. They sat in their row.

Then the old man stopped shouting, and Morrow fell out of the air.

Then they went.

Skell didn't remember it. He remembered gorse under his feet and men running and Tor on his neck, and a taste in his mouth. When it was over he was standing on the hillside again in the row with the others, and below them on the stones at the water's edge Morrow was lying with the old man's head on her foreleg. The man from the water knelt beside them holding the old man's hand.

The old man put his other hand up, once, on Morrow's face. Then down.

---

They stayed three days.

Nobody said they would. Nobody said anything. The ten sat in the heather in a row above the ford with their riders beside them, and nobody lit a fire, and nobody went home. Below, the black dragon lay at the edge of the water with the dead man against her, and her eyes open.

She talked once, that first evening, before the light went. Not to them. To the old bronze on the ridge, the farmer's one, across the water. But they were all within sight, so they heard it, all ten, in the last of the light, the old dry voice gone very thin.

*Go. I will stay with him. It won't be long.*

And then, after a while, to the farmer's old one again: *Thank you for staying.*

Skell had never heard a dragon say *thank you*. He didn't know they had it.

Tor sat beside him in the heather with his knees up and his arms round them and didn't say anything all night, and the line between them was so quiet Skell put his chin on Tor's shoulder to make sure he was there.

He watched her go. Over three days. Something going out of her, slowly, a little more each time the light changed. On the first day she still lifted her head when the wind moved. On the second, she didn't. On the third her breath got long, and longer, with more and more nothing between, and in the evening, when the light was going, she lifted her head off the old man's chest, once, and looked up the hillside at the ten.

Every one of them stood up. Skell too. He didn't decide to.

She put her head back down on the old man and breathed out, a long breath, longer than any breath he'd heard, and didn't breathe in.

The sound came up out of the farmer's old one first, on the ridge, low and long and older than the hill. Then out of the ten. Out of Skell. He'd cried for the dead before, but this came from further down, from the bottom of him where the hum was, and went on until there wasn't any more of it.

---

They carried them to Coldtarn that night.

Morrow in ropes, slung under two of the old ones. The old man on the neck of a third. Tor said, "Me," before anyone else could, and nobody argued. They lifted him up onto Skell's neck in his grey cloak, in front of Tor, and Tor put his arms round him and held him there, and they went up off the slope into the dark, nine of them, slow, heavy, with one staying behind.

He was very light, the old man. Lighter than Tor. Skell felt Tor holding him all the way, and the line between them so tight and so quiet that it hummed.

At the tarn they burned them both, on the burned stones of the shore under the black cliffs. In the morning there was the ash. And Tor took it in a grey jar out along the flat black stone that runs out into the water like a finger, and stood at the end of it a long time, and then tipped it.

Skell watched from the shore.

The ash lay on the black water, grey, spreading. Then in the middle of it something began to draw in. The whole grey shape of it moving, slowly, toward one place. And at that place it went down. Drawn down, all of it, into the middle of the black, and the black closed over, and was still.

So that was where.

He'd always seen it out there, the boat, when Tor said it. On the black water, drifting. He hadn't known where it was drifting to. Now he did. Out to the middle. Where the ash goes down.

Tor came back along the stone with the empty jar and stood beside him.

*Morrow had three days,* said Skell.

"Morrow was old. It's the old ones that get three days. And she was the one left." Tor looked at the water. "It's not the same the other way. If it's the dragon goes first, you don't get three days. Nobody does. Gunna says it's a breath."

Skell thought about that.

*Then I'll not go first.*

"Right," said Tor. "Good. That's settled, then."

He put his hand up without looking and scratched under Skell's jaw, in the soft place, and they stood there on the shore and looked at the black water until it was time to fly home.

Less than a month after the ford, the old bronze came home.

She came walking up the glen at dusk, the farmer's old one, on four legs, with a wing hanging, very slow, with the red walking at her side and a green flying over and a man in a farmer's coat at her head. The ledges saw her first. Somebody cried out, high, and then everybody, all at once, the whole cliff of the Roosts, and Skell was crying with them before he'd seen what for, and the cry went up at the end.

Up. Not down. He'd never heard it go up.

"They're saying she came home," Tor said, beside him, on the ledge. His voice was strange. "She's older than the hold. She's older than anyone. She was here before the Varrs."

Skell looked down at her. The old bronze in the heather by the road, with her head on the ground. She was the biggest thing he had ever seen alive, and she was lying down like a ewe that won't get up again.

*She's got three ropes,* he said.

"The farmer has. She's one of them."

*Can you feel his?*

"No." Tor was quiet. "Only mine."

Skell put his chin over Tor's shoulder from behind and felt the knot at the back of his own head, and the line, and Tor at the end of it. One rope. He tried to think of a man with three of them going into him, three knots in the middle of him, three boats. It made his head ache. He stopped.

They kept the farmer on the ledges that spring and summer. The setters' ledge, high up, with the old one lying on the warm rock with her wing in splints, and the green on a rock behind, not talking. Kin-talk is never quiet on the Roosts, but the green was. A young dragon with a quick voice, everyone said, and from the early summer she didn't use it. She lay on the rock in the evenings with her head turned east. The red lay on the high ledge across his cave mouth and growled at nothing.

The pens shut in the spring, as they did. Two to a pen, chosen. Skell flew past them with Tor on his neck and looked down through the oak bars at the dragons on the straw with the light across them in stripes, and was glad, glad all the way down, that he was out, and didn't say it, and Tor felt it anyway.

---

This was the second midsummer.

It was the ledges' custom that a pair stood with the ledges at the choosing only from its second. The first year you stood in the yard, at the back, with your family. So that the young ones on the ledge didn't take you for one of the line, Gunna said, and so that you'd remember what it was like down there not knowing. Last midsummer Tor had stood in the yard and Skell behind him, and nobody had been chosen.

This year they stood with the ledges.

At the near end of the Choosing Ledge, by the head of the stair. The ledges' end. Tor in his grey cloak with no thread, and his axe at his belt because the ledges wear them at the choosing, the old ones said, though none of them could say why. Skell beside him, on the inside, against the cliff. The lip of the ledge was on Tor's side, two long strides off, and the dark of the glen under it.

He knew where it was. He always would.

Below them, the yard. The whole of the hold was in it, more people than he'd ever seen at once. Fires and torches. People in red thread round their arms, and people in plain grey, and they stood apart in the yard in two pieces with a space between, like two flocks in one fold. A big grey man by the west wall with twenty more around him. A man in black on the steps of the hall.

"That's Ulric," Tor said, low. "By the wall. That's the uncle. Arn's let him in for it. First word he's sent him since the ford."

*The one in black?*

"That's Arn."

*Are they going to fight?*

"Not tonight. It's the choosing. Nobody fights at the choosing." Tor looked down at them. "They might after."

Skell put his chin on Tor's shoulder from behind and looked at the yard over it. Tor reached up without looking and scratched under his jaw.

At the edge of the yard by the gate there was an old woman with a stick, and a smaller woman, and some small ones, and a big grey dray-beast standing behind the smallest of them like a wall. The farmer's people. And up on the cliff, above, on the ledge below the setters', Gunna sat on her old grey-green's foreleg with her clay pipe going, and looked down at the line. Above her, the setters' ledge, with the farmer on it and the old one with her head up. Above that, the red, lying across his cave mouth.

All of them, up the cliff. All of it, in its places. Skell had it in his head like the floor of a cave: the drop, the rock, the stair, the yard, the fires, and where everybody was.

The keepers opened the pens.

---

They came out in a rush.

Forty of them. Yearlings no bigger than the dray-beast, and two-year-olds nearly as big as the farmer's green, blues and blacks and reds and bronzes and a green or two, out of the pens and onto the Choosing Ledge all at once, like hatchlings to the barrow, and stopped. All of them, at once. On the long ledge in the firelight, with the fires on the ledges and the yard below them and the Roosts going up, and the line.

Skell had been one of them. Two midsummers ago. He looked at them and remembered it with his whole skin: the cold of the pens still on you, and the light, and the noise, and everybody looking.

The kin-talk off them was like the first cave. Forty small fires. *Here. Who. Here. Look. Who's that. Look.* He heard every one.

And the line. Thirty of them this year, at the far end of the ledge with their backs to the cliff. Red thread and grey together, all mixed, Tor said, because the line goes by age and not by side, and nobody had said anything about it. They smelled as the line always smelled: soap, and fear, and their mothers' cooking. One of them at the far end of the line had her eyes shut.

The young ones began to walk.

Skell watched them go up and down. He watched them keep to the cliff side, some of them, and wondered if any of them knew where the drop was as he did. He thought not. He thought it was only him.

A big young bronze went down the line slowly, looking. It went past the girl with her eyes shut, and stopped, and turned round, and came back. And stood in front of her. With its head down. Looking at her.

She didn't know. She stood there with her eyes shut, shaking.

The yard went quiet. Skell felt it go. All the noise of all those people, the shut noise he'd heard all his life, stopping, from the fires outward, until there was only the fires.

Somebody in the yard said something. One word. A woman's voice.

"Somebody's said her name," Tor said, very low. "She's one of Skarrow's."

The girl opened her eyes.

Skell put his chin on Tor's shoulder and felt him smiling. Felt it come along the line, warm. Under it, something else, smaller: Tor remembering standing on this ledge with his heels on the edge, looking down. And a blue coming down the line to push him off it.

*You were too near the edge,* said Skell.

"I was," said Tor. "Look at her. Look at her face."

---

He smelled it an hour after dark.

Before anybody. Dragons do; the ledges say a dragon smells weather a day before a shepherd and trouble two. It came down the glen on the wind from the north-east, over the cliff-top, faint. Sweet.

He lifted his head off Tor's shoulder.

It was the warm.

That was what it smelled like. Not like anything he could have said. Like the floor of the caves. Like lying on the warm with his eyes shut and the stone breathing under him. Honey, and something like pine-gum, and under both of them the warm, the first thing, the thing he'd been carried away from in a basket and never had again. It came down the glen in the dark, sweet, and went into his nose and into his head, and something in him that had been holding all his life, since the basket, since the pens, put itself down.

*Somebody's warming something,* he said.

"What?"

*Up on the moor. Somebody's warming something. Can you smell it?*

Tor lifted his nose. Skell felt him not smell it, and then smell it. And then felt Tor go cold. All at once, all the way down the line, cold as the pens.

"Skell."

Up on the setters' ledge the old one had lifted her head off her forelegs.

Over the cliff-top, against the stars, there was a white. Coming. Rolling down off the heather over the lip of the cliff in a long low bank, slow, like mist coming down off the tops in the morning into the high glens, only it was night, and it smelled of the warm. It came down over the cave mouths. Over the high ledges. Over the red.

Skell heard the red's voice go.

It had been there all evening in the kin-talk, high above, the red's growl going on under everything. Now it went thick. Slow. As if somebody had thrown a fleece over the fire.

And then the next voice down the cliff went. And the next. One after another, down the Roosts, the kin-voices of the ledges and the old ones going thick and slow and out, as the white came down over them. Like fires on the ledges going out one at a time, top to bottom, as the rain gets to them.

Behind him, in the line of the young ones on the Choosing Ledge, the forty were still talking. *Here. Look. Who's that. What's that.*

Then the white came down the last of the cliff and over the Choosing Ledge, and they stopped. All at once. Forty small fires, out.

It came over Skell.

---

It was so warm.

It came into his nose and his mouth and his chest and it was the floor of the caves, all of it, all at once, coming up under him from very deep, and he wanted to lie down in it. He wanted to put himself down flat on the stone with his eyes shut and all four feet out and stay there for ever. His knees went. He stood. His head came down. He couldn't hold it up. It was too heavy and too warm and there was no reason to.

And the knot at the back of his head began to come undone.

He felt it. The knot at the root of his skull where the line tied on, that had tied itself on the ledge two midsummers ago and never once loosened since, not at the ford, not at the tarn. It loosened. One turn. Slowly, as a wet knot does when you pick at it. Then another turn. And the line going out from it to Tor slackened, and the slack went out across the space between them, and Tor was further away.

Not on the ledge. He could smell Tor on the ledge still, right there, peat smoke and wet wool and tallow and the bog-myrtle in his shirt. On the line. On the line Tor was further, and further, as if the ledge between them had got longer, and was getting longer all the time, and was going out under him, and he was going out on it.

Out on the water.

He saw it. He couldn't help it. The black water under the caves, still, round, and the thing on it. Tor's word, the stick you sit in. And the rope going slack, and the black water taking him, out, off the shore, slowly, slowly, toward the middle.

*Tor,* he said. *I'm coming off.*

*No you're not.*

Tor's voice. Along the line. Far.

*I'm coming off the rope. Like a boat. Like a boat off its rope. Tor, it's warm, I'm going out on the water, I can't...*

*You're not. You're not. I've got it.* Tor's voice going hard, and thin, as his face went thin when he was trying not to be afraid. *I've got the rope. Skell. Here. You're here. You're on the ledge. You're by the stair. Feel it. Here.*

He felt Tor take hold.

Felt it at the other end of the line, very far: hands. Not real hands. Tor, taking hold of his end and holding it, and pulling, a little, as you'd pull a strap tight. The slack came out of the line, a little. Then it went out again. The knot at the back of Skell's head came loose another turn, and the water took the boat a little further out, and Tor held on.

---

Above him, the red came back roaring.

He heard it from out on the water. Far off and very loud. A roar that came down the cliff and shook stones off it, and his voice in the kin-talk not thick any more, all fire, no words in it at all, and then light. Red light. A great sheet of it over the cliff-top, at the white, at the stars, so that everything on the Roosts went red at once.

In the red, he saw them.

Men. Coming over the lip of the Choosing Ledge out of the dark of the glen, on ropes, with iron hooks. Coming down the face of the cliff from above, on ropes, past the cave mouths. With white cloths over their faces; he smelled them, wet. Strangers' boots, new leather, a smell that wasn't the Clan's. And among them men with pots on chains, swinging, smoking, the warm pouring off them in white ropes. And things on their arms, iron, open, with a hinge, that clinked.

And over the white, two shapes coming in from the moor. Flying. With men on their backs.

Wyverns. Even out on the water some old small part of him knew it, and was insulted. Count the legs. Two, and wings they walked on when they were down, and a collar on each, and their eyes in the red light with nothing in them at all. They smelled of nothing. That was the worst of it. Every beast smells of something. These smelled of iron and the men on their backs, and nothing else, as if there were nobody inside.

It all happened on the shore. Little, and far, and red, and he was out on the water, and it was very warm.

Down the ledge the young ones stood in the smoke with their heads down. All forty. He couldn't hear them. Where there had been forty fires there was nothing, a ledge of nothing, and the men with the pots walked down it, looking, and stopped, here, and here, and put their hands on heads.

Something came down out of the white above with a scream in the kin-talk that went right through the warm into him. Tamsin. Gunna's old grey-green, the steadiest flier on the ledges, coming down half blind with something white over her nose and Gunna flat on her neck, onto the ledge, onto the men. Two of them went over the lip without a sound. Somebody swung a pot. It broke. Gold came out of it. A sheet of gold, liquid, running over the stone, over Tamsin's wing where it lay out on the ledge, over Gunna's cloak, and Tamsin screamed and screamed in his head, and under it Gunna's voice, low, steady, making noise at her dragon like the keepers' noise over the barrows.

Then other people. The stair. Shapes in the red, dragging.

*Skell.*

Tor.

*Skell, there's a man.*

---

He smelled him first.

A man coming along the ledge, alone. Not one of the ones who'd stopped by the four. Another. Behind the others, late. Burns on his hands, old burns, that smelled of the pots; skin that had been burned and healed and burned again. Sweat that had gone sour with being afraid. Honey. And iron: one of the open things on his arm, with its hinge, clinking as he came.

He was looking for something. Skell could smell that too. The man was walking along the ledge looking, as a crag-eagle looks along a slope for a ewe that's down.

He stopped in front of Skell.

He put his hand on Skell's head.

Flat. On top, behind the eyes, between the first plates. Where nobody put a hand. Where Tor never did; Tor's hand went under the jaw.

And something came in.

It came in through the hand, cold. Like the cold that came in through the wicker of the basket, the first flight, when the warm went away under him. It came into the place in him where he was, the place the knot was tied in, and it filled. He felt it fill. It didn't push. There wasn't any pushing. It just came in, and kept coming, and there was more of it than there was room, and where it went there wasn't room for him.

It had a shape. It was a wanting. Somebody else's. It came in and took the shape of him from the inside, as water takes the shape of a bowl, and it had nobody in it. Only the wanting. *Mine. This one. Mine.* A shape put on him like the iron on the man's arm.

So he went further out.

He didn't choose to. There wasn't room. The cold came in and he went out to make room for it, out from the shore, out onto the black water, further and further, and the knot at the back of his head where the line was tied came loose another turn. And another. Until there was only the last turn left, the last of the knot, holding by nearly nothing, and the line going back across the black to the shore, slack, long, very long, and at the other end of it, very small, a long way off, a voice.

*SKELL.*

And then the man's hand came off his head.

---

He didn't see it. He felt it. The hand went, all at once. And something hit the stone of the ledge in front of him, heavy, soft, a man's weight going down. And the smell of blood. And Tor's smell, very close. Peat smoke and tallow and bog-myrtle, and iron, wet.

The axe. One blow.

The cold stopped coming.

But it was in him. All that had come in, half of all there was going to be, was in him still, and the man who'd poured it was lying on the ledge with his hand still lifted, and there was nobody behind it now. Nobody at all. Only the shape. The wanting, with no one wanting it. It sat in the place where Skell had been and filled it, and it didn't know whose it was, and it began to move.

It lifted him.

Up, out of the smoke. His head came up. His legs straightened under him. Not because he did it. He was out on the water. The shape did it, the dead man's wanting, filling him up like a skin, standing him up on the ledge in the red light and turning his head.

It didn't know anything. It didn't know the ledge or the stair or the drop. It didn't know which way was the cliff. It knew one thing: near. Whatever was near. It turned toward near.

There was a thing in front of him.

He didn't know what it was. He had no name for it. It was warm and upright and close, closer than anything, standing on the ledge in front of him with something in one hand, iron, wet, held low at its side, not lifted. And its other hand out. Open. Toward him. Coming up, toward his face, toward under his jaw.

He didn't know it.

His head came round, fast. The shape brought it round. His mouth opened. His teeth.

The thing didn't move. It stood with the iron at its side and the open hand coming up, and didn't lift the iron.

Out on the water, a long way out, almost at the middle, where the black begins to draw in, Skell felt the line.

The last turn of the knot. Still there. Holding by nothing. And the line going back from it, long, slack, across all that water to a shore he couldn't see any more. And at the other end, something holding it.

He didn't know what. He didn't know what was on the shore. He couldn't feel it, or smell it, or see it. He only knew that the line was still tied at his end, by one turn, and that at the other end something had hold of it, and wasn't letting go, and was holding it open. Open, like a hand.

*Let go,* said the shape. Not in words. It hadn't any. In the wanting, in the cold. *There's nothing there. Let it go. Mine.*

He could. He could feel how easy. One turn, and the knot would come off, and the line would go, and he'd be out, all the way out, and the shape could have the rest of him, and it wouldn't be cold any more. It would be warm. It would be the floor of the caves for ever.

He didn't know what was holding the other end.

He held his.

That was all. He didn't pull it in. He couldn't; he hadn't the strength, out there. He didn't go back to the shore. He only didn't let it go. He took the last turn of the knot at the back of his head, the nearly-nothing that was left of it, and he kept it, and he didn't know why, and he didn't know who for.

His teeth stopped.

A hand's breadth from the open hand. In the red light. With the shape screaming at him in the cold, *near, near, mine*, he stopped them, and held them there, open, not closing, and held his end of the line.

---

Something went *tock* above him.

A small dry sound, up in the red, from the back of one of the shapes with two legs. He knew it from somewhere. A sound like Gunna knocking her pipe out on a stone.

Then the soft place.

Under his jaw. Where the plates don't meet and the skin is thin, where nobody had ever touched but one. Something went into it.

It was cold, and then it wasn't. It was hot. It was very hot, and very big, much bigger than the small thing that had gone in, and his head went back.

And the black water drew in.

He felt it go. The whole tarn, under him, beginning to move. Slowly, at first. Then all of it, toward one place. Toward the middle. Where the ash goes. He was very near the middle, he'd been carried nearly all the way, and now the water was drawing in and he was going with it, down.

And the line came taut.

All of it. From the last turn of the knot at the back of his head, all the long slack way across the black water to the shore, it came tight, as a strap comes tight when a weight goes onto it. And he felt the weight. At the other end.

The shore was coming.

It came along the line after him, over the black water, toward the middle, fast, faster than anything had ever moved. Not pulled. He wasn't pulling. He hadn't the strength to pull a feather. It came because the line was tied at both ends, and both ends were holding, and when the boat goes down the middle of the black water the shore goes too. That was all. That was how it was. Morrow had had three days because she was the one left. This was the other way, and it was a breath.

Less.

---

He came down onto the stone.

His legs went from under him, and he came down on the ledge in the smoke, and the shape that had filled him went out of him all at once, as cold goes out of a cave when the sun comes round to the mouth. Gone. Nothing in the place where it had been. Only him. Only the place, empty, with him in it, and the warm.

Not the smoke's warm. The other.

Something was lying on the stone in front of him. Close. He'd come down beside it. Long, and thin, folded down on the ledge on its side, with its knees drawn up. It smelled of peat smoke. And wet wool. And tallow. And bog-myrtle, green and bitter, crushed in a shirt.

He knew the smell before he knew anything.

He knew where his chin went.

Not the shoulder, now. The shoulder was down on the stone. The knees. They were there, in front of him, bent, still warm, and his head was very heavy and very hot and there was nothing to hold it up, and he let it come down across them. It fitted. It had always fitted.

The hand was still open. Lying on the stone by his face. He could smell it. Iron, and peat smoke, and his own skin from under his own jaw, where it had scratched him every night since the choosing without looking.

*Tor,* he said.

It meant him.
