---

On the Oneroad the milestones counted to the bridge.

They counted from both ends. Walk the long road down out of the north, out of the snow-country where the rivers start, and the stones by the road said thirty, and twenty-nine, and twenty-eight, all the way down the valley of the Wend; walk it up out of the south from the sea, through the oak country and the beech, and the stones said the same, the numbers getting smaller under your hand. Whichever end you came from, you were going toward the same place. The drovers who had walked the road all their lives said it was the middle of the world, and laughed when they said it, and didn't mean it as a joke.

The bridge was seven arches from the west bank to the island, and seven more from the island to the east. It was made of the old pale stone that nobody had quarried in living memory, and in the sun it was white. In rain it went grey, so the songs said white and the drovers said grey, and both were right. The road ran over the top of it wide enough for four carts abreast, and on the island, in the middle of the fourteen arches, the road went through a fort with a gate at each side and a round paved place between the gates, and over the round stood a tower with a roof that shone like a new pan, and on top of the roof, day and night, burned a fire.

Somebody had to carry the oil up.

---

Ennis carried it up at first light with the jar against his chest and his free hand on the wall, because the stair was worn in the middle of every step into a dip like a saddle, and the dip held the dew.

The fire was low. It always was at that hour. He poured, and it took, and came up yellow, and for a moment there was nothing in the world but the heat on his face and the smell of oil. Then he stood back from it and looked out.

He did that every morning. He'd been told to, the first year, by Marrit, who had been told by somebody before her: *you go up, you feed it, you look.* He had asked what he was looking for. She'd said, *You'll know.* He was thirty-four now and he had looked every morning for sixteen years and never seen anything but the river.

It was enough. The Wend came down out of the north wide and slow and brown, and split on the island's head in two long folds of water, and joined again under the south tip and went on. The west bank was march-land: woods, and the smoke of a few farms, and far down the river, three and a half leagues off, the long hump of the river hill above the bend with the quarry on its face, pale as a cut loaf. The east bank went up in fields to dark woods and dark hills. Mist lay on the water. The bridge went out from under him both ways into it, white, and disappeared, as if it had no ends at all.

There was a dragon asleep on the west parapet.

She lay along the top of it with her tail hanging down over the river and her chin on the warm stone, where the sun would come first, and in the grey light she was the colour of an old coin. When the sun came over the east hills and reached her she'd be a new one. She knew that. She arranged herself for it every morning, and pretended she hadn't.

*You are looking at me,* said Aurveth, without opening her eyes.

"I'm looking at the river."

*You are looking at me and the river is behind me.*

He went down.

---

The cat was on the tower steps.

There were nine steps up from the round to the arch in the tower's side, broad and shallow, and the cat lay across the top four of them in the first sun with its forepaws stretched out in front of it and its head up. It was bigger than any cat had a right to be. Bigger than Aurveth, though she'd never let him say so. It was iron grey, the grey of a wet blade, and on its flanks the grey went over into a gold like dry grass, and its mane stood up round its head like a cloud standing up over the east hills on a still afternoon. Its eyes were pale. Not blue, not green. The colour of the river when there's ice coming.

He stopped at the foot of the steps. He put his hand on his heart. And he bowed, low, as Marrit had shown him with her stiff old back the first morning, as someone had once shown her.

The cat looked at him.

Then, slowly, as if it were a thing that cost nothing and was worth a great deal, it lowered its head.

That was all. It had been all for sixteen years. On the mornings when Aurveth walked across the round with him, it looked at her too, before it lowered its head, and she held herself very straight and pretended not to notice. He straightened up and went on across the round to the gate, and behind him the cat lifted its head again and looked out over the bridge, west, as if it owned the bridge and the river and everything on them, and was counting.

---

By the time the mist was off the water the road was full.

It came from both ends. From the west, from the march-land and the hills beyond it and the long road from the south, a train of stone-carts first, as always, because the quarrymen liked to be over before the heat; and behind the stone-carts a family from somewhere far off in the sun, with brown faces and bright cloth and a grey beast with a spiral horn walking loose at the back of them, which they didn't lead and which didn't need leading. From the east a drove of black-faced sheep, and behind the sheep a big Wyldmarch woman on a horse with a great spotted cat padding beside her stirrup. A pedlar from the north, with tin. Two smiths from the iron hills who argued the whole width of the bridge about the price of charcoal, in a tongue he half knew, and stopped arguing in the round to look up at the fire, and started again on the other side.

And the beasts.

They came over on their own feet, among the people, as they always had, and nobody on the bridge thought anything of it. A greyhart, tall as a cart, picking its way among the sheep with its nose up. Dogs. A white ox with a hump, belonging to nobody he could see, who walked from the east gate to the west gate at his own pace, stopping in the round to drink from the trough and look at the cat on the steps; and the cat looked back at him, and the ox went on. Birds over the road. Under the third arch from the west end a pair of wyverns had come up out of their roost to lie on the parapet in the sun, black-green and gleaming and bad-tempered, and people went round them.

Ennis went up and down the road and was useful.

That was what a warden was. Marrit said it, and the warden before her had said it to her: *we keep the road.* You helped a cart with a split felloe off to the side where it wouldn't block the gate. You told the sheep-man that his sheep were his own business but the drink-trough was everybody's. You stood between two men with knives out over a matter of a woman in a town neither of them would see again, and talked, slowly, about the weather, until one of them laughed. You did it every day, and nobody paid you, and nobody needed to; there had been wardens on the bridge longer than there had been anything else on either bank.

Once there had been twelve. Now there were three, and the fort was mostly empty rooms with swallows in them.

Marrit sat in the shade of the east gate on a stool, because of her knees. She'd been a warden fifty years. She knew every carter on the road by his wheel-squeak, and she could read the old letters faster than Ennis could read the new.

"Ysel's late," she said, as he went by.

"She's always late."

"She's later." Marrit squinted west along the bridge. "She'll have been talking."

Cadoc was at the west gate, which was where they put him, because the west gate got the stone-carts and nobody could get a stone-cart through a gate faster than Cadoc. He was nineteen. He wanted very much to do something on the bridge one day that would go into a song, and he hadn't yet, and Ennis hoped he never would.

---

Children were what took the time.

There were always children on the bridge, and the children always wanted the dragon. They came to the parapet in twos and threes, from carts, from the drove, from the family with the horned grey, and stood and looked up at her, and she let them. She was vain. She'd lift her head when a small one came, so that the sun went down the length of her neck and every scale caught it, and then lower it again and close her eyes, as if none of it had anything to do with her.

*That one has a sticky hand,* she said.

"They all have sticky hands."

*That one has a sticky hand and is going to put it on me.*

"Let him."

*I am letting him.* A pause. *It is honey.*

The boy with the sticky hand wanted to know if she had a name. Ennis told him. The boy wanted to know if Ennis had a name. Ennis told him that too, and then, because the boy didn't believe him, crouched on the warm stones of the road and wrote it in the dust with his finger, in the new letters, big. He did the first one as his mother had taught him, with a line through it, which nobody else did and which Marrit had given up telling him was wrong.

The boy looked at it and said it out loud. He got it nearly right. Ennis laughed with his mouth shut, and the boy, who could see his shoulders going, laughed too, and ran back to his cart.

*He has gone,* said Aurveth, *and left me sticky.*

"You'll live."

*It is on the bronze part.*

"It's all the bronze part."

She opened one eye. It was gold, and the size of a cart's hub, and there was nothing in the world more scornful.

*Yes,* she said, and shut it.

---

She had come down the north road in her first summer, alone.

He'd been a warden two years then. It was a wet year. A dragon came down out of the rain one evening at the north end of the round, small as dragons went, a yearling, bronze, with mud to her knees, and lay down in the gateway out of the wind, and wouldn't go. Marrit said dragons came south sometimes, from the high caves in the north where they hatched, and stayed a while and went back. This one stayed. She lay on the parapet. She watched the road. She watched Ennis, mostly, though when he said so to Marrit, Marrit said that was a young man's idea of the world, and she was very likely watching the sheep.

It was nearly two years. He talked to her, because he talked to everything, and she never answered, because a beast doesn't answer a stranger. He fed her when there was anything to feed her. She took it from the ground where he put it and never from his hand.

Then one morning he was going round the round, as wardens did, laying his hand on the six stones, and she got down off the parapet and came after him. She walked at his shoulder, all the way round, as close as a dog. At the north stone, the one that said *to the cold*, he put his hand on it, and she put her head down beside his hand and leaned on him, and something opened.

He still didn't have a better word for it. Something opened. Like a door you've leaned your shoulder on for two years, that gives all at once, so you go through and fall on the floor on the other side, and there's somebody in there.

*You smell of oil,* she'd said. The first thing. *All the time.*

He'd sat down on the stones of the round with his back against the north stone, and laughed until he couldn't, and she had looked at him with her head on one side and waited for him to finish, and when he went on she'd said, *I do not understand what is funny,* and that had been worse.

The mark had come up on the palm of his right hand by evening. Bronze, round, the size of a coin, warm. It was still there. He looked at it sometimes, in the night, when he couldn't sleep, as a married man looks at his ring.

---

He read her the verse every time they passed the arch.

He'd read it to her the first morning after, and she'd made him read it again, and after that it was something they did, like the word a house says before it eats. He stood at the foot of the steps, with the cat lying above them in the sun, and put his hand on her neck, and read it off the stone over the arch. Six lines, cut deep, in the old square letters with the hooks on the ends of the strokes.

*Here the road of the one realm crossed the water,*
*WINSHIRE, where the old ones walked unbound,*
*and when the old ones went into the light*
*the last great one lay down to sleep below the stone,*
*and its own kin kept the door. Let no hand wake it.*
*Keep the watch below, and do not ask.*

*Again,* she'd say, some mornings. Not often. Mostly she listened to the end, and was quiet, and then said something about the sheep.

"They aren't men's letters," he told her once. "Marrit says. Men were given them."

*Who by?*

He nodded up the steps. "Ask the cat."

She looked at the cat. The cat looked out over the bridge.

*It does not talk to me,* she said at last. *It looks at me as if I have come early.*

"That's how it looks at everybody."

*It does not look at you like that.*

He didn't answer. He didn't know how it looked at him. He knew how it lowered its head.

There was a stair down, inside the arch. Everyone on the bridge knew it was there. You could see the top of it from the round, if you stood in the right place: the doorway, and inside it the dark, and a step going down into the dark, and another. Nobody went down. The wardens didn't. Marrit had stood with him in the round on his first morning and pointed at the doorway and said, *That's not ours. We keep the road. It keeps the door.* And she'd nodded up at the cat, who was asleep.

He'd asked what was down there. It was the only time in sixteen years she'd been angry with him.

"Read it," she'd said. "It's written over your head. It tells you not to ask, and you ask."

The stones of the round were warm at night. Even in winter. Even in a frost, when the bridge went white all over and the road rang under the carts, the stones in the round stayed dark and dry and warm as a hearth-stone in the morning, and the dogs on the bridge knew it and slept there. He'd lain on them himself once, on a bad night, with his cheek on the stone, and felt something in them, very low, like a cart going over a bridge a long way off. Then he'd got up and gone to bed, because it wasn't his business.

*Here the road of the one realm crossed the water.*

"On the bridge it's still one," Marrit said. She said it when the smiths argued in the round, or when the Wyldmarch woman's cat and a farm dog went nose to nose by the trough, or when a woman from the sun country and a man from the snow country couldn't make each other understand the price of a goat and stood there, both of them, laughing at it. "The rest of it went. Six bits of it, and a lot of lords. On the bridge it's still one."

---

Ysel came in at noon, late, with a load of quarry stone and a face like news.

She was a big woman with a bad wheel who carried stone for the river hill and had done since before Cadoc was born. She pulled her cart up out of the way by the drink-trough in the round, which she never did, and got down, which she never did, and stood there with her hands on her hips looking at the cat on the steps.

"They've gone into the hill," she said.

Ennis waited.

"The quarry. The new face, at the top, where the good stone is. Back in the spring. They were cutting, and the face went through. Into a room." She spread her hands. "A room. In the hill. Floor, walls. Old. Cut. Marks on the walls like the ones on your stones." She jerked her chin at the arch. "Like those."

"A room," said Marrit, from the gate.

"And a stair." Ysel looked at Ennis. "Going down."

Nobody said anything. The road went on round them: the drove, the carts, a child crying somewhere on the east arches. On the steps the cat lay with its head up and its eyes on the west, on the long white road going out toward the march-land and the hill.

"Who went down?" said Ennis.

"Who do you think? Him." Ysel spat, neatly, into the gutter. "Aldric. Lord of the river hill, he signs himself now, in ink, which his father never troubled with. With torches, and two of his men, and the boy who writes for him. Down the stair. Gone half a day. Came up white." She picked at her teeth with a fingernail. "Shut the quarry to everybody but his own. I've been cutting off the low face ever since, and paid the same, and nobody's said a word, and that's how I know it's something."

"What's down there?"

"How would I know? I'm not one of his." She climbed back up onto her cart. "But he's had a man go north. Up the road. Your road. Riding hard." She took the reins. "He's writing to somebody."

She went on through the east gate with her wheel squealing, and Ennis stood in the round in the noon sun and looked at the doorway in the arch, and the dark inside it, and the first step down.

*She smelled of fear,* said Aurveth from the parapet. *The stone-woman. Under the dust.*

"Ysel's not frightened of anything."

*She was frightened of that.*

---

That night the cat wasn't on the steps.

He went out late, as he did, to look at the fire from below and see that the gates were barred. The round was dark and warm. The fire burned on top of the tower against the stars. And the steps were bare.

He stood a moment, with his heart going, before he saw it.

It had come down off the steps and lain across the arch itself, in the doorway, across the threshold of the stone, so that its great grey body filled the opening and its head lay on its paws in the dark mouth of the door with the first step down behind it. Its eyes were open. He saw the pale of them. It was looking at him.

He didn't bow. He didn't know whether you did, at night. He stood there, and it looked at him, and then it shut its eyes.

In the morning it was back on the steps in the sun, as if it had never moved. But that night it was in the door again. And the next. And every night after that, all summer.

---

From the top of the tower, in the first weeks of summer, you could see the hill working.

He watched it every morning when he'd fed the fire. Three and a half leagues down the river, the pale face of the quarry, and at the top of it, where the good stone was, a small dark mark like a thumbprint. Carts on the river road below it that weren't stone-carts. Smoke. Once, at dusk, a line of lights going up the hill and into the dark mark and not coming out.

He watched it, and went down, and bowed, and kept the road.

*You are thinking about the hill,* said Aurveth.

"I'm thinking about the felloe on that cart."

*You are thinking about the hill and putting the cart in front of it, so I will not see.* She lifted her head off the parapet and looked at him with one gold eye. *I can feel you put it there. It is like a man standing in front of a door.*

He laughed. He couldn't help it. She waited, as she always did, for him to finish.

*I do not understand what is funny,* she said.

---

The College men came at midsummer.

There were three of them, down the north road on good horses, with a string of pack-mules behind and two hired men to see to the mules. They came into the round by the north way in the morning in long dark gowns gone pale with dust, and the gowns were the first thing Ennis saw and the rings were the second. They had rings on their hands. The oldest of them had a heavy one, with marks cut in it, and the two younger had lighter ones, and they rode in that order, the old one first, and nobody had to tell anybody on the bridge which was which.

They didn't stop at the trough. They stopped at the arch.

The old one got down off his horse, stiffly, and gave the reins to one of the young ones without looking, and stood at the foot of the tower steps with his head back, and read the verse. Out loud. In a dry clear voice that carried across the round, a voice used to rooms where people kept quiet.

He read it right through. Every line. Ennis, who had read it aloud nearly every day since she came, heard it said in another man's mouth for the first time, and didn't like it.

"*And do not ask,*" said the old man, at the end, and smiled.

The two younger ones had taken things out of their saddlebags. Glasses. Little discs of green glass in frames of brass, which they held up to their eyes, and lowered, and held up again, and looked at each other. One of them knelt down in the round and laid his glass flat on the warm stones and stared at it, and frowned, and wrote something in a book.

The cat lay on the steps above them and watched.

The old man didn't bow to it. He looked at it, a long time, with his head on one side, like a carter looking over a horse somebody else wants to sell him. Then he looked at the doorway in the arch, at the dark behind the cat.

"You're a warden," he said to Ennis. He didn't turn round to say it.

"Yes."

"Of the bridge."

"Yes."

"And the door?" The old man nodded at it. "Who keeps that?"

"It keeps itself." Ennis looked at the cat. "With help."

The old man followed his eyes, and looked at the cat again. The cat looked back at him. It didn't lower its head.

"What's below?"

"It says not to ask."

"Yes," said the old man. "It says." He turned round at last, and he had a pleasant face, lined, tired from the road, with eyes that went over Ennis once from his boots to his crooked nose and came back down again with what they wanted. "We measure."

He held out his hand to the young one for the reins, and got back up on his horse, and the three of them rode on through the west gate and away down the bridge toward the march-land with the mules behind them, and at the end of the seventh arch they turned off the road and down the river track along the west bank, toward the hill.

*He smells of ink and cold,* said Aurveth.

"He's a scholar."

*He smells of ink and cold and he did not bow.*

"Not everybody bows."

*Everybody bows.* She was quiet a moment, on the parapet, with her head up, watching the dust go down the river road. *I would like to put my foot on him.*

"Well, you can't."

*I know.* She put her chin back down on the stone. *I would like to.*

---

After that the road went thin.

Not all at once. A summer is a long time on a bridge and there were good days still, full ones, with the drove and the carts and the Wyldmarch riders and the children. But there were fewer of them, and the ones who came talked. Men on the river road. Men on the hill who weren't quarrymen. Iron going down to the hill on carts, a great deal of iron, bar and pig and old scrap, from the smiths in the iron hills who'd argued about charcoal; and charcoal going after it; and a smell, the carters said, on the wind off the hill some evenings, sweet, like a hive when the comb's being taken, but burnt.

A family from the sun country came as far as the round, and stood there, and the woman looked down the river road at the hill a long time with her hand over her eyes, and said something to her husband, and they turned their carts round in the round, which took a great deal of doing, and went back the way they'd come.

"They'll go round by the fords," said Marrit. "Thirty leagues out of their way."

"Why?"

Marrit shrugged. "Why does a horse shy?"

Ysel stopped coming. Somebody else brought the quarry stone, a young man Ennis didn't know, who didn't stop or talk. When Ennis asked after Ysel, he said she'd gone up the hill to work for the lord, at the new face, for twice the pay, and then he looked at the cat on the steps and didn't say anything else, and drove on.

---

He took Aurveth down to look, in the end, because she asked.

*I want to see it,* she said, one evening when the road was empty. *The hill. I want to see what you are always standing in front of.*

They went at dusk. He didn't ride her much. She didn't like it and neither did he; a warden's place was on the road, and the road was underfoot. But he climbed up onto her neck behind the head, where the plates left a narrow ridge to sit astride, and she went off the parapet and dropped, and caught the air under the third arch so low that the wyverns in their roost went up screaming, and then climbed out over the river, slowly, beating, with the bridge going away small and white behind them and the fire on the tower a spark.

The river went south under them in the last light. The march-land was dark. The hill came up out of it, the long pale face of the quarry and the dark mark at the top, and the mark had grown into a hole. A black mouth in the white face, with timber round it, and a road cut up to it, and sheds, a great many sheds, long and low, with smoke coming out of holes in their roofs, and men. And lights. Torches going in at the black mouth, one after another, and the lights going down and down inside the hill until they were too small to see.

The wind turned and came up the hill toward them.

Aurveth stopped flying.

He felt it before he understood it. Through the mark, through his palm on her neck, all through him at once: she had smelled something, and every part of her had gone tight, and the beat of her wings had stopped dead, so that they hung for a moment over the dark hill on spread wings and began to fall. Then she turned. She turned so hard and so fast he lost his seat and went over sideways and hung on to the ridge with both arms and his face against her neck, and she went away from the hill, low, back up the river, flying as he had never felt her fly, short hard beats, not looking back.

*What is it,* she said. *What is it, what is that, what is that smell.*

He could smell it now. Faint, on her, on the wind. Sweet. Hives, and burning.

*It is in me,* she said. *It went in. Up my nose and in. It is warm. I do not like it. I do not like it, Ennis, I do not like that it is warm.*

"It's gone. We're past it. It's gone."

*It is not gone. I can still feel where it was.*

She put him down in the round and went up onto the parapet and lay along it with her head toward the north, away from the hill, and didn't speak again that night. He sat on the warm stones of the round below her a long time, with his back against the north stone, *to the cold*, and his hand open on his knee with the mark on it. It was warm. It was always warm. He could feel her through it, on the parapet above him, very still, as a beast is still when it has smelled something on the wind and is waiting for it to come closer.

On the steps, in the door, the cat lay with its eyes open and looked south.

---

Two summers before, they'd walked the whole of the long road.

Wardens did it once. Marrit had, when she was young; the one before her had. You went north to the last stone in the snow-country and laid your hand on it, and turned round, and came south down the whole of the road to the sea, and laid your hand on the last stone there, and came home. And on the way, wherever a stone had gone soft with weather or moss had got into the letters, you stopped and cut them fresh with the chisel and the mallet you carried, so that whoever came after you could read them.

It took them most of a summer. She'd grumbled the first week, at the walking, and then stopped grumbling and walked at his shoulder through the snow-country and the river-country and the oak forests in the south as if she'd been born on the road, and every stone they came to, he put his hand on it. It was like touching a gatepost. You knew the house was there.

They were coming home, the last of the summer, through the big forest in the south where the light comes down green and gold, when they came to a stone he'd cut on the way down, a month before, still sharp, still white. He put his hand on it. It was warm from the sun.

"Nineteen," he'd said. He was pleased. It meant they were nearly home. "Nineteen to the bridge."

*You say that at every stone,* said Aurveth.

"I say the number at every stone."

*You say it as if it were good news.*

"It is good news."

*It is a number.*

"It's a number that's smaller than the last one."

She thought about that for half a league of road. Then she said, *Yes. That is good news,* in a voice he'd never heard her use, and he'd laughed with his mouth shut all the way to the next stone.

---

The lord's men came at the end of summer.

The songs of the river hill call them the first knights. On the bridge that morning they were a hundred men or so, on foot, with carts.

Ennis saw them from the top of the tower with the oil-jar in his hands. The mist was on the water and the bridge went out of it white, and out of the mist at the west end, on the seventh arch, came the carts. Big ones, heavy, drawn by oxen, coming slow, and the sound of them came up to him over the water before he could see what was on them. Iron. Iron sliding on iron. The noise a chain makes when the cart it's on goes over a stone.

He went down the stair so fast he dropped the jar.

He heard it break above him and didn't go back. He came out of the arch onto the top of the steps beside the cat, and the cat was on its feet.

He'd never seen it on its feet in the morning. It stood across the top of the steps, all of it, its head lowered, looking west along the bridge, and it was much bigger standing than lying, so much bigger that he went back a step from it without meaning to and felt the arch against his shoulders. Its mane stood up. Its tail went once from side to side, slow, and was still.

"Marrit!" he shouted. "Cadoc! The road! Clear the road!"

---

There were people on the bridge. There were always people on the bridge.

He went down the steps and across the round and out through the west gate onto the arches, running, shouting, and they turned and looked at him: a drover with forty sheep, a woman with a handcart, two boys, the white ox with the hump standing on the fourth arch looking down at the river. He sent them back. He took the woman's handcart himself and turned it and ran it back up into the fort and through the round and out the east gate, and Marrit was there, on her feet with her stick, sending them on, east, over the seven east arches to the Saltreach bank. "Go on. Go on, go. Off the bridge. Don't stop." The drover wouldn't leave his sheep. Marrit hit him with the stick. He went, and the sheep went after him because the dogs did, and the ox went after the sheep because it had decided to.

"Cadoc," said Ennis.

"Gone." Marrit didn't look at him. "Went over the side at the west gate when he saw the carts. Swimming for the east bank. Good boy." She said it as if she meant it. "Good boy. He'll live."

"Go with them."

"I'm a warden."

"Marrit…"

"I'm a warden fifty years." She looked at him at last. "And I can't run, and I can't fight, and I'll be in your way." She put her hand on his arm, her old hand, hard and dry. "Go and stand where you stand. I'll get these off the bridge."

She went. He watched her a moment, going east over the arches with her stick behind the sheep, slow, very straight. Then he went back into the round.

---

They came through the west gate in the order they'd been put in, and it was a long order, and it took time.

Men first. Forty or fifty, in leather, with spears and with poles, and at the end of each pole a pot, black, with a lid. Then the carts, with the oxen blowing and the carters walking at their heads, and on the carts iron: bar, and long links of chain, each link as long as a man's forearm, thick as a wrist, piled on the cart-beds like hawsers on a quay. Then more men, carrying a thing between four of them on poles that clinked when it moved, a net. A net made of chain. And another. And behind them, walking apart, five or six men in plain brown with nothing in their hands at all, and a smell coming off them, sweet, that Ennis knew.

Behind them, on their horses, the College men. The old one and the two young. They didn't come into the round. They stopped in the gate.

And behind everybody, out on the seventh arch where the bridge touched the west bank, a man on a grey horse sat and didn't come any further. He was too far off to see properly. A big man, bareheaded. He sat his horse on the last arch of the bridge with the march-land behind him and watched, and he never once, that day or after, set foot on the white stones.

A young man came out of the press of the others into the round, with an inkhorn on a string round his neck and a board under his arm with paper on it. He looked about twenty. He looked at the cat on the steps, and went white, and stopped.

Ennis knew him.

He didn't know from where, for a moment. Then he did. A stone-cart, years back, nine or ten years, one of the quarry carts from the hill, with a boy riding on the load. The boy had climbed down in the round to see the cat, and stood at the foot of the steps a long time, and then put his hand on his heart and bowed, all by himself, because he'd seen Ennis do it. And the cat had looked at him. And the boy had run back to the cart so fast he fell over the tailboard, and lay on the stones in the back of it white as paper, and the next time the cart came over he'd climbed down and done it again.

"You bowed to it," said Ennis.

The young man looked at him. He opened his mouth. Then a man in front of him said something without turning round, and he went to the side of the round by the trough, out of the way, and stood there with the board against his chest, and opened his inkhorn, and began to write.

---

The man who'd spoken came across the round. He was square, grey, short-bearded, with a long knife on his belt and no spear. He stopped twenty paces from the steps, and looked up at the cat, standing above Ennis in the arch, and Ennis watched him decide not to look at it again.

"You're the warden."

"One of them."

"There's only you now." The square man nodded east, where Marrit had gone. "The old woman's off the bridge. The boy's in the river." He didn't say it unkindly. "My lord's compliments. You can go after them."

"It's not your lord's bridge."

"It's on his river."

"The river was here before him. So was the bridge." Ennis heard his own voice. It was steady. He was glad of that. "It's the road's."

"Then the road can come and tell me so." The square man looked past him, into the arch, at the doorway and the dark behind it. "We're going down. That's all. Through there. We have to bring the iron down, and the hill end's too narrow for it, and this end isn't." He shrugged. "Nobody's touching your bridge. Move, and nobody touches you."

"It says not to wake it."

The square man looked up at the verse over the arch. He couldn't read it. Ennis could see that he couldn't. But he knew what it said; somebody had read it to him, and very likely recently.

"It says keep the watch," the square man said. "We're going to." He turned his head and nodded to the men with the poles.

They took the lids off the pots.

---

He would remember it in pieces, after. What there was of after.

The smell first. Not the sweet smell. That came later. This was pitch, black and thick and choking, the smoke of it going up off the pole-ends in greasy rolls as the men touched torches to them, forty fires on forty poles all at once, and the round full of it, and the light, red, in the morning.

The cat came down the steps.

It didn't spring. He'd thought it would spring. It walked down, two steps, three, and stood on the round with its head low and its mane up, and the men with the poles came at it in a line, shouting, and the shout went up off the round and the tower walls and the fort walls and came back at them all, so loud the oxen in the gate screamed and tried to turn.

The cat went into them.

He saw a man go down under it. He saw a pole go spinning up into the air with its fire on the end and come down in the trough with a hiss. He saw the cat's great forefoot, as broad as a shield, come down on the stones of the round, and he felt it through the soles of his boots, the whole round jumping, and the bottom step of the tower came up out of the round on its edge like a plank when you tread on the end of it, and a man went under it as it fell. The weight, he thought, after. It must have been the weight. And the tower behind him shook. He felt the tower shake. He had leaned against it for sixteen years and it had never moved, and it moved, the whole white height of it, and a little dust came down out of the arch onto his shoulders.

But there were forty of them, and they had fire.

They came on in their line with the poles held out in front of them, pitch flaring, the burning pots swinging, and where the cat turned there was fire, and where it went there was fire, and men behind the fire with spears. They didn't try to kill it. He saw that, and didn't understand it until much later. They drove it. They pushed fire at it from three sides and left it the fourth, and the fourth side was the east gate, and the east arches, and the long white road going away over the river to the Saltreach bank.

He heard it roar.

He had never heard it make a sound. It roared once, in the round, with fire all round it and a pole jammed against its flank and the pitch from the pot running down its side in a long black burning tongue, and the sound of it went into the stones under him and through the tower at his back and out across the river, and every bird on the bridge went up at once off the parapets, and the wyverns, and the swallows out of the empty rooms, and the river under the arches went flat and strange for a moment, as if the wind had gone across it the wrong way.

Then it went.

East. Out through the east gate, with the fire behind it. He saw it go over the arches, grey and gold and black with smoke, running, the men running after it with the fire, and the white road going away under it, one arch, two, three, all seven, to the Saltreach end, and off the end of the bridge into the trees on the far bank, and gone.

The men stopped on the last arch with their poles held out at the trees, burning. Nothing came out.

It was very quiet in the round. A pole lay on the stones, smoking. A man by the trough sat holding his arm and making a high thin sound. Another lay where the cat had come down on him and didn't make any sound at all.

By the trough, with his board against his chest, the young man was writing.

---

*Ennis.*

She was in the air.

He'd felt her go up when the fires were lit; he'd felt her all the time, a long way back behind everything, a pressure and a heat and a voice saying his name over and over, and he hadn't been able to answer. Now he looked up and there she was, over the round, over the tower, beating, bronze in the morning sun, with her head down and her eyes on him.

*Ennis. I am coming down.*

*No.*

*I am coming down to you.*

*Stay up. Stay up there.*

There were men on the walls. He saw them now. On the fort walls, either side of the round, on the walk above the gates where the wardens had used to stand when there were twelve of them. Men with the chain-nets, the long heavy things that clinked, held out between four of them, ready, like eel-men holding a net over a pool.

*They have nets,* he said. *On the walls. Stay up.*

*You are on your own.*

*I know.*

*You are on your own and the cat has gone.*

*I know.* He was standing at the top of the steps. In the arch. With the dark of the doorway behind him and the first step down at his heels. He hadn't decided to stand there. He'd come back to it, as you come back to your own door. *Stay up.*

She didn't stay up.

She came down behind him. Not into the round, where the nets were. Behind him, onto the tower itself, onto the slope of the shining roof beside the fire, with her claws scraping on the copper so that the sound went through his teeth, and then down the side of the tower, head first, like a lizard down a wall, until she was over the arch, over him, over the verse, with her head down beside his and her great wings half open on either side of the doorway like a second arch.

It was the stupidest thing he had ever seen. He loved her so much he couldn't breathe.

*Now,* she said, *we are both on our own.*

---

They didn't come at him.

All morning, and into the afternoon, nobody came at him. The square man stood in the round with his arms folded and looked at him, and at the dragon over the arch, and at the doorway behind them both. Men took the dead man away. Men put the fires out. Men sat on the carts and ate bread. The oxen stood in the gate.

The old man from the College came into the round on foot at noon, with his gown held up out of the pitch, and stood beside the square man, and they talked. Ennis couldn't hear them. He saw the old man look up at Aurveth for a long time with his head on one side. He saw him say something, and the square man shake his head, and the old man say it again, and the square man shrug.

*What are they saying?* said Aurveth.

*I don't know.*

*You can hear things. You hear the smiths in the round.*

*I can't hear that.*

*Then they are saying something about me.* Her head hung over him, warm. He could feel her breathing, the great slow heave of it, on the back of his neck. *I can smell the sweet-smoke. On the brown men. It is in their clothes. It is in their hands.*

*I know.*

*I do not want it in me again.*

*I know.*

The men in brown stood together at the side of the round by the east gate, in the shade, five of them. They hadn't moved all day. One was old and small, with yellow fingers. One was young. They didn't talk to each other. They stood and looked at Aurveth on the tower, steadily, all of them, as the College men had looked at the cat.

Behind them, by the trough, the young clerk sat on the edge of it with his board on his knees and his inkhorn, and wrote, and looked up, and wrote.

---

They threw the nets at the turn of the afternoon.

He never saw who gave the word. He heard it: the chain going, the long rattling rush of it, from the walls on both sides at once. He looked up. The nets came over the round in the sun, spreading, heavy, black, slow, like a cast net going out over a pool, that opens and hangs before it drops, and they didn't drop on the round. They dropped on the tower. On her.

She came down off the tower with the nets on her. All of it. The nets and her and the noise, down the face of the tower in a great sliding roar of iron and bronze, over him, past him, and he threw himself flat on the top step and felt her go over, and the wind of her, and the end of her tail go across his back like a beam swinging. She hit the round.

She fought. She was young and she was strong and she was a dragon, and she fought the nets on the stones of the round with the men hauling on the ropes at the edges, and the stones cracked under her, and one of the men on the ropes went down under her and didn't get up. But the nets were chain, and there were three of them, and more men on every rope than he could count, and every time she threw the chain off one wing another rope came tight across it.

*Ennis. Ennis. Ennis.*

He was off the step. He was down in the round. He had his hands on the chain, on the nearest net, pulling at it, at the links, and somebody hit him from behind, hard, on the side of the head, and the round went sideways and the stones came up and hit him in the face.

When he could see again, the men in brown were walking out from the side of the round with the pots.

---

They chained him to the beast-ring by the arch.

It was an old ring, an iron one, set in the wall of the tower at the foot of the steps for travellers to tie a beast to while they drank. Nobody had used it in his lifetime. Nobody on the bridge tied a beast. They put a chain round his waist and through the ring and locked it, and left his hands loose, because there was nothing he could do with them.

He could see her. That was what they meant him to have. She lay ten paces off in the middle of the round, in the nets, on her side, with her head toward him and her wings pinned flat under the chain, and round her, in a ring, five pots on the stones, smoking.

The smoke was sweet.

It went up off the pots in thin grey threads and leaned together in the still air over her and came down on her, and he could smell it from where he sat. Hives. The comb being taken. Burnt. He watched it go in at her nostrils every time she breathed, and he felt it come into her through the mark in his hand, warm.

*It is in me,* she said.

*I know.*

*It is warm. It is in me and it is warm and it is making me… it is making me…*

She didn't finish. He felt her not finish. He felt the thing she was trying to say go slow and soft and come apart, like a loaf left out in rain.

The old binder with the yellow fingers came and squatted by one of the pots, and took a pinch of something out of a pouch at his belt, and dropped it on the coals, and the smoke came up thicker.

---

Three days.

He would have said, if anyone had asked him, that he couldn't sit chained to a wall for three days and watch them do that to her. He found out that you can. You can do anything that's done to you, if they don't let you stop.

The first day was the worst, because she fought it. She fought it as she had fought the nets, with everything, and it didn't help, and he felt her fight it until she was so tired that the tiredness came through the mark into him and laid him down on the stones by the ring, and he slept, and woke, and she was still fighting.

*Talk to me,* she said, in the night. Her voice was thick. *Ennis. Talk. Say something.*

He said the stones.

It was what came. He sat against the tower with the chain round his waist and his right hand open on his knee, on the mark, and said them to her, one after another, down the whole long road. The last stone in the snow-country, at the head of the valley, with the ice on it. Thirty. Twenty-nine. The one by the waterfall that had fallen over and that he'd stood up again with a crowbar and both of them shoving. The one with a bird's nest on top. Down and down, into the river-country, into the oak forest.

*Nineteen,* she said.

*Nineteen.*

*To the bridge.* A long breath. *That is good news.*

---

On the second day they went down.

He watched them do it. He had to; it was ten paces from him, up nine steps. They brought the carts up into the round, as close to the steps as they'd go, round her, round the pots, and unloaded the iron, and carried it up the steps on their shoulders, two men to a length of chain, and in through the arch and down. Down the stair that nobody went down. He watched the first two men go into the doorway with the chain between them and the dark close over them, and heard their feet going down inside the tower, down and down, and the clink of the chain getting fainter.

Nobody came out till the shadows had gone round. Then they did. White. Sweating. One of them sat down on the top step and put his head between his knees.

After that it went on all day. Iron in, men in, men out. Torches in, a great many torches. The College men went in, the old one and both the young, with their glasses and their books, and were gone till dusk. The square man went in, and came out, and stood on the top step and looked down at the round with something in his face that Ennis couldn't read, and said to nobody, "All the way under the river."

Then he wiped his mouth with the back of his hand, as if he'd drunk something bad, and went down again.

At dusk the stones of the round were warmer than he'd ever felt them. He put his free hand flat on them and they were warm as a beast's flank, and under the warm, very low, a long way off, there was the thing he'd felt once on a bad night, with his cheek on the stone. The cart going over the far bridge. Going over. And over.

The young clerk came out of the arch last of all, with his board, and stood on the steps a moment with his hand on the doorway. Then he came down and sat by the trough. He didn't write. He sat with the board on his knees and looked at nothing.

After a while he got up and filled a cup at the trough and brought it over to the ring, and held it out.

Ennis took it. He drank.

"You bowed to it," he said again.

"I was a boy." The clerk didn't look at him. He looked at the arch. "I couldn't sleep. After. A week. I couldn't sleep." He took the cup back. His hand was shaking. "I didn't want to."

He went back to the trough.

That night, for the first time in Ennis's life, nobody carried the oil up, and the fire on the tower burned down to red, and to nothing, and the top of the tower was dark against the stars.

---

On the third day she stopped fighting.

He felt it happen. In the morning, when the binders put fresh coals in the pots. She'd been holding on all night to something, he didn't know what, the shape of herself, the edges, as a man in the river holds on to a rope. And in the morning the old binder put a pinch of the brown stuff on the coals, and the smoke came up, and she breathed it, and let go.

He knew what her sleep was like; he'd felt it every night for years, through the mark, a slow warm weight, a big beast lying down. This was nothing like it. This was more like a door. A door left open in a wind, swinging. He could feel the inside of her through it as he'd never felt it, all of her, open, warm, slow, with nothing in front of any of it.

*Ennis,* she said. From very far off, like a voice across the river at dusk, from the other bank, when you can't see who's calling. *Ennis. It is warm.*

*I know.*

*I am not frightened any more.*

*I know.*

*Is that good news?*

He didn't answer that.

---

The binders came at noon.

All five of them, out of the shade by the east gate, walking across the round to the ring, and the square man with them, and behind them the old man from the College with his gown held up. They stopped in front of him. The old binder with the yellow fingers squatted down on his heels in front of Ennis, close, and looked at him, at his face and then down at his right hand, open on his knee, and the mark on it.

"There," the binder said, to the young one behind him. "In the hand. You see it?"

"Yes."

"Bronze. That's her." The old man looked up into Ennis's face. He had small brown eyes and they were quite kind. "We've come for the witness," he said. "In her. You understand?"

Ennis didn't.

"She's seen it," said the old man patiently. "The door. The stair. What's going down it. All of this." He waved a yellow hand at the round, at the carts, the iron, the pots. "She's seen all of it, and she'll remember it, and one day she'll have somebody else, and tell him." He shook his head. "Can't have that. So we take it out."

"Take it out."

"You." The old man nodded at the mark. "You're the witness. In her. You're where she keeps it." He held up his hand, his left hand, with the fingers spread. "We take you out of her. And she forgets. Not everything. Enough. Where you were, there's nothing to hang it on."

The square man was looking at the river.

"And then," said Ennis.

Nobody answered. That was the answer. He looked at the square man, and the square man went on looking at the river.

"Why not just kill us both?" said Ennis. His voice sounded very far off to him, as if it were somebody else's, across the water. "You'd have to kill me, and she'd go with me. Everyone knows that. Why not let her go with me?"

"Kill a dragon on the Oneroad," said the old man from the College, from behind them all, quite gently, "and six lands sing it by winter." He looked over at Aurveth in her nets, in her smoke. "Let her fly off empty, and she's weather."

---

He understood, then.

He sat against the tower with the chain round his waist and understood it all at once, as you see the whole of a river from the top of the tower. If he died with her in him, she'd come after him. Down the mark. Down whatever it was that ran between them, like water over a weir, as every beast that ever lost its partner had gone after them, and lain down, and not got up. Everyone knew that. You didn't have to be a warden to know it. Everyone who had ever loved a beast knew it, and had been glad of it, because it meant you'd never have to leave each other.

If they cut him out first, she'd live.

He could hold on. He knew that too. He could feel it, as plainly as the chain round his waist. The young binder had his hand out now, reaching for the mark, and Ennis could close his fist on it, and close everything else, every door in him, and hold her, and they'd have to kill him with her still in him, and she'd go after him, and they'd be together, wherever it was that beasts and wardens went.

The binder's hand stopped an inch from his.

"Easier," the old man said, "if you don't hold."

---

*Aurveth.*

*Ennis.* Far off. Slow. Across the water. *It is warm.*

*Listen to me.*

*I am listening.* A pause. *I am trying to listen.*

*They're going to do something. To you. To me. In a minute. You'll feel it.* He kept his voice steady. In his head, where nobody could hear it but her, he kept it as steady as he'd ever kept anything. *Don't fight it.*

*I have stopped fighting.*

*I know. Don't start. Let them.*

*Let them what?*

He didn't tell her. He couldn't. If he told her, she'd hold on, and she was a dragon and he was only a man, and she'd hold on harder than he could ever let go.

*Let go of me,* he said.

*I do not understand.*

*I know. You never do.* He felt his shoulders go, and his mouth stay shut. Laughing. Even now. *Let go of me. And then forget me.*

*Forget what?*

*Everything. Me. All of it. Let it go. You're young. The road's long.* He opened his hand, flat on his knee, with the mark up, warm, the old bronze coin of her in his palm. The young binder's fingers came down on it, cold. *Then go north. When they let you up. Don't come down. Don't look for anything. Go north, to the cold, where you came from.*

*To the cold,* said Aurveth. Slowly. Somewhere very far away, she was trying to see the north stone. He felt her try. *The one you put your hand on.*

*Yes.*

*Will you come?*

The old binder had got up off his heels. He walked across the round to her, through the smoke, and squatted again, and put his hand flat on her head between the eyes. She didn't move. He began to talk, low, the same few sounds over and over, words in no tongue Ennis knew, steady as a man pulling oars.

The young binder took a knife out of his belt. Short. Thin. Very bright.

*Will you come?* said Aurveth again.

*Go north,* said Ennis. *Live.*

The knife went across his palm.

It didn't hurt. He'd thought it would; he'd thought there'd be a great deal of hurt and he'd braced himself for it, and there was only a line of cold, from one side of his hand to the other, straight through the middle of the mark. And the old man on the far side of the round said one word, not loud, a short hard sound like a stone going into water.

And she wasn't there.

---

It was the quiet he hadn't known about.

All those years of her, every hour, awake and asleep, at the back of everything, the warmth and the weight and the voice; and he hadn't known it made a sound until it stopped. Like a mill-race, that you stop hearing, that you only hear when they close the sluice and the wheel stops. There was nothing in his hand. There was a cut, bleeding, and under the cut a mark the colour of an old coin, going dull while he looked at it, and nothing.

He said her name. In his head. Where she'd always been.

Nothing came back. Not a word. Not a warmth. Not the feeling of a big beast a long way off, lying down. It was a room with nobody in it.

He sat by the ring with his hand open on his knee and the blood running off it onto the white stones, and found that he was still alive, and that he would be for a little while, and that he didn't mind.

By the trough the young clerk had stopped writing. He sat with the pen in the air over the paper, looking at Ennis's hand, and his face had gone the colour of the board. Then he put the pen down to the paper again, and wrote. Slowly. One line. Ennis watched him do it.

---

They took the nets off her at dusk.

They did it carefully, from the walls, with long hooks, hauling the chain back up off her a length at a time, and the men on the walls didn't go down from the walls afterward. They stayed up there, with the nets gathered again between them and held ready, all along the walk above the round.

The binders put the lids on the pots. The smoke thinned and went.

She lay on the stones. A long time. Then her head came up.

He watched her. There was nothing else he could do. He watched her lift her head off the stones of the round, slowly, and look about her, at the walls and the men and the carts and the tower, as if she had just been put down there out of the sky by somebody else. She got up. She stood. She shook herself all over, as she did after rain, and the light went down her in a long bronze shiver.

She looked at the tower. She looked at the arch.

She looked at him.

Her eye went over him. Gold, the size of a cart's hub. It went over him, chained at the foot of the steps with his hand in his lap, and it didn't stop, and went on to the trough, and the clerk, and the gate.

Then she went up.

---

She went up off the round in one long heave of wings that blew the last of the smoke flat across the stones and set the men on the walls staggering. Up past the tower, past the dead fire on the roof, up into the evening, beating hard. And then she turned.

He'd thought she'd go. North, as he'd told her. Straight, away, like a bird when you open your hands.

She didn't. She went out over the river, west, wide, and turned back over the bridge, and came round again, low, over the round, and the men on the walls lifted their nets, and she pulled up hard and went away from them and came round again. And again. Over the bridge and the island and the water, round and round, in the last light, with the river going red under her.

She was crying.

He knew the sound. A dragon doesn't cry as a man does. It's a sound in the throat, high and long, like a mooring rope stretched too far in a flood, and he'd heard it from her once, the winter a hard frost killed the old ox that used to sleep by the trough. She made it now. Over and over, going round over the river. She looked down at the bridge as she passed, and at the tower, and at the white road going out both ways into the dusk, and at everything, as if she'd put something down somewhere and couldn't think what, or where, or what it had been.

She didn't look at him. There was no reason she should.

He sat by the ring in the dusk with his hand open in his lap and watched her go round. The men on the walls watched her. The square man in the round. The clerk, by the trough, with his board, had stopped writing and was only looking up, with his mouth open, like a boy.

On the seventh arch the man on the grey horse turned his horse round and rode away into the march-land.

She went round once more, high, over the island, with the dark coming up out of the river round the piers and the last of the sun on her, so that for a moment, the whole length of her, she was new bronze.

Then she turned north, up the river, toward the cold, and went on crying, alone, over the water, and didn't know for whom.
