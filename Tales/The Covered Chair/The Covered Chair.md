On the ledges they say a dragon has two songs and a hum. The hum is for the living. It goes on under everything, low, and means *here, you're here*. The songs are cries. One goes down at the end, for the dead. The other goes up, for one that comes home. A child of the hold learns the three before it learns its letters, and most children of the hold never learn their letters, so that is all right.

Nobody had a word for what the dragons did on the evening the six went up. There hadn't been a need. In three hundred years of keeping dragons on the Scarfell the Clan had never heard every one of them stop at once.

---

Arn was on the third ledge.

Not the high ledge, where the riders stood. He'd been there at noon and couldn't stay. Fifty riders and their dragons on a shelf of black stone, the dragons humming, the riders not talking, all of them looking up the long brown slope to where the moor ran up under the crags above Coldtarn, five leagues off. He'd stood among them an hour and then gone down the cut steps to the third ledge and sat on it with his legs over the edge.

Teo's ledge. Teo had sat there to watch the riders go out, every morning he was at the hold, from when he was small enough to be carried up. You could see their faces from the third ledge as they went off, he'd said. From the high ledge you only saw their backs.

The stone was warm from the day. Below him the hold. The ring of black longhouses inside the rampart, the hall with its scale roof, the yard, smoke going up straight. Nobody in the yard. Everyone who could climb was on a ledge or a roof or the rampart. Women with children on their hips. Old men. Leir the keeper on the pen rail, far below, with his barrow empty beside him, looking up.

And above all of it, on the skyline, the six.

---

The first of them had been there three days.

From here they were hills. That was the trouble with them. Six humps on the high moor where there'd been no humps, grey and dark in the evening light, very still, and if you looked away and back you couldn't be sure which was the one that had been there before. Then something on one of them caught the sun, a horn, and threw a long gold line across the moor, and you were sure.

The dragons had hummed at them two nights through. Every dragon on the Roosts and the lower cliffs and in the pens. He'd lain in the hall and felt it in the bench under him, in his teeth. Nobody had slept. Nobody had said so.

Behind him on the cut steps, boots. Heavy. A man breathing as if he'd carried something up.

He didn't turn round.

"That's a long stair," said Ulric, "for an old man."

"It's a long stair for anybody."

"Whoever cut it didn't like being followed." Ulric came out onto the ledge and stood behind him, with the evening on his big grey head and the white scar through his eyebrow. He looked at the ledge. At Arn sitting on it. "Budge up."

"It's Teo's."

"I know whose it is. I hauled him back onto it by his belt when he was six." Ulric lowered himself down, slowly, with a grunt, and sat, and put his legs over. The ledge took them both. Just. "He bit me."

Arn looked at the moor.

---

They went up at dusk.

He saw them stand. All six together, as one beast gets up off the ground, and the first thing he knew of it was the heather. The whole slope of the high moor moving at once, going flat, outward, a great grey ring of it spreading from the place where they'd lain, and the ring coming on down the long slope toward the hold as wind comes over barley, until the ling on the slope below the Roosts lay down too and it was only air, and it reached them, and went past.

Then the hum stopped.

He'd never heard the hold without it. Nobody had. It was there when you were born and there when they burned you. It stopped as a held note stops, and there was nothing behind it, and in the nothing he heard a child cry out on the rampart and be hushed.

The six were in the air.

Over the crags. Over Coldtarn. Over the whole Scarfell, so big that he couldn't tell how far off they were and so slow they seemed to hang. The one with the forest round it, and the trees in the air with it, their roots trailing. The long pale one. The low one, glowing at the edges like a coal pulled out of a fire. The one with the horn, lighting the undersides of the others. The one with a broken temple on its back, and a bell in the temple, ringing, a long note that came down over the moor a breath behind the sight of it. And in the middle the grey one, the first one, the one from under the river, with the six places in its back.

They turned. Together. Slowly. As starlings turn over a stubble field before they go.

Then east.

Toward the Worldsend. Over the last of the Scarfell and the dark foothills and the first white mountains, getting smaller, the horn's light going small with them, until he couldn't tell them from the dusk, and then there was only the dusk.

---

Nobody on the ledges moved.

The dragons lay as they'd lain, heads out toward the empty moor. Not a sound from any of them. Fifty dragons above him and a hundred more in the pens below, and not one breath he could hear.

"Well," said Ulric.

Arn waited for the rest of it. There wasn't any.

"Somebody'll want to know what it means," he said.

"The farmer'll know."

"The farmer never knows anything."

"That's why they tell him," said Ulric. He looked at the dark where the six had gone. "Nobody ever told me anything. I had to find out."

Below them, in the yard, a dog began to bark. Then another. Then all the dogs of the hold together, at nothing, at the quiet, and somebody shouted at them and they went on.

Ulric got up. It took him a while. He stood on the ledge and put his hand on Arn's shoulder, heavily, and left it there a moment, and took it away.

"Come down and eat," he said. "Your father's chair won't sit in itself."

"Nobody's sitting in it."

"I know," said Ulric. "I was making a joke. Teo's were better."

He went back up the stair. Arn listened to his boots go up, slow, one step and then the next. He sat on the ledge until the moor was black and the stars were out over the place where the six had been, and the dragons above him still hadn't made a sound.

---

The chair was under its cloth.

He sat beside it that night, on the bench, as he'd sat every night since Teo burned. The two tables full. Red on one side of the hall, grey on the other, eating, not saying much, every head turning at every sound outside as if the six might come back down through the roof.

Black oak. Dragons up the legs, dragons on the arms. A dragon across the back, its wings out, worn smooth in the middle. He'd sat in it once as a boy, when his father was out on the moor, with his feet not reaching the floor and his hands on the dragons' heads, and been found, and been beaten for it in the yard with a strap while Teo, who was four, cried as if it were him.

The cloth was a horse-blanket. Grey wool. The kind the hold's women wove by the dozen every winter.

He remembered saying *Cover it*. The night of the pyre, in the rain, with the smoke coming in at the door and his hands still smelling of the torch. He'd come in from the yard and seen the chair with his father's cloak on it, where Teo had put it, and Teo's armband on the seat where he'd put that himself after the ford, and said *Cover it*. To somebody. He didn't remember who. When he came back in at dawn there was a horse-blanket over the lot, and he'd never lifted it, and he didn't know where his father's cloak was now or the armband either.

Under the blanket, under there somewhere. Or not.

He'd never looked.

---

Gorm Askell was in the tally-house in the morning.

Of course he was. He was always in the tally-house in the morning, among the shelves, with the shutters half open for the light and the brazier going for the damp, writing. Long and sour, with ink to the elbow and his bad leg up on a stool. He looked at Arn in the doorway and didn't get up.

"Paper," said Arn.

"What for?"

"For the farmer."

Gorm put his pen down.

"The farmer," he said, "has paper. The farmer had a quire off me at midwinter for his letters to the lion-woman, and a quire at the thaw, and I've no doubt he's covered them both in that hand of his, with the letters falling over like drunk men." He looked at Arn. "What's the farmer want more paper for?"

"He didn't ask."

"Then why's he getting it?"

Arn didn't know how to say it. He'd looked in at the salt-store door at Gullhaven, the night after the burning, and seen the farmer at a trestle among the salt sacks with the harbour-master's pen in his fist and his tongue in his teeth, writing. A whole page, both sides, the letters getting smaller and smaller at the bottom where he'd run out of room and not wanted to ask for another sheet. Nobody had made him. A man who'd started writing things down like that wasn't going to stop.

"Because he'll need it," he said.

Gorm looked at him for a while longer. Then he got up, which took some time, and went along the shelves to the end and took down a ream in its wrapper, the Clan's grey paper, and put it on the table.

"It's damp," he said.

"It's not damp."

"Everything in this hold is damp. I've told your father so for twenty years and I've told you since." He pushed it across the table. "Do you want me to write him a line with it?"

"No."

---

He wrote it himself. At Gorm's table, on a scrap off the end of a sheet, with Gorm's pen.

He was slow at it. He'd always been slow at it. His father had had a steward to read him his levies and Arn had had Gorm, and the dues-school, and a brother who could read a page while Arn was still on the first line and would read it aloud to him, quick and bored, so that Arn never had to say he couldn't keep up.

He spoiled the first. The *F* came out with too many arms. He spoiled the second because Gorm was watching him and his hand knew it. The third time Gorm got up and went to the far shelf and stood with his back turned, looking at nothing, and Arn wrote it.

*For the farmer.*

Three words. He looked at them. They were the right words and they looked like a child's.

He tucked the scrap under the string.

"I'll send it up with the boy," said Gorm, from the shelf. Not turning round.

---

The boy was Haki.

Twelve now. Thin, quick, with a pony he'd been given for running messages and treated like a lord's horse. He did the chief's stall. He'd done it since Teo burned, when Arn had put Teo's bay in it because there was nowhere else Arn could bear to see it, and Haki had come with the bay as the bay's halter came with it, and nobody had sent him away.

Arn found him there with the paper already tied on the pony behind the saddle. Haki was in the stall. Brushing the bay.

Skerry. Teo's gelding. Bay, with a white sock behind and an eye like a wet stone. He was fat. Nobody rode him. Haki walked him round the yard on a rope morning and evening and fed him and brushed him and talked to him, and in the winter he'd put a grey blanket over him, and the bay stood in the chief's stall in the warm and was a horse nobody rode.

Haki saw him and stopped brushing.

"Go on," said Arn. "Up to the caves. Give it to the farmer's mother, not to him. Don't say who it's from."

"It says who it's from," said Haki. "On the paper. Gorm said."

"It says it's for the farmer. It doesn't say who."

Haki thought about it. He was a careful boy. Teo had liked him.

"They'll know," he said.

"Go on."

He went. Arn stood in the stall door after he'd gone, and the bay put his head round and looked at him, and then put it down again and pulled at his hay, because Arn had never once in his life brought him an apple.

---

They sat at the chair on the second morning. Both halves.

It was Ulric's thought. He'd said it at the meal: they'd better be ready to say what the Clan would do when somebody came asking, and somebody would. The farmer, or the knights, or the reader with his glasses, or the Council with its contracts. So in the morning both tables were full, the riders of the ledges and the old ones and the heads of the steadings who'd come in to the hold when the six lay on the moor, and Gorm with his book at the end, and Arn on the bench by the chair, and Ulric across from him on the grey bench with his arms folded.

The Clan would fly. Where it was needed. All of it. Red and grey together, as they'd flown to the river.

He said it. Ulric said it after him, in other words. Brede said it for the old ones. Hulda Sarr said it, and then said it again louder because nobody at the far end had heard her the first time.

Nobody argued.

He sat with his hand on the bench beside the chair and waited for it. The quarrel. Somebody from the red end saying the grey had flown slow at Gullhaven, somebody from the grey saying the red had come home first and left them the dead to burn. It had been like that at every sitting since the pour. You put a thing on the table, and it went round, and before it had gone halfway somebody had picked up something else, and then they were on the old thing, which was always the same thing.

It went round. It came back to him. Nobody had picked anything up.

He looked across the hall at his uncle. Ulric had his eyebrows up.

---

Then Bjarke stood up.

From the red end. Young, loud, one of the high-ledge riders, with a cup he'd forgotten to put down.

"The three," he said.

The hall looked at him.

"If the Clan flies where the farmer says," said Bjarke, "and it will, because it always does now, then the farmer's three fly with the Clan. Under the thread. Red and grey. On their harness. As they were Sigrun's and Dag's and Imra's." He looked round at the red table. Several of them were nodding. "They're the Clan's. They were bred here. They're on the books. If we're the farmer's flights, the farmer's dragons can be ours."

He sat down.

There was a noise along the red table. Not a quarrel. Men agreeing with each other, low, and looking at Arn.

He'd wanted the three back. He'd stood at the gate in the torchlight, the spring the old one came home with her wing broken, and said so, in front of everyone, and the farmer had said yes, and the old one had said yes before him. The Clan's law was the Clan's law: a bonded dragon goes where its partner goes, and its partner was a barley farmer who went wherever he liked.

"I'll ask him," said Arn.

"Ask him what?" said Bjarke.

"What you said."

Across the hall Ulric unfolded his arms.

"I'll come with you," he said. "I want to see his face."

---

He rode up to the high moor that afternoon on his own.

On the black horse, the long way, up the brown slope past the turning for Coldtarn where the bier-road went, and on up over the shoulder where the heather began. He told nobody where he was going. Nobody asked. The hold was busy with being quiet.

It was spring on the Scarfell. Not much of it. Lambing on the low moor, the hold's ewes in their folds by the beck with the shepherds' fires smoking, and a raw green coming through in the wet places, and larks. Up here the snow lay on in the north sides of the hags, grey, and the wind came off the crags and found every gap in his coat.

He smelled it before he came over the last rise. Burning. Old burning, gone cold. And something under it he didn't know, sharp and clean, like a smith's quench-tub.

The horse stopped on the rise and wouldn't go on.

He sat it and looked.

---

The heather was down for a league.

All of it. Flat. Laid outward in one direction from the middle, every stem of it, as rain lays a cornfield, so the whole moor had a grain to it you could see from the rise, a great wheel of dull brown with its spokes all pointing out at him. And in the middle of the wheel, six hollows.

Each the size of a steading. Sunk into the moor where something heavy had lain three days and pressed it down. You could see the shape of each one still, as a ewe's lie shows in long grass in the morning, only these were a field wide.

He left the horse on the rise and walked down.

The first was black. The heather in it and for a hundred paces round had gone black and brittle and broke under his boot like frost-killed bracken, and the peat under it was hard as stone, and cold. Colder than the air. The pale one had lain here. He put his hand flat on the peat and took it away quick.

The next had glass in it.

Where the low one had lain, the heather had gone to glass. Every stem. Standing up out of the burnt ground, green-black and shining, thin as straws and sharp, and the wind going through them made a sound like a handful of knives being sorted. Round the edge of it the moor still smoked a little, here and there, without any flame.

The next was holes. Hundreds of them. Deep, ragged, as if somebody had pulled up a wood by hand and taken it with them, which somebody had. The edges of the holes crumbling into each other, and long white roots of heather hanging into them, and at the bottom of one, water, already.

One was hardly marked at all. Only scorched, in long straight streaks going out from the middle, as if somebody had stood a lamp there that was too bright for the moor.

He walked between them. He didn't know what he'd come for.

The last of the hollows was the deepest and the longest, smoothed out like the bed of a beck in flood, and at the low end of it, where something had dragged as it rose, a whole bank of the moor had been pulled up and turned over, and lay with its black underside to the sky. The bell one. It had come out of the sea. There were barnacles in the peat. White, and small, and dead, on a moor where nobody had ever seen the sea.

He picked one up. He didn't know why. He turned it over and put it in his coat.

The grey one's hollow, the first one's, was in the middle. Shallower than the others. As if it had lain lightest. The heather in it wasn't even broken, only flat, and starting already, here and there, to lift.

---

He went down to the tarn on the way back.

He hadn't meant to. The horse took the bier-road at the turning because it knew it, and he let it, and it brought him down the last of the slope into the bowl as the sun went off the water.

Coldtarn. Black. It was always black. The cliffs went up on three sides with the ledges on them, and the old ones' marks, and on the eastern rim the dark mouth of the caves with the steam coming out of it and the hangings across, and a thread of smoke. The flat black stone ran out into the water like a jetty.

He left the horse at the shore and walked out on it.

To the end. Where the urns went. Where his father had gone into the water, and his brother, and Tor Brecc and Skell and the riders from the stair. He stood at the end and looked down. You couldn't see anything. You never could. The water was so black and so still that he could see himself in it, and the cliff behind, upside down, and the first star.

He'd always lost to Teo, here. At holding his breath. They'd come up in the summers with their father, when Teo was small and Arn was less small, and their father had taught them both to swim off this stone, and after that the game was to go down. Hold the stone's edge under the water and go down along it into the black, as far as you dared, and stay. Teo always stayed longer. Teo came up laughing with the cold, blue at the lips, and said he'd seen the bottom, and Arn said he hadn't, and Teo said *Go and look, then*.

Arn had never gone and looked.

He didn't now. He stood on the stone with his hands at his sides.

---

Behind him, on the shore, something came down out of the air.

Not big. A thump, and a scatter of stones, and the black horse threw its head up and backed on its rope.

He turned.

The young red one from Vael. The girl's. She stood at the root of the stone where it met the shore, with her wings half folded, as if she hadn't decided yet whether she'd landed. She was taller than she'd been in the autumn. Taller than a grey beast. Dark red, so dark on the side away from the sun that it was nearly black, and on the side toward it, where the last of the light was, red-gold. A pale belly. A stub of horn over one eye, broken off short. And round her neck a band where the scales had gone dull, as if they'd been rubbed with sand, that didn't take the light at all.

She looked at him.

He'd seen her eyes in the caves, by the spring, with nothing in them. Dark gold. The binder's emptiness, that Gunna said you couldn't hurry. He'd looked into them and seen the cave wall.

There was somebody in them now.

"Go back," he said.

---

She folded her wings.

She came out along the stone. Carefully. The stone was wet, and she didn't know it, and her claws slid and she put her feet down again further apart, and came on, and stopped a length off. Between him and the shore.

"Go back to the girl," said Arn.

She didn't.

He walked past her. Off the stone, to the shore, to the horse, which was still backing and showing the whites of its eyes. He got hold of its rope. Behind him, claws on wet stone, careful. When he turned she was on the shore too, a length behind, standing.

"Go on," he said. He pointed across the tarn to the caves. "There. Go."

She looked where he pointed. Then back at him.

She went. Up off the shore, short and hard, as young ones go before they've learned that the air will hold them, and across the water low, so low her wingtips touched it and left rings, and up onto the rim by the cave mouth. And stood there. Looking back across the tarn.

He got on the horse.

When he turned it toward the slope she was in the air again, coming back over the water.

---

She landed further off, this time. Ten lengths. She walked behind the horse up the first of the bier-road, and the horse wouldn't walk with her behind it, and went sideways up the road with its quarters tucked under it and its ears flat, and he had to get off and lead it.

"Go back," he said.

He said it four times. Five. She stopped each time he turned round, and looked at him, and didn't go.

Then, from the rim across the water, small and high and very clear in the still air, somebody called.

"Ildra!"

A girl's voice. Thin. Carrying.

"Ildra!"

The young dragon's head came round. She stood on the bier-road with the dusk on her and her head turned back toward the caves, and he saw her decide, if that was what it was. Then she went. Up off the road and back across Coldtarn, low, leaving rings, toward the voice.

He stood on the road with the horse and watched her come down on the rim beside a small figure, and the small figure put up its hand, and the dragon put its head down to the hand.

He got back on the horse. His hands were cold. He'd been holding the rope too hard.

---

On the third night there was a light in the east.

Low, over the Worldsend. A glow, as if a town were burning on the far side of the mountains, except there was no town there and never had been. He saw it from the rampart. Half the hold saw it. Nobody said what it was.

The dragons still weren't humming.

The rider from the coast came up on the third day, late, on a brown dragon so tired it lay down in the yard and wouldn't get up for its mutton. Over the Ashlings and onto the Scarfell from the south. He'd been at Gullhaven. He'd been on the Coast Road. He gave his news in the hall to Ulric, because Ulric was the one who asked, and Arn sat on the bench beside the chair and listened to his uncle ask it.

The spit's people put off the salt-pan farms. A thousand on the road west with nothing. And a paper, torn off a nail on the harbour-master's door, with a blue wave at the head of it.

Ulric read it. Then he folded it and put it in his coat.

"Ten thousand gold," he said. "For three dragons the Council's never seen close." He looked at Arn across the hall. "They'd have had them cheaper off Gorm's books."

---

They went up to the caves at noon on the fourth day.

On two of the Clan's dragons, behind their riders. Arn on his red off the high ledge, Ulric on the grey off the lower cliff, side by side over the moor with the wind in their faces too loud to talk. Which suited them both.

The old one was across the cave mouth. Too big for it. Her head inside, by the hearth. Her crooked wing folded against the rock and the rest of her lying out along the ledge in the cold, and Ivar Leck's moss packed under her plates where the old wound was, dark and wet. She opened one gold eye at them as they came down and shut it again.

Inside, the roof over the hearth was black. Wall to wall. As if somebody had set fire to the whole cave and changed their mind.

Ulric looked at it and grinned.

Arn didn't.

---

They sat at Bel Fenn's table. The long one from the hold kitchen that she'd had carried up in pieces in the autumn, by the warm spring, with the hangings over the mouth and the children's blankets in a hatching bowl and the farmer's mother at the end with her bad hands in her lap.

The farmer had his right hand in a wet cloth. He held it a little away from himself. Nobody said anything about it, and nobody needed to, with the roof.

And on the table, at the farmer's elbow, a stack of grey paper. The Clan's. With a line on the top sheet in a big square careful hand, and another line under it, crossed out, and written again.

Arn didn't look at it. He looked at the table.

Behind him, claws on the cave floor.

He didn't turn round. He knew. She'd done it every time he came up, since the winter: left the girl, wherever the girl was, and walked across the whole cave to stand behind him. She stood behind his bench now with her head over his shoulder, looking at the table, at the bread, at the farmer's wrapped hand, breathing on his ear. Warm. Smelling of the spring and of mutton.

He put his hand up without looking and pushed her nose away.

"Go back to the girl," he said.

She didn't go.

---

"The Clan will fly," said Arn.

He said it to the table. He'd made up his mind on the moor that he'd say it first, before Ulric could, so that it came from the chair's side.

"Where?" said the farmer.

"Wherever it's needed. All of it. Red and grey. The way we came to the river." He looked at his hands. "We've said so at the chair. Both halves. Nobody argued."

"Nobody argued for a whole morning," said Ulric. "First time since Teo."

Arn didn't smile.

He could feel the young one behind him. Not touching. Near enough that he could feel her breath stop and start when Ulric laughed, as if she were listening to that too.

"The riders want something," he said.

And then he couldn't say it. He sat with it in his mouth and couldn't get it out, because it was Bjarke's and he'd brought it anyway, and the farmer was looking at him with his mother's look, patient, as if he'd wait all day.

Ulric said it for him.

---

He watched the farmer's face while Ulric said it. The three. Under the banner. As they were Sigrun's and Dag's and Imra's.

It didn't change. The farmer looked at Ulric and then at the cave mouth and back, and said what Arn had known he'd say since Bjarke stood up.

"They're not mine to give."

Ulric laughed. "I told him you'd say that," he said. To Arn. "Didn't I?" And to the farmer: "Ask them, then."

So the farmer asked them.

Arn heard none of it. Nobody did. He watched a man sit at a table and turn his head toward a red dragon lying on the rock with its chin on its feet, and wait, and get something, and turn his head further, to the old one in the mouth of the cave with her eyes shut. And wait a long time. Long enough that the fire settled in the black hearth and the farmer's mother shifted on her bench.

He'd watched Sigrun do that. Dag. Imra Sallow, laughing at something her green one had said that nobody else would ever hear. He'd grown up with it. Riders at his father's table listening to the air.

The farmer turned back.

"Kharrak says no," he said. "Lisska's away." And then the old one's answer, all of it, in her words as near as he could get them, with the joke on the end: that they could put their thread on Kharrak if they could catch him.

---

Ulric laughed until he had to put his head down on Bel Fenn's table.

He laughed until the children woke up in their hatching bowl and stared at him. Until the farmer's mother said, without moving, "There's nothing wrong with that joke that it needs all that," which set him off again.

Arn looked at the cave mouth.

At the old one, lying across it, with the light behind her. She'd been the Clan's longer than there'd been a Varr in the chair. She'd flown his grandfather's flights. His father's. She'd come down low over the hold in the smoke of his brother's pyre and made a sound he still heard some nights. And she'd made a joke about Kharrak, and sent it to the hold by him, and he'd have to carry it down the hall between the tables to Bjarke.

"I'll tell the riders," he said.

Behind him the young one put her head over his shoulder again. He didn't push it away. He'd forgotten she was there.

---

He didn't tell them.

Not that night. Ulric told the hall the coast news at the meal, and the hall talked about the contract and the Council and what the Council could do with its ten thousand gold, and nobody asked about the three because everyone thought there'd be time.

That was the night of the lights, too. He was on the rampart when the whole sky over the Worldsend went white. Not a glow now. White and gold, pulsing, slow, like a smithy when the bellows go, so bright the crags above Coldtarn threw shadows west across the moor at midnight. And the dragons began again.

All of them, at once. Up on the Roosts and along the lower cliffs and down in the pens, the hum came back up out of them as if it had only been waiting. Low. Long. Into the stone. He felt it come up through the rampart into his boots and stood there with his hand on the wet turf and was so glad of it that he didn't trust himself to go in among people.

He went in in the end. Bjarke was at the red table.

"Did you ask him?"

"I asked."

"And?"

"I'll tell it at the tables," said Arn. "When they're full."

Bjarke looked at the hall, which was full. Then at Arn. He didn't say so.

Arn didn't tell them the next night either, or the one after.

---

Teo would have told it the first night.

Teo would have come in off the dragon with it already half told, laughing in the doorway, and told it from the middle of the hall between the tables with a jug in his hand, standing where nobody else would stand. And done Aurveth's voice. He'd been good at voices. He'd done the old one's slow way of talking so well when he was fifteen that Sigrun had cuffed him for it and then gone off round the corner of the byre where she thought nobody could see and laughed.

He'd have had the red table laughing at itself before they knew they'd been insulted. That was what Teo did. He walked down the middle and took what one side said and turned it round till the other side could carry it, and the hall breathed out, and Teo sat down at the bench by the door with whoever there was, the drovers, the old women, the farmer from the Marches with his grain-dust, and nobody ever thanked him, because nobody ever saw him do it.

Arn had seen.

He'd sat at the left-hand table drunk every night for a month and seen him do it and hated him a little for it. For being able to. For walking down the middle of their father's hall with his hands empty and making it look like nothing.

And now there was nobody in the middle. There was the chair at the top, under its blanket, and the bench beside it, and him on the bench, and between the two tables the long empty floor with the rushes on it, where nobody walked, because nobody knew how.

He had a joke of the old one's in his pocket. He couldn't tell a joke. He never had. He told things as they were and people looked at him.

---

He walked the pens every morning.

Down the stair from the hall to the lower ledges, along the rail, past every pen. His father had. His father's father. A hundred dragons and more behind the oak bars, bred on the ledges, fed on the Clan's mutton, written in Gorm's books. Most of them would never choose. A dragon chose, or it didn't, and most didn't. The Clan kept them anyway.

They hummed at night now. They hummed in the mornings, with their heads up and turned east, toward the light that wasn't there in the day. Leir walked the passage with his barrow and they took the mutton without looking at it.

"They're listening," said Leir.

"To what?"

"I don't know," said Leir. "I've kept them fifty years. They've never listened to anything but the barrow."

---

He went to the chief's stall in the evenings.

He didn't know why. He'd never gone in Teo's lifetime; it had been his father's horse in it then, and he and his father hadn't had much to say to each other over a horse. Now he went in the dusk, after the meal, when Haki had done the bay and gone, and stood in the stall door.

Skerry looked at him over the partition. Then went back to his hay.

The stall smelled of hay and hoof-oil and the bay's warm hide. The good saddle hung on its peg on the wall, Teo's, with the grey cloth under it and the black hound stitched on the cloth, and the hound's thread going pale where the light from the door fell on it every day. Nobody had taken it down. Nobody had used it.

Teo had ridden down to the ford on that saddle in the dark, before light, to be first in the water. He always came early. He'd have been standing in the beck at first light with his hands out.

Arn stood in the door.

The bay pulled his hay. Outside, up on the Roosts, the dragons hummed. In an hour the sky over the Worldsend would go white.

---

On the eighth day at the evening meal he told them.

He hadn't decided to. He'd sat down on the bench beside the chair and looked down the hall at both tables full, and the young ones on the steps at the back because there wasn't room, and the farmer's three were in his pocket like a stone in a boot, and he stood up.

"The farmer says they're not his to give," said Arn.

He said it flat. He heard himself say it flat. As he'd have read out the levy.

"The three," he said. "He says they're not his. He says ask them."

"And?" said Bjarke.

"And the old one says she's tired," said Arn. "She says she was the Clan's for three hundred years and three partners, and she'll fly where the farmer goes, and we can put our thread on the red one if we can catch him."

Nobody on the red side laughed.

He'd known they wouldn't. He'd known it on the moor and at Bel Fenn's table and every night since. He stood at the top of the hall beside his father's chair and watched them not laugh, Bjarke looking at his cup, the others looking at Bjarke, and it was as bad as he'd thought and no worse.

At the end of the grey table, old Hulda Sarr barked. Like a fox in a field at night. She put her hand over her mouth and laughed into it until her eyes ran.

"If we can catch him," she said. "Oh, that's the old one. That's her to the bone."

Ulric was grinning, across the hall.

Arn sat down.

---

After that the days went fast.

Gorm with his ledgers in the morning, three books under his arm and the old one's name in his cramped hand, and the green one's under it, Imra Sallow's, *bonded in her fifteenth year*. Then say it to the green one, Arn had told him, and Gorm had said she'd bite him, and she would. The pens on the ninth day, silent since dawn, the hum gone out of them all at once as a door shuts, and every dragon in every pen standing with its head to the east, and Leir's empty face. The rider from the eastern edge at noon with her dragon blowing on the hall steps behind her. *They've come down. All six. The Plains are burning.*

And the girl in the yard. The farmer's girl, come down from the caves that morning on the old grey beast with the farmer's mother up on its back, to see her father off. And behind them, the young one.

He saw her see him.

He saw her leave the girl and come across the yard, and he went down the steps and along the yard and down the stair to the pens, and she came after him. A length behind. Down the stair, carefully, her claws on the old stone. Along the rail between a hundred silent dragons. He sent her back four times. Every time she went up the stair, and every time she came down it again when he wasn't looking. The fifth time the girl came down the stair and took her by the stub of horn.

"She likes you," said Wren.

"She's not mine."

"That's what I say."

His hands were shaking when they'd gone. He put them on the rail. At the far end of the pens Gunna Tarne sat on the rail with her clay pipe, half her face scar, and said, "Five times," and said not one of these had ever followed him a length, and he hadn't had an answer for that either.

---

Then the farmer in the yard below the hall in the afternoon, talking out loud to his three, and the young riders on the steps laughing at him, and the three heads coming up and round, all at once. Bjarke putting his cup down on the step beside him very carefully, as if it might break.

Arn watched it from the hall door.

He'd have laughed, once. At the riders. He'd have laughed at Bjarke's face. He stood in the doorway and didn't, and thought that Teo would have, and would have gone down and sat on the step beside Bjarke and said something that made him laugh too, after.

He went inside.

---

Then the night.

The farmer at the foot of the hall between the tables, where a petitioner stood, asking for the flights. Gullhaven's people on the road. The contract nailed on every gate. The one with the bell on its back lying in the light in the mountains with its head turned toward its sea.

Arn had known what he'd say since the rider came in from the edge with the smoke. He said it. The Clan's place was the Scarfell. Six of them on the Plains, not a hundred leagues off. The Clan's families on this moor and the caves full of children. *Your own.*

And Ulric, from the grey table, not loudly, looking at his cup.

"Teo would have gone."

---

He did know it.

He'd known it on the moor in the wheel of flattened heather and on the black stone and in the stall door and every night on the bench. He knew it as he knew what was under the blanket without lifting it. Teo would have gone. Teo would have been off the ledge before the farmer finished asking.

And Arn was sitting on a bench beside an empty chair, saying the Clan's place was the Scarfell, because it was. Because somebody had to say it. Because the Clan's families were on this moor and somebody had to stand on the moor with them while Teo went off the ledge with his arm round the farmer's neck.

That was what galled him: that it was true, and the other thing was true too, and there'd only ever been room in the hall for one of them to say his and be loved for it.

"Half," he said.

He heard his own voice say it.

---

He found Ulric in the chief's stall that night.

He hadn't expected to. He went across the yard late, when the hall was emptying and the farmer had gone out to his three, and the stall door was open and there was a lantern on the hook and his uncle was in there with the bay.

Ulric had his hand on Skerry's near fore. Running it down the tendon, slow, with his head bent, as he did with his own mares at Skarrow. The bay stood for it. The bay had never stood for Arn in his life.

"He's fat," said Ulric, without looking up.

"Nobody rides him."

"I can see that." Ulric straightened, with a hand on the bay's shoulder. "He's a good horse. Teo had an eye. I told him so when he bought him, and he said he'd bought him because he liked how he looked at apples." He patted the bay's neck. "Ride him. Or give him to somebody who will. You're making a pet of a dead man's horse."

"Haki rides him."

"Haki walks him on a rope round your yard." Ulric looked at him. "I've seen him. Twice a day, and talking to him."

Arn stood in the door.

---

"You said yes," said Ulric.

"You said Teo."

"I did." Ulric reached up and turned the lantern down a little on its hook. "I'll say it again if it gets the flights to the coast. I'll say his name at every table on the Scarfell. He'd not mind. He'd have said it himself." He looked at the saddle on its peg. At the hound on the cloth. "He'd have said it and then gone and done it, and left you here to hold the moor, and never once thought to ask if you minded."

"I don't mind."

"You mind," said Ulric. "You sat there with a face like a winter dyke. Half the hall saw it."

"I'll hold the Scarfell."

"I know you will. That's why I'm going." Ulric came out of the stall, past him, into the yard. The lantern light behind him. He stopped. "Forty pairs. You said it before I could ask for fifty. That was well done."

He started across the yard toward the east longhouse.

"Uncle."

Ulric stopped again.

"Sit in it," said Arn. "When you're back. Somebody should."

---

Ulric turned round.

He looked at Arn across the dark yard. Then at the hall, with the last of the fire showing at the door.

"No," he said.

"You've wanted it thirty years. Everyone says so."

"Everyone says a lot of things." Ulric folded his arms. "I've been looked at over that chair for thirty years. By your father. By you. By every man on this moor who thought I'd sit in it the minute I could. I'm not going to sit in it now, and have them all say *there, he did*." He looked at the hall door. "I'll sit beside it. As you do. And we'll see who gets tired first."

He didn't move off.

"That red one," he said.

Arn waited.

"The little one. The girl's." Ulric was looking up past him, at the hall roof, at the dark ledges above it. "She's picked."

"She's the girl's."

"The girl says not."

"The girl's ten," said Arn.

Ulric made a sound that might have been a laugh. He unfolded his arms.

"Sigrun was seventeen," he said. "Nobody asked the old one if that was old enough." He went off across the yard into the dark. His voice came back. "Don't stand in that door all night. The bay doesn't want you either."

---

They went at dusk on the tenth day.

All day the yard was harness and baskets and riders shouting. The war-baskets brought up from the byres, the ones they'd carried Gullhaven's people off the roofs in, mended, twenty to a pair. Mutton barrows. Oil. Ivar Leck going up and down the high ledge with his bag, putting his hands on wings. The farmer in the middle of it, in everyone's way, not knowing which end of a basket was which and too stubborn to ask. His old one lying by the gate with her tail out in the lane and the children of the hold climbing on it.

The girl sat on the hall steps all afternoon with the young one lying beside her.

Not looking at Arn. Neither of them. He'd look up from a harness buckle and they'd both be looking at the yard. He'd look down, and he'd feel it.

---

Off the Roosts. Forty pairs, red thread and grey, mixed, as they'd flown at Gullhaven. The farmer's three among them, the old one slow at the back with the red one off her crooked side. Ulric at the front behind Sigge Brack on Sigge's grey, not looking back.

Arn stood on the high ledge and watched them go.

Down off the Scarfell into the valley of the Wend. South. Low over the river. Breathing as they went, one after another, short gold fire going out ahead of them into the dusk to light the way, down the river toward Millbrook and the coast. A line of it. Going small. Going out.

On the ledge beside him the riders who were staying stood and watched it too, and nobody said anything, and behind him somewhere on the stair a child asked where they were going and was told.

He watched until the last of the fire was too small to see.

Then he went down.

---

The hall was empty. The tables cleared. The fire low.

The grey bench across from his was empty. He couldn't remember when he'd last seen it empty. He sat down on his own bench beside the chair and looked at the place where his uncle sat, and the cup ring on the board where he put his cup, and the chair beside him under its blanket.

He didn't know how long he sat. The fire went to embers.

When he looked up, she was in the doorway.

At the far end of the hall. In the porch. Not coming in. Her head through the door and the rest of her out in the dark, and the ember light just reaching her, so that one side of her face was red-gold and the other side was nothing, and the eye on the lit side was looking down the length of the empty hall at him.

He didn't send her away.

He didn't call her either.

He sat on the bench beside his father's chair and looked at her down the hall, and she looked back, and neither of them moved, until the fire was out.

---

In the morning he was ashamed of it.

He couldn't have said of what. He woke on the bench with his neck stiff and the hall grey and cold and the porch empty, and for a moment he thought she'd gone up to the caves in the night and was glad, and then he went out into the yard and she was lying across the foot of the hall steps in the first sun with her chin on the cobbles.

She lifted her head when he came out.

He went past her to the well.

---

The farmer's mother and the girl went up to the caves at mid-morning.

On the old grey beast, as they'd come. The old woman up on its back with a basket on her knees and her stick across it, and the girl walking at its head, and the beast walking as it walked everywhere, slow and sure, with its big head low beside the girl's shoulder. They'd stayed two nights in a room off the hold kitchen. They'd said their goodbyes to the farmer on the high ledge at dusk, and the girl had made him say everything twice, Arn had heard her from the stair.

He walked them to the gate. It was the chief's courtesy. His father had walked every guest to the gate.

The young one got up off the steps and came too.

---

At the gate the farmer's mother looked down at him from the grey beast's back.

"You'll send to us," she said. "When the smoke comes."

"If."

"When," said Ada Fenn. "I'm not a fool and nor are you." She looked past him. At the young one standing in the gateway behind him, a length off, with her head up. "Bel says you're to send her back up if she's a nuisance."

"She's a nuisance."

"Then send her back," said Ada, and nudged the grey beast with her heel, and it went out through the gate and onto the moor road, and the girl went with it.

The young one didn't.

---

He turned round.

"Go on," he said. "Go with them."

She looked at him.

"Go," he said. Louder. He pointed up the road, after the grey beast and the girl and the old woman, going up the long brown slope toward the turning. "Up. Go home. Go on."

She didn't move.

He took a step at her. Then another. He'd driven sheep. He'd driven horses that didn't want to go through a gate. He went at her with his arms out, as you go at a ewe that's broken back, and said *Go on* in the voice for it, and she went back. Out through the gate. Onto the road. He followed her out, step by step, his arms out, until she turned and went up off the road, short and hard, and flew. Up the slope after the grey beast, low over the heather, and came down on the road ahead of them, and stood.

The girl stopped. The grey beast stopped. The old woman said something he couldn't hear.

He stood in the road outside his own gate with his arms out like a fool, and watched.

The young one stood in the road above them. She looked at the girl. Then she looked back down the slope at him.

Then she came back.

---

She came down the slope on foot. Not flying. Walking, as if she had all day, the dull band at her throat not catching the sun and the rest of her catching all of it. Past him. Through the gate. Across the yard. She lay down in the porch of the hall, the whole length of it, with her tail across the threshold, and put her chin on her feet.

Up on the slope the girl was standing beside the grey beast with her hands on her hips.

Arn walked up to her. It was a long walk. He was out of breath at the end of it, and angry, and he didn't know who at.

"Call her," he said.

Wren looked up at him. Her father's chin. Her plait over her shoulder.

"She comes when you talk," said Arn. "You said. Talk to her. Call her."

"No," said Wren.

"Why not?"

The girl thought about it. She frowned at the road, as if the answer were written on it and she was reading it with her finger.

"I'm not talking," she said.

She turned round and took hold of the grey beast's halter, and the old woman on its back looked down at Arn for a long moment and didn't say anything at all, which was worse, and they went on up the road toward the turning, and didn't look back.

---

On the eleventh day the riders on the edge sent word that the smoke in the east was thicker.

He rode out to look at it himself in the afternoon. The eastern watch, where the Scarfell broke off into scrub above the Plains, three of the hold's dragons lying on the rocks there with their riders and their heads to the east. The smoke lay along the whole rim of the world. Black. Low. Not like a burning he knew. A burning he knew went up. This lay along, and thickened, and didn't go anywhere.

Gunna was there on Tamsin. She'd come out with the watch.

"Your girl's in the porch," she said. Without turning round.

"She's not my girl."

Gunna took her pipe out of her mouth and looked at the smoke a while.

"No," she said. "She's not anybody's. I heard you from the Roosts this morning. *Go on. Go on.*" She put the pipe back. "You've a voice for sheep, Arn Varr. I'll give you that."

---

On the twelfth day the smoke was moving.

West. Slowly. Toward the border. The rider who brought it had come in at the gallop and her dragon lay on the hall steps with its sides going in and out.

Arn doubled the watch on the edge.

He sent two riders to the caves to tell Bel Fenn to be ready to move the children. He sent Gorm to count the hold's carts and the hold's ponies and the hold's grain, and Gorm came back by dusk with the count and said it was damp.

The riders came back from the caves after dark.

"Bel Fenn says," said the elder, a grey-thread woman with the wind still in her face, standing at the foot of the hall between the tables where a petitioner stood. "She says she's been ready since the autumn, thank you." She cleared her throat. "And where would you like them moved to."

The hall was quiet.

He sat on the bench beside the chair.

He didn't have an answer.

---

He went out late. Along the rampart, and back. To the pens, which were silent, a hundred dragons standing in the dark with their heads to the east. To the stall, where Haki had left the bay with its blanket on and a lantern turned down to almost nothing, and the bay asleep on his feet, and the good saddle on its peg with the hound on the cloth.

He stood in the stall door. Then he went across the yard to the hall.

She was in the porch.

Lying the whole length of it in the dark, as she'd lain all day and the night before. Her tail across the threshold. Her head on her feet by the outer step. He couldn't see her eyes. He could hear her breathing, slow, and feel the warm come off her into the cold of the porch, like the warm off a byre wall in winter when the beasts are in.

He stood over her a moment.

Then he lifted his foot, and stepped over her tail, carefully, and went into the hall. The fire was low. The two tables ran away from him down the dark to the top of the hall, and at the top of the hall, between them, the chair stood under its blanket, and the bench beside it, empty, waiting for him to come and sit.

Behind him in the porch, she shifted. He heard her lift her head. Then the small sound of it going down again, onto her feet, by the door, where she could see him.
