---

On the Saltreach coast they say a Blackwater man will sell you the rope to hang him and charge you for the knot. They say Blackwater has no chain because it has nothing worth stealing that hasn't been stolen already, and no harbour-master because the last three are still in the inlet. They say a great many things about Blackwater, and most of them are true, and the people of Blackwater say them too, louder, because a bad name keeps the Council's clerks away better than any wall.

And they say the Merrins go fishing for rocks.

They have said it for eighty years. It is a joke on the coast. Every few years the Merrin ketch goes out through the reefs south-west of the inlet, into the Shattered Sea, where there is nothing but broken water and birds, and is gone a month or two, and comes back low in the water with grey stone in her hold. Rocks. The Merrins carry rocks home and break them small and sell them, and the Saffery house on Gallows Quay puts a stamp on them and calls them old charms, and somehow everyone gets paid. The coast laughs about it and buys them anyway.

The Merrins let the coast laugh. A joke is a kind of wall too.

---

Kit Merrin did her sums on a slate.

She'd always done them on a slate. Paper cost money and kept things, and she didn't want her sums kept. She sat at the table in the *Kittiwake*'s stern cabin with the lamp swinging over her, and her father's charts in their rack, and her grandmother's sea-chest under the bunk, and the old tin luck-tag nailed over the door, and she wrote numbers and rubbed them out with the heel of her hand.

The letter lay beside the slate. It had come up the coast from Wendmouth on a salt boat. Ivo Sallow, Saffery's man on Gallows Quay, in his clerk's hand that leaned backward as if it didn't want to be read:

*Everything with a word on it is selling. They say there are beasts waking in the north. Send what you have and more if you have it. I.S.*

She had what she had. She'd been down in the hold that morning. Forty-odd blocks of grey stone stacked on their timber bearers between the stilts, and most of them were ballast now, too big or too plain or too much a part of the ship's trim to break. The rest were chips. A bucket of flakes by the chisel-bench, and the dust of them on the floor that got into your boots and hummed there, very faintly, if you stood still.

A bucket wouldn't pay six crew through a winter.

---

Wages. Stores for fifty days. Salt pork, biscuit, water, the boat's new strakes. The mainsail, which had one more summer in it if nobody looked at it hard. Against that, a hold of stone, broken small, at half a silver a flake to Sallow, which was what Saffery paid for a thing it sold at two.

She wrote it down. She rubbed it out. She wrote it down again with the stores cut, and looked at it.

It came out. Not by much. By a summer's weather and nobody hurt.

"Well?" said Jory.

He was in the doorway. She hadn't heard him come down. Eighteen, thin, brown as a nut, with their father's long hands on the doorframe on either side of him, as though he were holding the cabin up.

"Four years," she said. "Since the last."

"I know."

"It's a long time out there. The reefs move."

"They don't move," said Jory, to the doorframe. "They fall down. I'll see it."

She looked at the slate a while longer. Then she wiped it clean with her sleeve.

"Tell Ferris," she said.

---

Ferris knew already. Ferris always knew. He'd sailed with her father for thirty years and stood in the bows for every one of his crossings, and he had a way of being on deck splicing something when a decision came up the companion ladder, so that it walked straight into him.

"High summer," he said. "Good. The swell's long."

"You'll want to tell Tansy."

"Tansy knows," said Ferris. "She's been salting pork since the letter came."

---

The crew signed new articles on the walkway outside the house, in the sun, with the house's ochre shutters peeling at their backs.

The Merrin articles were short. Wages, shares, the hours of a watch, what happened to a man who stole from the hold, which was nothing pleasant. And at the bottom, in her great-grandfather's words, copied out fresh every time by whoever could write best:

*No hand aboard shall sleep ashore over there, on pain of his wages and his place.*

This year it was Corrie who copied it. That was why Kit had taken her on in the spring: a Blackwater girl, nineteen, quiet, a net-mender's daughter from the stilt-houses at the inlet's head, who could write a fair hand and count without moving her lips. Kit could write. She just didn't like to. Corrie would keep the tally of the stone, every block and every flake, and Kit would check it, and between them nobody would be cheated who didn't deserve it.

Corrie copied the rule out and read it through and didn't ask anything.

Sim did.

---

Sim was sixteen and his brother Nye fifteen, Blackwater boys both, quick aloft and quicker to eat. This was their first crossing. They'd sailed salvage with her in the Shatters for two summers, round the near reefs, but never out past the last tooth of rock.

"Over where?" said Sim.

"Where we're going."

"Where's that?"

"Where the stone is."

Sim signed. He had a big careful signature, much practised. Then he looked at the line above it.

"Why can't we sleep ashore?"

Lorn, who was old and deaf in one ear, didn't hear. Tansy, broad and red-armed, who'd crossed three times with Hal, heard, and found something to do with the pork barrel. Ferris looked at the inlet.

"Because it's in your articles," said Kit.

Sim waited. Kit waited longer. She'd had a great deal more practice.

"Right," said Sim, and grinned, and went off along the walkway to tell Nye something that made Nye hit him.

---

They went out at dawn on the ebb.

The inlet was still. Black water, black cliffs going up into cloud, the smoke from the stilt-houses standing straight up in the windless air. Somebody's dog barked at them from a walkway. On the shingle the sellswords' fires were ash, and the men round them were asleep in their cloaks, and one of them lifted his head as the *Kittiwake* went past under her foresail and put it down again. Merrins fishing for rocks.

Jory took her out through the channel. Kit let him.

---

He didn't look at the water. He never had. He stood at the tiller with his long hands loose on it and looked at the reefs going by on either side, the brown weed lifting and falling on them, the little white breaks where the swell found a rock, and he put the ketch through the bends of the Merrins' channel as if he were walking down a lane he'd been born in. Which he had. Near enough.

Kit stood at the rail with the book under her arm and didn't open it. She could feel her hand wanting to.

At the head of the channel the pier stood up out of the reef.

The Gatepost, her father had called it. The only one so near the coast. A column of grey stone the height of a house, square, faced, standing on the reef's edge where the channel opened into the Shatters, with the sea round its foot and weed to the high-water mark, and on its top a great square block like a lid on a box. The hooked writing on the block's face, worn shallow. Birds on it, white, a great many, lifting off and screaming as the ketch came close.

Ferris had the boathook ready. He gave it to her.

She reached out and touched the pier's foot with it as they went by. Wood on stone. A small knock.

That was all. Her father had done it, and his mother before him, and Abram, who'd found the road. Out and home. You knocked on the Gatepost. Nobody had ever said what would happen if you didn't, and nobody had ever wanted to find out.

The birds settled again behind them.

---

The Shatters took two days.

There's no describing them to anyone who hasn't been in them, and the Merrins never tried. Reef in every direction, as far as you could see from the masthead, brown and black and white, rock teeth standing up out of the swell and rock shelves lying just under it, and channels between that went everywhere and nowhere, and changed with the tide, and killed the people who thought they knew them. A Corsa pilot had once told Kit's father that the Shatters were what the sea would look like if somebody dropped a plate on it. Her father had said that was about right, except you couldn't sweep it up.

Kit read the turns from the book.

Abram's book. A sailcloth binding gone soft as skin, salt in every page. On each page, his drawing of what you'd see: a rock like a hand, a rock with a hole through it, two rocks leaning together like old men at a fire. The bearing. The turn. And in the margins, the corrections. Her grandmother's small fierce letters. Her father's, which slanted. Her own, which didn't.

None of Jory's. Jory didn't need the book.

He was aloft at the crosstrees most of the first day, calling down. "Hand rock, a cable off the bow." Kit looked at the page and found the hand. "Turn to the west of it." She'd find the line in the book that said so. He was always first. He was always right.

She kept reading the book anyway. One day he'd have to sail without her, and one day she might have to sail without him, and it was the second that kept her up at night.

---

At noon on the second day they came to the tooth.

A rock like a broken tooth, with a second rock behind it like its shadow, and a channel between the two no wider than a cart-track, dark and deep, going south-west. Abram had drawn it eighty years ago. It was the last turn in the Shatters. Past it, the reef ended, as if somebody had drawn a line across the sea and told it to stop.

Ferris in the bows called the soundings. Lorn on the mainsheet, Tansy singing under her breath in the galley because she always sang there and never knew she was doing it. Nye and Sim at the shrouds with their mouths open.

The *Kittiwake* went through between the tooth and its shadow with a fathom to spare on either side, and the reef fell away astern, and the water under her went from brown to green to blue to a blue so dark it was nearly black.

Long swell. Deep water. Nothing.

"Where's the bottom?" said Sim.

"Nobody knows," said Kit. "My father sounded for it with every line on the ship knotted together. Didn't find it."

Sim looked over the side for a while. Then he went and stood a little further from the rail.

---

There were reefwyrms that afternoon. Three of them, following the wake, long and grey under the surface with their fins just showing, waiting for scraps. Tansy threw them the pork-rinds over the stern, a little at a time, and called them names. You fed a reefwyrm or it came looking. Kit had seen one put its back under a fishing smack off the Teeth one summer and roll her over, just to see what fell out.

They dropped back at dusk. They always did. They didn't like deep water either.

---

The first pier came on the third day.

Out of open sea. A column standing up alone in the blue, square, grey, broken off at the top in a jagged line twice the height of the *Kittiwake*'s mast, white with birds. The swell lifting round its foot and breaking a little and falling. And on the broken top, upright among the birds, a square stone.

Sim said something Kit didn't hear and Nye laughed at him, not unkindly.

Kit took the glass to it. She knew it. She'd known it since she was ten: the crack down its northern face like a lightning-stroke, the birds always thickest on the western edge. She turned to the page and there it was in Abram's hand, the crack and the birds, and the stone on top drawn exactly, mark by mark. The hooked writing, the same on every milestone on the road. And then a little group of marks after it that was different on every one.

Merrins couldn't read it. Nobody could. Her father had called that last group the stone's number, for want of a better word, and the word had stuck, though none of them knew if it was a number or a name or a curse. You didn't need to read it. You needed to know it when you saw it, and know which page it was on, and what the bearing was to the next.

She gave the glass to Corrie, who'd asked.

Corrie looked a long time. Then she gave the glass back and took her tally-book out of her shirt and drew the milestone's marks on a fresh page, slowly, stroke by stroke, with her tongue between her teeth.

"What's that for?" said Kit.

"I don't know," said Corrie. "I wanted to."

Kit let her. She'd done the same at ten.

---

After that the piers came two in a day, sometimes three.

South-west. Always south-west, over the long blue swell, from one to the next. Some were taller than any tower in Corsa, with the birds going round them in white wheels and their tops lost in the glare. Some were no taller than the ketch's rail, and the swell went over them green, and the milestone stood up out of the white water like a man wading. Each one was broken at a different height. Each one had its stone.

Between them, nothing. Not a gull that didn't belong to a pier. Not a log. Not a sail. Once, at dawn, a whale, a long way off, blowing. Nye and Sim fought over the glass for that.

At night they lay to.

You didn't sail the road at night. Abram hadn't. Her father hadn't. You came up under a pier in the last of the light and put a long line onto its foot, if there was anything to put a line on, or lay off it with the sails backed if there wasn't, and you waited for morning with the swell lifting you up the column's side and dropping you, and the birds settling on the stone above, muttering.

Everyone slept aboard. The rule was for over there, but nobody would have slept on a pier anyway. You couldn't. They were columns in the sea.

---

Sim asked again on the seventh night.

He had the anchor watch with her. The others were below. The pier above them was a tall one, and its top was so high in the dark that the birds' noise came down to them as if from the sky.

"What happens?" he said. "If you sleep ashore."

"You lose your wages and your place."

"Over there, I mean. What happens to you?"

Kit looked at the swell.

"I've never slept ashore over there," she said. "So I couldn't tell you."

Which was true, and was not the answer, and Sim was sixteen and not a fool, and knew it. He sat with his arms round his knees and looked up at the pier.

"My mam says you Merrins have treasure buried over there. And the rocks are only to fool Ostrey."

"Your mam's thought about it more than I have."

"She says that's why you sleep on the boat. So nobody follows you to it in the night."

"Then your mam knows more than I do," said Kit. "Go and fetch the kettle."

He went. He was grinning again. She was glad.

---

On the sixteenth day they came to the ninth.

She knew it from a league off. She always did. It wasn't tall. It wasn't anything. A plain grey pier, broken off low, with a reef round its foot that showed brown at low water and white at high, and the milestone on its top standing a little crooked, leaning, as if something had struck it once and it had never quite got over it.

Jory came down from the crosstrees without being told.

Ferris went to the helm. Kit gave it to him and went below.

---

The dish was in the galley, on the shelf over Tansy's fire. A clay dish with a chip out of its rim, older than the ketch, full of coarse grey sea-salt from the pans at Gullhaven. It was the dish the house swore its contracts on. It was also the dish Tansy salted the pork from, because a dish that sat on a shelf being sacred was a waste of a dish.

Tansy saw her take it and moved her pan off the heat and went up the ladder ahead of her without a word.

---

They were all at the rail when she came up. Lorn with his good ear turned to the pier. Tansy with her red arms folded. Corrie with the tally-book shut in her hand. Nye and Sim, who'd been told by somebody, quiet for once.

Ferris hove her to. Close in. Closer than they lay to any of the others. The swell lifted the *Kittiwake* toward the reef at the pier's foot and let her down again.

Kit took a pinch of salt between her finger and thumb.

She held it over the water. Then she let it go.

It went into the sea at the pier's foot. White, for a moment, in the blue. Then nothing.

Ferris took off his cap.

---

She'd been twenty-eight.

Ten years and more. She could still feel the tiller under her hands that day. Her father had been in the boat.

There was a block at the pier's foot. It had come off the pier's face in some winter none of them had seen, and lay on the reef two fathoms down, square and faced, with the marks on it. Her father had wanted it for three crossings. The best stone on the road, he used to say, was the road. Low water, a flat calm, the boat over the side. He'd gone alone.

"One man in a boat sounds as well as two," he'd said, with his leg over the gunwale, "and drowns cheaper."

He'd laughed. She'd laughed. It was the kind of thing he said.

---

She'd had the *Kittiwake* a cable off, lying to. Ferris in the bows. Jory on the ballast in the hold, eight years old, his chin on the hatch-coaming, watching their father through the gap. Her father in the boat by the pier, leaning over with the lead and a line, feeling down for the block's edge.

The squall came down out of the south-west.

She saw it come. She always told herself she'd seen it come, and that was a lie she let herself have. What she'd seen was the sea go dark a league off, and then half a league, and then the dark was white, a line of white water coming over the swell toward them faster than a horse could run, and the sky over it the colour of a bruise.

She'd shouted. Ferris had shouted.

Her father had looked up. She'd seen his face turn toward them. Then he'd bent to the oars.

---

It hit the boat first.

She saw that. The white came over the reef and over the boat and the boat stood up on its end against the pier, and something went out of it that might have been an oar and might have been him, and the white went over the place where it had been.

Then it hit the *Kittiwake* and she saw nothing for the rest of it but rain and the deck tilting under her and the tiller trying to break her arms.

It went through in the time it takes to say a prayer, if you know one.

The sea behind it was grey and short and full of foam. The boat was upside down on the reef at the pier's foot, stove in along her side. The oars were gone. The line was gone.

He was gone.

---

They looked till dark. Ferris and Lorn in the ketch's other boat, which was a rotten thing, and which they took in so close to the reef that she screamed at them to come off, and they didn't. Kit at the masthead with the glass, going round and round the pier until her eyes ran. Tansy at the rail. Jory on the ballast where she'd put him and told him to stay.

There was nothing. The sea round the ninth was deep on its western side, deeper than any line. Whatever it took, it kept.

They lay to that night under the pier, because you didn't sail the road at night, and she didn't sleep, and in the morning they looked again, and at noon Ferris came and stood by her at the rail and said, "Kit," and nothing else, and she gave the order to turn for home.

---

She'd brought the ketch home herself.

Thirty-odd piers and the Shatters, with the book open on a board across the binnacle and her finger on her father's corrections, and the crew doing what she said without being told twice, which was the only kindness any of them could think of.

And Jory. Eight. He came up from the ballast on the first morning going back and stood at her elbow and looked at the next pier as it came up, and said the page it was on. Before she'd found it. Then the next one. Then the next. All the way home. One by one, by eye, in his small level voice, as if he were reading her a story.

He didn't cry. Not once, not on the whole road. She'd thought that was the worst thing she'd ever seen, a child not crying.

Later she'd thought it was the best pilot she'd ever seen. Both were true. She'd never told him either.

---

She'd gone back for the block.

On the next crossing, six years later, with Jory fourteen and as tall as she was. A flat calm, low water. The two of them in the new boat, and a cable off, the *Kittiwake* lying to with Ferris at the helm and every soul aboard watching the sky in the south-west.

They'd got a line on it in an hour. Brought it up between the boat and the ketch's fall. A square block a yard on a side, faced, with the marks on it.

It lay in the ballast now, by the mast-step. It would never be sold. Nobody had said so. Nobody had to. Corrie had already found it in the tally, and asked about the pencilled line beside it, and been told by Ferris, kindly, to leave it where it was.

---

"On," said Kit.

She took the dish below and put it back on the shelf over the fire. When she came up Ferris had his cap on again and the ketch had the wind, and the ninth was going astern, leaning a little in the late light.

Jory stood at the rail and watched it go. He always did. Then he went back up to the crosstrees.

---

Five days more.

The piers came and went. The swell was long and blue and the wind held fair from the east, and Tansy's pork held out, and nobody was hurt. Corrie had drawn every milestone in her tally-book since the first, until the book was more milestones than tally, and showed them to nobody except Jory, who looked through them on the afterdeck and said, "This one's wrong," and pointed, and she drew it again.

On the twentieth night the light came up in the south-west after dark, low, under the edge of the sea.

Kit saw the boys see it. She saw Corrie see it. She went to the tiller and stood there with her back to it and her eyes on the compass, as her father had at that place and Abram before him, and said to anyone listening, "Anchor watch. Ferris. Lorn. The rest of you, below."

They went below. She heard them talking down there for an hour, low, in the dark.

---

The last pier stood at the edge of a shelf.

She came up on it at noon on the twenty-first. Taller than most, whole or nearly, with its milestone upright under a ring of white birds. And on either side of it, under the clear water, the shelf: old stone lying broken a fathom down, two fathoms, slabs and blocks with long straight edges that no reef ever had, going away to east and west as far as she could see, with the sea breaking white along its outer edge where the swell met it.

Beyond it, a lagoon. Pale green and calm.

Beyond that, four leagues off, low and long and pale, the shore.

She didn't look at the shore. Her father had taught her that. On the last stretch you look at the Needle.

---

The Needle was a gap in the shelf by the last pier, on its western side, where two slabs of the old road had come apart and left a channel between them a ketch's width and a little more. Six fathoms in it at the top of the flood. Less every hour after. Abram had drawn it on his last page but one, the pier and the gap and a line through the gap, and under the line, in his square careful hand: *top of flood only.*

They lay off a cable and waited two hours for the tide.

The boys didn't like it. Kit could see them not liking it, and she didn't blame them. You lay off a cable from a shelf of old stone with the swell going white along its edge and the pier above you full of screaming birds, and you waited, and there was nothing to do but look at the water breaking on the rocks you were going to go through.

Ferris lit his pipe. Lorn slept, sitting up, against the mainmast. Tansy sang.

At the top of the flood Kit took the tiller from Ferris.

She put the *Kittiwake* at the Needle and took her in.

---

It was always quicker than she remembered. The pier on the port hand, close, the birds lifting off it. Old stone either side, pale through the clear water a fathom down, going by. The gap ahead, straight and narrow. Ferris in the bows calling six, six, five and a half. The swell lifting her stern and pushing, gently, like a hand on your back at a door.

Then they were out of it, into the lagoon, and the water went flat all at once, and pale green, with white sand under it a fathom down, and the ketch slid over it as if it were glass.

Sim said, "Oh."

That was all. Just that. Kit thought it was a better thing to say than most.

---

She anchored a league off the shore at dusk. Four fathoms, on white sand. The hook down and the sails furled and the anchor-light up before the sun touched the sea, as her father had always done, and Abram.

Then she called them all on deck.

"Nobody goes ashore tonight," she said. "Nobody sleeps ashore, ever. That's the rule. It's in your articles."

They looked at her. The boys, Corrie. Lorn, who'd heard this time, because she'd said it to his good side.

"Tomorrow we go in by day," she said. "Two at a time. The boat's never left. Back aboard by dusk. Every soul. Every night." She looked at Sim. "If anybody's not in the boat by dusk, the boat comes back without them, and I go in myself in the morning and fetch them, and they'll wish I hadn't."

Nobody laughed. She'd meant somebody to.

"Supper," said Tansy, and they went below.

---

That was the night she sat with her back to the shore.

She took the first anchor watch herself. She always did, over there. She sat on the afterdeck coaming by the anchor-light with her back against the rail and her eyes on the anchor cable where it went down into the pale water, and she watched it, and that was all she did.

Behind her, the hills did what they did after dark.

She knew without turning. The water round the ketch went paler, and the cable showed whiter in it, and her own shadow came and went on the deck in front of her, slow, like a shadow by a fire that's breathing. She watched her shadow. She watched the cable. She didn't turn round.

Her father had told her the rest once. At the tiller, coming home, the year she was nineteen, with his long hands over hers and his eyes on the next pier, so that he didn't have to look at her while he said it. He'd said it once and never again.

She kept it where he'd put it, under the rule, and didn't take it out.

---

Sim came up at the change of the watch. He wasn't due. He stood at the head of the companion ladder with his blanket round him and looked past her, at the shore, and his face was pale in the light off it.

"Kit," he said.

"Back below."

"I only want to see."

"You've seen. Below."

He stood a moment more. Then he went. She heard his bare feet on the ladder, slowly, as if every rung were a thing he had to think about.

She watched the cable.

---

Four days.

By day it was work, and work was a thing she understood. The boat in to the beach at first light, two of the crew and Kit or Ferris at the oars. The boat drawn up above the tide-line and somebody always with it. The stone off the old buildings at the head of the beach, where the sand hadn't got them yet: grey, square-cut, faced on one side, the kind that hummed in your teeth when you put your hand on it, and was worth more than anything else on the coast, and was rocks. Crowbars and rollers and the boat's tackle. Block after block down to the water's edge on rollers, and out to the ketch at the slack, slung under the boat, and up over the side with the fall, and down into the hold. Corrie on the hatch-coaming with the tally-book, writing each one.

Jory went in every day. He liked it over there. He didn't say so. He just worked faster ashore, and talked more, and once she heard him laugh on the beach at something Nye said and stopped with her hands on the crowbar to listen, because she couldn't remember the last time.

Nobody looked inland by day more than they had to. There was the work.

---

On the fourth evening, Ferris came and stood by her at the rail, as he had at the ninth.

"Hal's longest was six," he said.

"I know."

"We've the hold near full."

"I know that too."

He waited. He'd waited for her father thirty years, and for her ten. He was good at it.

"We'll go out on the morning flood," said Kit. "Through the Needle at the top. Tell Jory."

"Jory knows," said Ferris.

---

They came home in twenty-four days, which was good going laden, and nobody was hurt.

At the ninth Kit dropped a pinch of salt in the sea, and Ferris took his cap off, and Jory watched the pier go astern. At the Gatepost she knocked on its foot with the boathook, wood on stone, and the birds went up screaming, and settled. And then there was the inlet, and the black cliffs, and the smoke going straight up out of the stilt-houses, and somebody's dog barking at them from a walkway, and the sellswords on the shingle lifting their heads.

The Merrins had been fishing for rocks.

---

The summer went into the chisel-bench.

That was where a crossing went, after. Under the house, between the stilts, in the hold with the slip for the boat and the water slapping under the planks: the far-stone stacked on its bearers, and the chisel-bench, and the sound of iron on stone from first light to last. You found the grain. You set the chisel. You struck once, clean. A good flake came off thin and grey and true, the size of a thumbnail, and hummed in your palm, and a bad one came off thick and broke in your hand and was nothing.

Jory split better than anyone. Kit split better than Ferris. Corrie couldn't split at all, and wrote everything down, and found three silver's worth of flakes in the tally that had gone missing between the benches and the bucket, and said so, and wouldn't say whose bench it was until Kit asked her straight, and then said it was Nye's, and was right.

Kit stopped it out of Nye's share and told him why. Nye cried. Then he worked harder than anyone for a week.

"You were soft with him," said Ferris.

"I was," said Kit. "He's fifteen."

"Your grandmother would've put him off the walkway."

"My grandmother sold a feather rather than owe a house a copper," said Kit, "and look where it got her," and set her chisel, and struck.

---

She took the flakes to Wendmouth at the end of the summer.

Three oak boxes, iron-bound, packed in sawdust, in the *Kittiwake*'s stern cabin under the bunk where her grandmother's sea-chest had to move over. West along the coast. In through Wendmouth's chain at the river mouth, chain money paid, which Corrie wrote down without being asked.

Wendmouth was wrong.

She knew it from the quay. Wendmouth was always loud, and today it was loud wrong, like a market after a robbery. Men standing in knots. Clerks with Council wax on cords round their necks. And along Gallows Quay, where the Halloran counting-house stood at the end with its red and gold shutters, the shutters were shut and sealed, every one, with a great blob of grey wax on each and a Council stamp in it.

"Fallen," said Ivo Sallow.

He met her at the end of the fish-docks, not on Gallows Quay. A small damp man with a cold that never went away and a Saffery ring on his little finger. He looked at the three boxes and didn't open them.

"At Corsa," he said. "Last week. A barley-farmer with three dragons walked into the Tidehall with the Hallorans' own book under his arm and had it read out. They'd paid for a war, Mistress Merrin. Out of the Council's mint. And now there isn't a Halloran house." He wiped his nose. "Saffery's taking the quay. All of it. Every pen, every cage. Every Halloran man who can't show his papers is going east by tonight."

"And the flakes?"

"Half a silver," said Sallow. "As before." He looked at the boxes again. "Everything with a word on it is selling, Mistress Merrin. Everything. People will pay for anything that says it'll keep them dry and alive. Even yours."

"Especially mine," said Kit. "Mine sometimes work."

Sallow laughed, and it turned into his cough. He counted out the silver on a barrel-head. She counted it again. It was right. It was always right, with Sallow. That was why the Merrins had dealt with Saffery for fifty years.

---

She didn't go along Gallows Quay.

She could have. She had the afternoon. Ferris was seeing to the water, and Tansy to the stores, and Jory was asleep in the stern cabin with his boots on. She stood at the end of the fish-docks where the quay began and looked along it, past the cages and the beast-pens and the sealed red and gold shutters, to the counting-house at the end with its front window.

She hadn't walked along there since she was twelve.

She stood a while. Then she turned and went back to the ketch.

---

Gannet came to Blackwater at the first frost.

She saw him on the shingle. A thick man in a good coat gone shabby, with a sea-bag on his shoulder and a silver ring in each ear, walking along the beach below the stilt-houses as if he'd been born there and was looking for the house he'd been born in. Talking to the sellswords at their fires. Talking to the fishwives. Making them laugh. He had a smile that came up his face before the rest of him had arrived anywhere, and it arrived at everyone.

By noon he was on her walkway.

"Mistress Merrin," he said. "Captain Merrin." The smile. "You won't know me."

She looked at him.

She did, after a moment. It came up slowly from a long way down, from a summer when she'd been twenty and her father had raised a Strand hulk off the Teeth, and taken on two extra hands for it, from the river trade. One of them had been a long thin brown young man with a sharp nose and a laugh you could hear across the reef, who'd been very quick on a tackle and very quick with his hands generally, and whom her father had paid off at the end of the season with his wages in full and a look.

"Gannet," she said.

"There," said Gannet, delighted. "She remembers."

---

"You were long and thin."

"I was," said Gannet sadly. "I was. Wendmouth fed me." He put the sea-bag down on the walkway. "And now Wendmouth doesn't. Saffery's swept the Halloran men off Gallows Quay like rats off a granary floor, and here I am. A poor honest beast-man with no beasts."

"Honest."

"Bill of sale for everything," said Gannet. "Mostly." He looked past her at the *Kittiwake* on her mooring. Broad, ugly, shallow, low in the water still with the last of the summer's stone. "Your father said I was the best man on a tackle he'd ever paid off."

"He paid you off."

"He did," said Gannet, smiling. "With a look. I remember the look." He spread his hands. "I can handle a ship, Captain. I can splice anything. I can lie to an Ostrey man until he thanks me for it. And I'm cheap this winter, because nobody else on this coast will have me."

Kit looked at him for some time.

She trusted him exactly as far as she'd be able to see him, and she was thinking that a ketch was not very big.

"Bosun," she said. "Wages as the articles. You'll read them first."

"I always read everything," said Gannet.

---

He read them on the walkway, standing, while she watched.

He read them fast and well, a reader's reading, his lips not moving. When he got to the bottom he stopped, and read the last line again, and looked up, and the smile came.

"Over there," he said.

"Over there."

"Where's over there?"

"Where the stone is."

"Ah," said Gannet. He signed. A quick hand, with a flourish under it. "Rocks," he said, and laughed, and picked up his sea-bag.

---

He was a good bosun. That was the trouble with Gannet. He was good at everything, a little better than he needed to be, and he was always on deck splicing something when people talked, and always pleasant, and always smiling. The crew liked him by the end of the first week. Tansy liked him by the end of the first day. Corrie didn't like him, and didn't say so, and stopped leaving her tally-book on the hatch-coaming.

Ferris kept his own counsel. He'd been in the bows the summer of the Strand hulk.

---

The dragons came in the dead of winter.

She was on deck at dawn. She was always on deck at dawn. The inlet was grey and still and bitterly cold, the frost white on every walkway, the smoke standing up out of the stilt-houses in grey threads. Out on the north shore the boathouse, the big tarred shed over the water that some gentleman's men had rented at the start of the winter and kept a fire going in day and night, with a sweet smoke coming out of its vents that the whole inlet could smell when the wind was wrong. Nobody had asked what they kept in it. You didn't. Not in Blackwater.

Then the sky over the cliffs was full of dragons.

---

They came over the cliffs from the landward side, low, out of the grey. She counted nine. Two big ones. One small and green and quick. A long grey-green one, old, slow, that came down on the water at the boathouse's sea-door and simply sat on it, like a hen on a nest, with the black water slopping round her. And all of them, every one, with something tied over their noses. Rags. Wet cloths, strapped on behind the jaw. The riders on their backs had their faces covered too.

The men on the boathouse roof lit everything they had.

She could smell it at the inlet's eastern end, half a league off. Sweet. Burnt sweet, like a hive on fire. Thick grey smoke rolling off the shed's roof and out over the black water toward the town. The men on the roof throwing burning things up into the wind on poles. Gannet came up the companion ladder behind her in his shirt and stood at the rail and said, very quietly, for him, "Oh, now."

Then the biggest of the dragons, a scarred rust-red one, came down on the boathouse ridge with all four feet, and the roof went in.

---

She'd remember the sound. Tarred planks breaking, all at once, a great splintering crash that went round the cliffs and came back. And then the shed open to the sky like a box with its lid off, and the smoke pouring up out of it, and the red dragon standing on the broken ridge with its wings half open and something in its posture she could only call joy.

The small green one hung over the hole in the roof with her cloth off. Kit could see her mouth open. She couldn't hear what came out of it. A hum, maybe. She felt it more than heard it, faint, in the deck under her feet.

A man went into the water off the shed's sea-door. The grey-green dragon's tail had done it, lazily. Others on the shore threw down whatever they'd been holding.

Then it was over. It had been very short.

---

"Who is it?" said Corrie. She'd come up. They'd all come up. The whole inlet was out on its walkways in the frost, every stilt-house, black shapes in black doorways over black water.

"The farmer," said Gannet. He was still very quiet. "The Marches one. That brought down the Hallorans." He watched the red dragon on the ridge. "Those'll be the Clan's young ones in there. The ones that went missing." He smiled, then, slowly. "Some gentleman's going to be very disappointed."

Kit looked at him.

"You knew what was in there," she said.

"Everybody knew what was in there," said Gannet. "Nobody asked. It's Blackwater."

---

At dusk they burned something on the beach below the boathouse.

A big shape on the shingle. Bronze, Kit thought, by the last of the light on it. A young dragon, dead. They'd built a pyre round it of the boathouse's own broken planks, and they lit it, and it burned all evening with the smoke going straight up into the cold, and the other dragons lying round it on the shingle with their heads up, looking at the fire.

Blackwater watched from its stilt-houses. Nobody came near.

The dragons stayed three days.

---

On the third night she saw the farmer.

She'd gone ashore for salt pork. The beach was all theirs now, the sellswords' fires moved down the shingle to give the dragons room. And at the nearest fire a big quiet man was sitting with a board on his knees, writing.

Slowly. With his whole arm. A stub of charcoal in his fist, and a sheet of grey paper on the board, and the green dragon lying round behind him with her chin on the shingle near his elbow, watching him write as if it were the most interesting thing she'd ever seen.

He wrote a line. He stopped and looked at it. He wrote another.

He wrote, Kit thought, like a man carrying a full sack up a ladder: every rung on purpose.

She bought her pork and went back to the boat.

In the morning, at first light, the green dragon went up off the shingle alone, small and quick, and turned west along the coast with something tied to her foreleg, and was gone.

The rest went a day later, some north and some west along the coast, and two young ones among them that hadn't been there when they came. The boathouse stood open to the sky. Nobody put the roof back.

Kit thought it was the most honest building in the inlet.

---

The end of the winter came on the sea.

She was in the hold, at noon, at the chisel-bench. It was a grey still day, and the inlet was quiet, and she had a block of far-stone on the bench with her chisel set on its grain.

Then the water under the planks went out.

All at once, and nowhere near the tide. She heard it go: a long sucking under the floor, and the slip for the boat going dry, and the stilts groaning as the weight came off them, and from the inlet outside a sound she had no name for, a whole town of people shouting at once.

She went up the ladder. The inlet was half empty. Ships lay over on their sides on black mud where an hour ago there'd been three fathoms. The *Kittiwake* sat on the bottom on her flat keel, upright, built for it, with Ferris standing on her deck looking down at the mud round her as if it had insulted him.

Then it came back.

---

Not a wave. A rising. The water came in through the inlet's mouth, smooth and fast and grey, and up the mud and round the ships and up the stilts, a fathom above the high-water mark, two, with a hiss like a fire being put out. The stilt-houses shook. Ladders floated off. The ships came upright on it, swinging, snapping their cables. Somebody's whole walkway went.

Then it stopped. And slowly went down again to where it belonged.

Behind her in the hold, the stone was making a sound. Or Jory said it was. He came up the ladder after her white-faced and said the far-stone was louder. She went down and put her hand flat on the nearest block and couldn't tell. It hummed. It always hummed.

---

The news came in four days.

Gullhaven. The sea had gone out of Gullhaven's bay at noon, all the way out, past the reef, and come back with something in it. A Titan the size of an island, rising off the reef with a drowned temple on its back. The spit was gone. The town was gone. Two hundred and ninety names in the harbour-master's book and the Clan's dragons carrying people off the roofs till dark.

"The Bell," said Gannet, at the fire. "They're calling it the Bell. It's got a bell on it."

Nobody laughed.

Kit thought of the Shatters. Of the reefs, and the channels, and all that old stone standing up out of the sea between Gullhaven and the road. She thought about it all night.

---

At the next spring low water she took the boat out with Jory to the head of the channel.

He rowed. She looked.

At the channel's throat the Gatepost stood up out of the reef, as it always had. Grey. Square. Weed to the high-water mark. Birds.

Its top was gone.

Jory shipped his oars and let them drift. They looked down.

It was there. Under them. A yard under, at that tide. The great square block that had sat on the Gatepost's top like a lid on a box since before anybody living, lying on its side across the deep water at the channel's narrowest place, from one reef to the other, with the hooked writing on its upper face, under the clear water.

The size of a chapel.

---

"That's it, then," said Jory.

"No," said Kit.

But it was. She knew it in the boat and spent a month not knowing it. She went to every man on the coast who might know a way. A Strand captain who'd salvaged a whole ship off the Saltings with a sheer-hulk and capstans: he came out with her at low water, and looked, and laughed. A Kessmoor engineer passing through on a coaster, who asked how much it weighed and then what the Merrins could pay, and when she told him he looked at her with real pity. A house drawer, at Corsa, who would have done it for gold she didn't have, on land, and who told her very kindly that on water he was a man with his hands in his pockets.

And all the while, the eastern channels. Jory went out and sounded every one. The Bell had shaken the Shatters like a man shaking a rug. Half the channels were shut by new rock. The fourth was open, at two fathoms on the bar.

The *Kittiwake* drew two and a half with ballast. Without ballast she'd go over in the first swell past the Shatters.

---

There was no salvage that spring. There was no going out at all.

And there were six crew and a bosun to feed, and a ketch that needed tar, and a mainsail that had had its one more summer and been looked at hard. And Sallow's silver, which had gone where a crossing's silver always went: the last year's debts and the boat's new strakes and everybody's wages, until it was gone.

She did her sums on the slate. She rubbed them out with the heel of her hand. She did them again.

They didn't come out. Whichever way she wrote them.

---

Ostrey's factor came along the walkway on a wet morning at the end of the winter.

Master Orme Fosse. A soft, pleasant, round man in a grey coat with Ostrey's grey cuff, and round spectacles that misted in the rain, and a gull-hawk on a perch on his roof that brought him the price of everything in the League every noon. Ostrey insured half the cargoes that went out of Blackwater and didn't ask what they were. It was the only house in the League that kept a factor here. It was the only house that thought Blackwater was worth it.

She met him on the walkway. She didn't ask him in.

"Mistress Merrin." He looked at the rain. "I hear your channel's shut."

"Who told you?"

"Everyone," said Fosse, pleasantly. "It's Blackwater."

---

He'd brought the paper with him. Of course he had. He'd heard about the channel the day she found it and drawn the paper up that night.

*The House of Ostrey to the House of Merrin. The sum lent against the ketch* Kittiwake *falls due on the hundredth day. In default, the ketch and her gear pass to the House of Ostrey.*

She read it to the end, and the sum written in under it, which was enough.

"It's a fair paper," said Fosse. "The house would like you to know it's a fair paper. It regrets the necessity." He smiled. "It regrets very little else."

The rain came down on the walkway between them. Below, under the house, the water slapped against the stilts and the slip and the far-stone on its bearers.

---

Her grandmother had sold a feather rather than sign a paper like this.

Kit had held it once, the night her grandmother brought it down to the beach. In both hands, by the fire. Warm all through, like a hearthstone the morning after. Her grandmother had sold it to the Hallorans a week later, and built the keel of the *Kittiwake* with what it fetched, and never owed a house in the League a copper as long as she lived. She'd said a Merrin who borrowed from a house might as well put her own head on its counter.

Kit had no feather.

She had a ketch, and a channel with a chapel lying across it, and six crew and a bosun. And a book on a shelf in the stern cabin that nobody in the League but her family had ever opened.

"Pen," she said.

Fosse had one. He'd brought ink too, in a little horn bottle with a stopper, warm from his pocket so it wouldn't thicken in the cold. He held the paper flat for her on the walkway rail with his soft hand, and she wrote her name at the bottom of it in her own hand. *Kit Merrin, for the House.*

It looked very small.

---

Fosse blew on it. He folded it and put it inside his coat.

"And the house would still like to know," he said, as he did, as every Ostrey man had asked every Merrin since there had been an Ostrey man in Blackwater to ask, "what it is your family goes so far to fetch."

Kit looked at him through the rain.

"Rocks," she said.

"Rocks," said Fosse. "Of course."

He went back along the walkway with his paper. She watched him all the way to his own door. Then she went down the ladder to the hold, and sat on a block of far-stone in the dark among the flake-dust, with her hands between her knees, and listened to the water under the floor, and to the stone humming, long after there was any reason to.

---

Spring came. The rain stopped. Ships went the long way round the Shatters, or didn't go.

And one night, in the first warm days, Corrie came down the walkway from the inlet's head and called across the water to the ketch, and Kit came up on deck in her shirt and looked where Corrie was pointing.

North. Over the black cliffs behind the town, over the long southern tail of the mountains that ran down to the sea, where no dawn had ever come from.

Light.

---

It burned along the mountains' edge like a second dawn in the wrong place. Pale at first, then brighter, gold and white, flaring and sinking and flaring again, so that the cliffs above the inlet stood out black against it and the black water under them showed every ripple. It went on all night. The whole inlet came out on its walkways and watched.

Nobody knew what it was. Everyone had an answer. The Corsa smugglers said it was the Titans, all six of them, the one that had flown from the Marches and the five that had woken after it, gone up into the Worldsend together to do whatever a Titan did. The fishwives said it was the end of the world. Gannet said it was the price of heartstone going up.

Jory stood beside her at the rail. He watched the light on the mountains. Then he turned and looked the other way, out over the inlet's mouth, south-west, at the dark sea and the Shatters, where the road went.

She knew what he was thinking. She'd been thinking it herself.

"With what?" she said. "Through what?"

He didn't answer. He looked at the sea.

---

She went out to the stone at the bottom of the spring tide on the sixtieth day, and it was still there, and she'd known it would be.

Ostrey came on the sixty-second, with the paper.

And on the sixty-sixth, at the sellswords' fire, with salt pork in her lap, she heard a Corsa smuggler say that the lion's knight had stood up in Kessmoor before every crown in the world and asked for a ship to sail west past the Shatters, and been laughed at. And she sent Jory to find out where Ironhythe was, in days, by lion.

After that she did her sums every night on the slate. They came out now, whichever way she wrote them. She looked at them a long while each night before she wiped the slate with her sleeve.

---

The seventy-sixth day came in grey and stayed grey.

It was a nothing day. A day for tar and the chisel-bench. Gannet went ashore at noon and came back at dusk with a cask of water and no news, which wasn't like him. Ferris mended the mainsail where it couldn't be mended. Tansy sang. Lorn slept. Nye and Sim fished off the stern for whatever would bite, which was nothing.

Kit sat in the stern cabin with the book open on the table under the lamp, at the last page but one. The pier. The gap. The line through the gap, and *top of flood only*.

Twenty-four days to the hundredth.

At dusk she heard the shout.

---

Far off. From the head of the inlet, up toward the cliffs, where the path came down. A boy's voice, high, going up at the end. Then another voice, a man's, nearer. Then the shouting going round the inlet as news goes round Blackwater, house to house, faster than anyone could walk it.

Then the ladders.

She went up on deck. All round the inlet's head, in the dusk, the stilt-houses were pulling up their ladders. One and then the next and then the next, a clatter going away round the water like a stick dragged along a fence. Out in the deep water a smuggler's ship was slipping her cable. Then another. Men running on decks. Sails half up.

Ferris came and stood by her. Gannet behind him, his face lit by somebody's lantern, smiling at nothing.

On the shingle under the cliff path, the sellswords were leaving their fires. Walking backwards. Fast.

"Do we slip?" said Ferris.

"No," said Kit.

---

Corrie came out in the boat.

She came hard across the black water from the inlet's head, alone, pulling as if she'd been born to it, which she had, and brought the boat in under the *Kittiwake*'s side so fast that Nye had to fend her off with his foot. She looked up. Her face was white in the dusk and her eyes were very bright.

"It's a lion," she said.

"I can see it's a lion."

Kit could. There on the shingle among the abandoned fires, very large, getting carefully down. As big as a house, the coast said. The coast for once hadn't lied enough.

"Dabb was on the path," said Corrie. "Our Dabb. My cousin. Bringing the goats down. And it came over the top. With a woman on it, and an old man behind her, tied on." She took a breath. "And the woman asked him."

"Asked him what?"

"Where a body finds a reef-pilot," said Corrie. "In Blackwater. She said that. Dabb said she was very polite. He ran all the way down."

Behind Kit, Gannet laughed, very softly.

Kit stood at the rail of her ketch and looked across the black water at the lion lying down among the fires, and the small shape of the woman beside it, standing very straight, helping an old man down. At the far end of the inlet a last ladder went up, late, with a clatter.

Kit went below for the slate.
