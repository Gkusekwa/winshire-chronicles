In Vael they say the Old Reader came with the books.

They say that on the morning the first librarians carried the first cart of books up from the lake, they found it already sitting on the black oak at the hill end of the hall, as if it had been waiting, and that it told them where to put the shelves. They say that in all the years since it has never once been wrong about where a book is: take one down from the wrong stack at the lake end, and it will know by evening, half a league off, and tell you so in the morning. They say it is a raven. They say it is an owl that has forgotten how. In the Scribes' College they tell the first-years, on their first night, that it was once a man, a Keeper of the Script who read so long that his hands went to claws and his gown to feathers, and the first-years laugh, and look at their hands afterwards. In the lower city, where hardly anyone has seen it, they say it knows the name of everyone who will ever die in Vael, and keeps them in a book under its wing.

Vael is a city of readers, and a reader will believe anything with a source. The bird had heard all of it. It kept a note.

*Footnote: it had not come with the books. It remembered the carts, and the shelves going up bay by bay, and a time when the hall was a third full and the far stacks were bare oak and pigeons. Before that it remembered wind, and something white, and cold. It had had no words then. A thing without words could not be filed, and a thing that could not be filed was, by its own rule, not a memory. It was weather.*

*Correction, to the Scribes' College: it had never been a man. It had listened to men for a long time, which was not the same, though it had sometimes thought the difference smaller than either side would like.*

*Correction, to the lower city: no book. Nothing under its wing at night but its head.*

On the last night of its life it kept its head under its wing for an hour, and then took it out, because the hall was too loud to sleep in.

Every lamp in the Long Library was burning white.

They had burned white all summer, from the door to the hill end, with the hill full under them and humming. Then at noon four days ago they had gone out all at once, every one, in a single breath, and the hum with them. And that night they had come back. Whiter than before. Hard and cold and wrong, half a league of them in their iron cages down the length of the hall, so bright the shadows of the shelves lay on the floor like ink spilled from a ruler.

The hum had come back too. It wasn't the hum of the summer, the long even note in the stone that the bird had felt through its feet on the oak for fifty days and grown almost fond of. This one came and went. It came up through the floor in knocks. Like a junior trying to keep time with his pen on the desk, badly, while he thought about something else.

And there was frost.

It had come in on the first night of the flood and stayed. A fine white fur along the bottoms of the shelves, on the spines of the books in the lowest rows, in the joints of the old stone floor, on the long tables where the readers had left their cups. Frost on the reading-desk. Frost in the dish. Frost on the oak of the perch round the bird's feet, in a ring, very fine, that grew each night a little nearer its claws.

On its own feathers, nothing. Frost formed on them and went, as a breath goes off cold glass. It had watched this for four nights with its good eye, turning a wing to the lamp.

*Footnote: it had no explanation and declined to invent one. Everything in the hall was full tonight. The lamps, the stone, the frost, the ink in the open inkwells, which had stood up in the wells like small black hills. Perhaps it was full too. It had been, in its time, called a great many things by readers who had never cited anything, and full would not be the worst.*

The boy came at the second bell.

It heard him at the lake end, half a league off: the small side door, the latch, the boy's breath going in quick at the cold. Then his feet. Fast, and then slower as the hum got into them, and then fast again, as though he'd remembered he was frightened and decided to have it over with. A light step. Soft soles. A boy who'd been told, in his first week, that nobody ran in the Library, and had believed it, and was running now.

*Merriman. Junior under-clerk. Fifteen. Reads well and spells as if paid by the letter. Cited Tervel's* Lesser Tides *for Tervel's* Greater *in the spring, twice, and was told, and has not done it since. Brings scraps.*

He came out from the last of the shelves with a candle he didn't need, in all that white, and his grey gown buttoned to the neck, and something in a cloth. He stopped at the edge of the frost round the desk as if it were a line he might be fined for crossing.

"Old Reader," he said.

"Under-clerk."

"I've brought..." He unwrapped the cloth. Half a perch, frozen stiff, with the eye gone white. "It's what they had. On the Row. The ferry-folk. They said for the bird." He put it in the dish, on the frost. "I'm sorry it's not more."

"It's a perch," said the bird. "It's precisely as much as a perch."

He looked at it. He wasn't sure if that was a joke. The bird let him not be sure. Juniors needed something to puzzle over going home; it kept their minds off the dark.

He didn't go. He stood at the desk with his candle and looked up at the bird and then down the hall behind him, the half-league of white light and empty tables and frost, and the bird watched him decide to ask something.

"Is it true?" he said. "What Reader Vael says. Five days."

"Reader Vael is generally right," said the bird. "It's his least attractive quality."

The boy laughed. Short, surprised, too loud; it went off down the hall between the shelves, and he put his hand over his mouth.

"Where's your mother?" said the bird.

"On the Row. On the steps. Since the hill. With the ferry-folk." He took his hand away. "Reader Vael's sister said to stay above Candle Row. Five days. She says it every morning, to everybody, as if we'd forget."

"People forget," said the bird. "Tomorrow is the fifth. Stay with her."

"But who'll..." He looked at the dish.

"I am older than your college, under-clerk, and I've eaten on my own before." That was true in more ways than the boy knew. "Stay on the steps tomorrow with your mother. That's not a request. I'm told I make very few, and I'm told they're noticed."

He stood a moment more. Then he said, "Yes, Old Reader," and went.

It listened to him the whole way. Half a league. Fast, the whole of it, now he had leave. The side door. The latch.

Then it leaned down from the oak, slowly, with its wings half open for the balance, and took the perch out of the dish.

*Footnote: nobody had ever seen it eat. Readers had remarked on this for three centuries, and some had written it down, and two had written monographs, neither of which it thought much of. The truth was simpler. For most of three hundred years it had eaten the Library's mice. At night, along the bottom shelves, where the mice ate the bindings. It had not thought a keeper of books should be seen eating anything that also ate books. It would have given the wrong idea. Or, worse, the right one.*

*The mice had gone in the first week of the summer's hum. In three hundred years it had not known the Library's mice to leave the Library for anything. It had made a note. It had not liked the note.*

The perch was cold enough to hurt the beak. It ate it anyway, in the white light, alone, with the frost growing in its ring round its feet and not touching them.

The first word it understood was *where*.

It must have heard others first. It must have heard all of them, a thousand times, in the years before they meant anything: the readers talking low at the long tables, and the librarians calling down the stacks, and the boys at the lake end being told off. All of that it had, now, if it went looking. But it had them as a reader keeps the pattern of a binding after he's forgotten the book. It couldn't say what had been said. Before *where*, the readers had been weather too.

And then a reader at the hill end had stood at the foot of the oak, where the desk was even then, and said to a librarian, "*Where*, then? Where in all this?" and flung out his hand at the stacks. And the librarian had gone along the shelves and come back with a book. And the bird, on its perch, had understood that the sound went with the hand and the hand went with the shelves and the book had come back.

*Where.* It was a sound that made things come back.

It listened for it after that. It heard it a hundred times a day. *Where's the Tervel? Where did you put the Sarovel rolls? Where was I?* And it watched where the librarians went, every time, and where the books came from, and after a time, a long time, it knew where everything in the Long Library was. It knew where things were before it knew what anything was called. It knew the Kessmoor books by their smell of iron filings and the Sarovel ones by the oil they put on their bindings, and the Saltreach ones by salt, and the old-realm rolls by nothing at all, a smell like the inside of a stone.

The first word it ever said aloud was *there*.

It was to a junior. A girl, in the old grey gown they wore then, with the high collar, who'd been sent up from the lake end for a book and had been along every shelf of the hill bays twice and was crying, quietly, because her Master would beat her. She was standing under the oak with her face in her hands. And the bird, which had been watching her for an hour, and which knew exactly where the book was, opened its beak and said the sound.

It came out wrong. Too high. Like a hinge.

The girl looked up.

The bird turned its head and looked, with both its eyes, which were both good then, at the third shelf of the second bay. And said it again. *There.*

She went and looked. She came back with the book held against her chest and stood under the perch and stared at it with her mouth open.

*Footnote: she was called Annet Mayle, and she grew up to be the first woman to hold a Fourth ring in the Scribes' College, and she wrote, in her old age, a short history of the Long Library in which the bird appears on the ninth page as "the keeper, who first spoke to me." It is the earliest citation of the bird in any book. It is inaccurate in two particulars. The bird had never corrected it. It was the only error it had ever let stand.*

After that, they asked it.

Not at once. Readers are slow to ask a bird anything. But in a year or two the juniors were asking it where things were, and in ten years the readers, and in fifty the librarians had stopped keeping the finding-lists up to date, because there was no need. You went up to the hill end and you said *where* and the bird said *there*.

It learned the rest of the words the same way. Slowly. From the readers' mouths. They muttered as they read: the title, the page, the line, *cf.*, *see also*, *ibid.*, *but this is wrong*, *but Tervel says*. It learned language from the margins in. It learned that a sentence had a source and that you said where it came from, and that if you couldn't say where it came from you said that too, and that the most important thing a reader could say was *this is wrong*, and the second most important was *see*.

*Footnote: this is why it thought as it did. A reader who has learned his words in a nursery thinks in a nursery's way all his life, in stories with a beginning and an end. It had learned its words at the foot of a desk. It thought in notes. It could not help it, and by the second century it had stopped wishing to.*

*Footnote to the footnote: in three hundred years it was asked where something was some hundreds of thousands of times. It kept a tally for a while and then stopped, tallies being a vice of the College of Tides. It was asked what something meant perhaps a few thousand. It was asked why, by readers who wanted the answer, as opposed to readers who wanted to be seen to ask, eleven times.*

*Seven of those were the ferryman.*

The case came in a winter, three hundred and four years ago, at night.

It knew the night exactly. It had had words for a long time by then, more than it ever used. It understood most of what was said at the desk and said very little back. It couldn't read. It knew where every book in the hall was and what none of them said.

There was snow on the window behind the perch. The lamps were few then, and low. A man came up the hall in the dark without a lamp at all.

A young man. Tall, long-faced, in a wet coat over a College of Tides gown, with the iron ring on his right hand notched three times, and under his coat, held against his ribs with both arms, something long. He smelled of the lake and of the College's ink, which then was brown, and of a long road, and very strongly of fear. The bird knew him. He came up to the hill end every week, in daylight, with a Master's errands.

*Hew Corlis. Reader, Third ring, College of Tides. A good copying hand; the best in that college then. Had copied, the year before last, a long table of figures for his Master, very fast, at this desk, with his back to the hall, and taken it away again before the ink was dry. Never asked it where anything was. Always knew.*

He didn't look at the bird. He went round behind the perch, to the foot of the oak where it went into the floor, and knelt there in the dark and put his fingers on the wood.

The bird turned on its perch to watch him.

He knew where to put his fingers. That was the thing it noticed, then and afterwards. He felt along the carved panel at the back of the oak's foot, a panel of hooked marks so worn they were only shadows, and found a crack at its edge, and pulled, and the panel came open. And behind it there was a hollow in the foot of the perch, cut into the oak, the size of a book.

The bird had sat on that oak longer than the young man had been alive. It had not known there was anything in it.

*Footnote: sitting on a thing is not knowing it. It had said so once, recently, to the ferryman, and been rather pleased with it, and had not told him where it got it.*

Corlis took the long thing out from under his coat. A case of dark wood, round, bound at the ends with silver, new silver then, bright. He put it in the hollow. He shut the panel on it and leaned his forehead against the oak for a moment, breathing, with his eyes shut.

Then he stood up and looked at the bird.

"Keep it," he said.

The bird knew *keep*. Readers said it all the time. *Keep my place. Keep it for me. Keep it dry.*

"Nobody's to have it," said Hew Corlis. He was speaking very low, and fast, as people do in a hall at night, and the bird had to listen hard. "Do you hear? Not the College. Not the Master. Not anyone. Don't let them..." He stopped. He looked down the hall into the dark, toward the lake end and the door. "Don't show it to anyone. Not anyone." He put his hand flat on the oak, by the panel, as if he were swearing on it. "Unless..."

The bird waited.

He didn't finish. He stood with his hand on the oak and his mouth a little open and the rest of the sentence somewhere behind his teeth, and then he shut his mouth, and took his hand away, and went back down the hall in the dark the way he'd come.

He didn't come up the hall again. Not that winter. Not ever.

*Footnote: it found him eighty years later, by accident, looking for something else, in a roll of the College of Tides' readers for that decade. His name had a line drawn through it in a different ink, and in the margin, in a Master's hand, one word: removed. It did not know removed to where. It never found him in any other book, and in a library of that size that was its own kind of answer.*

*Footnote: unless. It had kept that too. Three hundred and four years. A sentence without an end. Of all the things it kept it thought, some nights, that one had been the heaviest, and it was only a word.*

It kept it.

It didn't know what it was keeping. It sat on its perch over the hollow in the oak and answered *where* and *there* and learned words and listened, and the case lay under it in the dark of the oak's foot, and nobody asked.

Nobody knew to. It thought later that that had been the point, and that Hew Corlis had chosen well. Nobody looks under a bird.

It learned to read in the next century.

Not from a book. From readers' fingers. It watched them go along the lines at the desk, under its perch, thousands of fingers over hundreds of years, and their lips moving with the fingers, and it put the shapes and the sounds together as it had put *where* and the shelves together, slowly, as a thing is learned when nobody is teaching it. The common hand first. Then the College hands, which were the common hand with airs. Then, much later, the hooked script.

That it learned from the Keepers. The Sixth rings. There were never more than two or three alive at once, and when the oldest stones came up to the Library, rubbings and copies and once a whole milestone on a cart, it was the Keepers who came up the hall to read them. They read at the desk at the hill end because the light was best. They read aloud, under their breath, in the old tongue, very slowly, because even for a Keeper the hooked script was slow. And the bird sat over them and watched the old fingers go along the hooks and listened, and it was patient, and it had more time than they did.

*Footnote: a Keeper of the Script, in that age, took forty years to make. The bird took about ninety. It did not think this reflected badly on either.*

It read the case's roll for the first time about two centuries ago.

At night. With the hall empty and the lamps low. It got down off the oak, which it was not in the habit of doing, and went round to the back of the foot, and put its beak in the crack at the edge of the panel as it had watched Hew Corlis put his fingers. The panel came open. It drew the case out by the silver at one end with its beak, a hand's breadth at a time, onto the stone, and the silver was black already. It took the vellum out and got it up onto the desk, which took most of the night, and unrolled it with its beak and its feet and weighted it with its own dish.

It read the table first, because numbers were easier.

It took eleven nights.

On the first night it understood that it was a contract, and that the College of Tides was one party to it, by its Master, a Keeper of the Script called Aldwyn Sarre, whose hand it knew from the College's old letters. On the third night it understood that the other party was a lord in the country by the Wend. On the fifth that there was something under that lord's hill, and that the College had measured it, and that the table it had read first was the measure. On the seventh, the chains. On the ninth, the charm for many voices, and its two lines.

*Keep the watch below.*

*And do not ask.*

It knew those words. The Library had the chronicles of every kingdom on its shelves, Hearthmere's among them, all of them written by the crown and dull, and it knew that every knight in Hearthmere stood at the noon bell and said those words facing the inner wall of his house, and had for a hundred years. It had read it in four books. It had thought it a pretty piece of piety, of the kind kingdoms went in for.

On the eleventh night it rolled the vellum up again and put it back in the case and the case back in the oak and shut the panel and went back up onto its perch, and sat there till morning, when a junior came up from the lake end and asked it where the Sarovel tide-tables were, and it said *there*.

---

The letter it found thirty years after that.

In a press of the College of Tides' letters, the ones the College had been sent and hadn't wanted, which it had sent up the hill to the Library to be shelved *outside study*. Fourth bay from the hill end, bottom shelf, behind the Kessmoor tariff-rolls. Nobody had opened the press in a hundred years. The bird found the letter because it went through the press one winter, a sheet a night, for no better reason than that it knew where everything in the hall was and didn't know what was in that.

A long letter, to the College, from the first archivist of the knights' house on the river hill. In a frightened hand. All flourishes. Everything he'd been ordered to cut out of his black book, written out again for the College, so that it would be kept somewhere.

It read it in one night. It didn't stop.

*It was the most beautiful thing I have ever seen.*

*Footnote: it had read, by then, perhaps a third of the books in the Long Library. In all of them, it had not found anyone write that sentence plainly about anything. It found it in the middle of a frightened clerk's flourishes, about a thing in chains under a hill, and it did not read any more that night, though it was only the second bell.*

It put the letter back behind the tariff-rolls.

And it did nothing.

That was the part it went back to, on its last night, in the white light, with the perch's bones in the dish. Not the reading. The nothing. It had known, for two hundred years, what was under the river hill, and who had sold the chains, and what the noon oath was for. It had known that every day at noon men in yards a long way south said a thing that the College of Tides had written so that they wouldn't ask what they were saying. And at noon every day, when Vael's own bells went, it thought of that. And then it thought about something else.

It had been told to keep it. It kept it. Nobody asked.

*Footnote: keeping a thing is easy. You put it somewhere dark and sit on it. It had said that to the ferryman too, on the night he brought the whitefish, and he had laughed, and it had let him think it was a remark about other people.*

---

There had been so many readers.

That was what came up, on its last night, more than the roll. Not the great things. The readers. They came up through the hum one after another as books come up from the stacks when you send for them, each one with its shelf-mark, and the bird let them come, because there was nothing else to do in the white light, and because it was old, and because it suspected, as it suspected most things, from the evidence, that there would not be many more nights to let them come in.

*A Sarovel woman, a hundred and sixty years ago, sent to copy a book of hymns, who copied for nine years and wept every afternoon at the same hymn, the forty-first, and never said why. The bird never found out. It looked at the forty-first hymn many times afterwards. It was not a good hymn.*

*A Kessmoor engineer who came up the hall with a measuring-rod and measured the bird, without asking, and wrote the figures in a little book, and was bitten.*

*Three boys of the Scribes' College, two centuries apart, who each tried, in turn, to teach it a rude word. It had known every rude word in four kingdoms since before their grandfathers were born. It declined to use any of them. It took the boys' names. All three were Fourth rings in time, and none of them, it noticed, ever asked it where anything was again.*

*Hollis Dane of the Blue College, at nineteen, who stood at the desk and recited the whole of the Statutes of Wardens to it from memory, to show that he could, and missed a clause in the ninth part. The bird told him which. He went red to the collar and down the hall and did not come up it again in thirty-one years. It had heard he had a Fifth ring now and turned it outward when he talked. It had heard he'd come home from the forest this summer with both hands burned. It found it was sorry. It had not expected to be.*

*Wystan Lusk, Fourth ring, Scribes' College. Under the third window from the hill end, at the desk there, every day for forty years. White-haired now, ink to the elbows. Compiling a concordance of every milestone inscription in the old realm, in the order of the roads. Had asked it where something was perhaps a dozen times in forty years and been told, and had been wrong in his concordance once, about the stone at the fourth league on the Vael road, which the bird had told him, and he had looked at it a long time and said "Yes," and crossed it through, and thanked it. The bird thought him the best reader in the hall. When the lion came up the half-league, in the summer, and every reader in the hall stood up and backed into the shelves, Wystan Lusk had looked up at it, and down at his page, and dipped his pen. The bird had been, for some days, nearly happy about that.*

*Annet Mayle, who was crying.*

*Hew Corlis, who didn't finish.*

A list of corrections. That was most of what remembering was, if you did it honestly. It had said that to the ferryman. Who sat at the desk. What they read. What they got wrong.

*Footnote: and the ones who got nothing wrong. There had been only one of those, and it had been careful never to tell him, for eleven years, because it was afraid it would go to his head; and then it had told him, on the night of the whitefish, because it was afraid there wouldn't be time.*

---

The ferryman had come up the hall at twenty, with a ferryman's hands.

Big hands, chapped, too big for a pen, holding a letter from a lake-village priest and the Hearthmere ledgers, which nobody else wanted. He'd sat down at the desk at the foot of the perch on his first day because it was the desk nobody else wanted, being under a bird, and he'd opened the first ledger, and he had looked up at the bird.

Most of them looked up at it the first time. Most of them said *good morning*, or asked how old it was, or said nothing and took their ledgers to a desk further down the hall.

He'd said, "Why do they keep a bird in a library?"

"Why do they keep a ferryman in one?" the bird had said.

He'd thought about it. "To read the boring ledgers."

"There," the bird had said. "You have your answer."

*Footnote: the first of the seven.*

It had read his ledgers.

All of them, over eleven years, forty-one volumes in the end, in his own black ink, that was blacker than the College's and cost a third as much. He brought the month's volume up to the desk to work at, and some nights he went down the hall to eat and left it there, and didn't come back till morning. On those nights, it got down off the oak and stood on the desk and turned the pages with its beak. The Hearthmere glasses. Read low. Every bell of every night. A hole under the river hill, in a ferryman's untidy sevens, drawn and redrawn, month after month, on the same grid, and in the margins his notes to himself, which got less hopeful over the years and never stopped. *A draw. As if pumped.* *Not a fault in the glass.* *Wrote it up; told to stop.* *Wrote it up again.*

It had read every page. It had known, every page, what the hole was. It had the reason for it in the oak under its feet.

It had corrected his citations.

*Footnote: it told him a great many things, in eleven years. Where the Tervel was. What Saddon meant by a "second tide." That his spelling was a disgrace to his college and that his college deserved it. It did not tell him why they kept telling him to stop. It had been told to keep a thing, by a young man who didn't finish his sentence, and the ferryman had never asked.*

*That was not quite true. He'd asked it everything else.*

---

Then the glasses broke.

It had heard them. The whole hill had heard them, at midnight, a sound from the College of Tides' east gallery like a feast ending. And in the morning the ferryman had come up the half-league with glass in his hair and a chart under his arm and put the chart on the desk, the old one and the new one side by side, and told it.

It had listened. It didn't interrupt. It never did with him; he told things in the right order without being asked to, which was rare in a reader and unheard of in a man from the lake.

And then it had asked him where the hole was, though it knew, because it wanted to hear a person say it.

"Under Hearthmere," he'd said. "Under the river hill on the border. Where Greyhold is."

And the bird had gone flat.

It remembered that very clearly and could not describe it. It had stopped. Everything in it had stopped: the noting, the filing, the small dry voice that went along under everything it saw making its marks. Like the ferryman's glass, it thought afterwards, when he showed it to it: not low, not high. Nothing. It had sat on the oak with its claws shut on the wood till the wood creaked, and the noting didn't come back, and the hall was very large round it, and then a voice it had not used in two hundred years said, out of its own beak:

"Then the door is open."

*Footnote: it did not cite sources for this remark. It never did, to him. It had a letter behind the Kessmoor tariff-rolls and a roll in the oak and the word* unless*, and none of them said* door*, quite. It was, it supposed, a conclusion. It had been drawing it for two hundred years and never once let itself write it down.*

He had asked it what door. It had told him to go home and sleep.

And then it had asked him for the green glasses. Overnight. *Leave them with me.*

It knew why, while it said it. It wanted to know whether he would keep a thing that was his when someone old asked him for it. Whether he would hold it, or hand it up. Because if he would hand it up to a bird he had been fond of for eleven years, he would hand it up to anyone, and then there would be no point.

He'd thought about it too long. He'd stood at the foot of the perch with the chart in his hands and the glass in his hair and thought about it, because the bird had asked him for something and it had never asked him for anything.

"No," he'd said. "I'm sorry. No."

And it had laughed. It hadn't known it was going to. A dry sound, out of its chest, like a page torn.

*Good. That's the right answer. Remember you gave it.*

*Footnote: two days later the Blue College took the glasses anyway, and all forty-one ledgers, and the bird thought that the ferryman would come up the hall that afternoon to say so and he did not. He did not come for ten days. When he came he sat on the floor at the foot of the perch for an hour and said nothing, and the bird said nothing, and toward the end of the hour it leaned down and took a loose thread from the hem of his coat in its beak, where something stiff was sewn into the lining, and pulled it, gently, and he put his hand over the place. It let go. It had only wanted to know he had kept something.*

---

The summer had been the hum.

The thing on the lake. The lamps going white on the hill and brown below it, and the line between them coming down the hill a street a week, and the stone under the hill end humming so loud the readers' teeth hurt and the mice went. The ferryman came back in the summer with a forest in his coat and sat at the desk and told it everything, in order, for an evening, and it didn't interrupt once. The Ring. The six. *Sleep*, six times. The stag.

At the six, its feathers had come up along its back. At the stag it had shut its eye. It couldn't help either.

At the end it had asked him whether the walker gave back.

*Footnote: it knew, from the archivist's letter, what had lain on the tower steps at the door before the knights came, and what had been done to it with fire. The ferryman's walker on his glasses, a little draw going along the Hearthmere bank in short steps, taking and giving back. It had wondered, every day since the breaking, and not asked, in case the answer was no.*

*Yes*, he'd said. *It gives back. It's the only thing on my glasses that does.*

And then: *What's under the hill?*

*Footnote: not a why, strictly. It counted it anyway. It said, Not yet. It had since thought of several better answers and would not have given any of them.*

---

It went to the window for the knight.

The first time it had walked the hall in living memory. It knew what the readers thought of that, because it heard them think it, a quarter of a league of them standing up from their tables and staring as it went past on foot, a hop and a hop, with its wings half open. It had not walked so far since the hall was a third full. Its feet hurt on the stone. It found it didn't mind them staring. It found it didn't mind anything much, that afternoon, and filed that too.

The window over the lake road. It got up onto the sill with its beak and its claws and sat in the narrow slot of it, high up in the hill's face, with the white light at its back, and looked down at the road.

It waited an hour.

They came up the road from the lake gate, with the street going quiet in front of them. A woman in a Hearthmere coat, on foot, with a sword. And beside her, walking as if the road were his, a grey lion as big as a dray-horse, with a band of pale at the roots of his mane.

It had read the letter forty times. It had never seen a drawing. It didn't need one.

The ferryman had said her name at the desk, a week before, among everything else. It had filed it. It took it out now and said it down out of the window, to the road, the one thing it said that afternoon.

"Maren Ashdown."

She looked up.

So did he.

*Footnote: it had been looked at by a great many readers in three hundred years, some of them very fierce. It had not, until that afternoon, been looked at by anything older than itself. It discovered that it did not know what to do with its face, and turned away from the window, and went back along the hall to the oak, and sat there with its eye shut till dark. The readers said afterwards that it had said something terrible out of the window. It let them.*

---

It had shown the ferryman the roll on the hundred-and-thirty-eighth night.

It had sent the under-clerk for him. It had told the boy to say *please*, because it had never said *please* to anyone in the boy's hearing and it wanted the ferryman to know, before he was up the half-league, that this was not citations. The boy had looked at it as though it had asked him to fly.

And the ferryman had come, at night, alone, with the lamps all white, walking slowly the whole length of the hall, as if he wanted to remember it. And it had got down off the oak and opened the panel with its beak and asked him to take the case out, because its beak was old.

*Footnote: this was true. It was also the first time since Hew Corlis that any hand had touched the case, and it had wanted to see his hands on it. Ferryman's hands. Too big for a pen.*

*Nobody has asked*, it had said. *Nobody knew to*, he said. *No. That was the point.*

He had read it all, half the night, kneeling on the stone with his palm on the vellum, and the bird had sat on the floor beside the desk on its own feet with its head on one side and watched him read. It had watched readers read for three hundred years. It had never watched one read a thing that was about him. His college. His hole. His eleven years. It watched his face go from a reader's face to something else, and it watched him find the noon oath in the third clause, and it watched him stop.

And at the end it had told him to read the appendix, and he'd read the working-words, and it had watched them go in.

It knew what that looked like. It had done it. You read a thing in the hooked script as a Keeper reads, all the way in, and it goes into you as ink goes into a good vellum, not lying on the top but into the skin of it, and after that there's no scraping it out.

He'd read them twice. He hadn't written them down.

*There*, it had thought. *Now it is kept somewhere it can't be impounded. And not by me.*

*Unless.* It had decided, sitting on the floor in the white light, that this was the end of Hew Corlis's sentence. That a man should not be the last to know what his own college had done. That the door was open and the door-keeper had walked past its window and lay on the shore of its lake. That it was old. It had told the ferryman some of that. *I am tired of keeping them by myself.* It had been the truest thing it had said aloud in a century, and he had looked as if he didn't know what to do with it, and he hadn't needed to.

*Footnote: it had not considered, that night, that a man can be impounded too. It considered it now, on the last night, with the frost round its feet. It filed it under its own name, which it did not have, and so had to file it under* bird*, with everything else.*

---

The lion had come up on the hundred-and-fortieth day, with the knight.

Through the great door, which was high enough, with his head down. Half a league. Past Wystan Lusk, dipping his pen. And he'd stopped at twenty paces from the oak and the bird had looked at him for a long time, its feathers lifting all down its back and staying up, and then it had said the only name it had for him.

*Door-keeper.*

He came to ten paces and lay down.

It had told him he'd kept it a long time, and the knight had said aloud, for it, what he answered. *Not long enough.*

*Nobody keeps anything long enough*, it had said, and believed it, and been sitting on three hundred and four years of the proof.

It had read the stubs in the black book and laughed at the frightened archivist who left a foot. It had read the charcoal at the back, the sentence she'd copied off the lock, and stopped laughing. It had given her one word of that. *Together.* It had kept the rest.

*Footnote: that one it kept on a shelf it did not take down, not even now, on the last night. It had made no note of what was on the shelf. It had made a note that the shelf was there.*

And on the hundred-and-fifty-second day it had told them the history. Once, plainly, from the letter, without the flourishes, with the knight writing it down in square river-watch letters in Kessmoor graphite and the ferryman on the floor with his knees up and the door-keeper at ten paces. The quarrymen. The steps going down. The most beautiful thing. The egg and the lantern. The chains already on it.

And the cat on the tower steps.

The lion had stood up.

It had seen him do it. It was the only one in the hall who saw it from the front. The great grey head coming up off the floor, slowly, and the body under it, and the old scar along the left flank where the fur grew wrong, and the eyes not on the bird at all but on something a long way behind it. *Fire,* the knight had said, white, with her hand flat on the desk, for him. *On the steps. He remembers the fire.*

*Footnote: it had read the account of that fire more often than anything else in the hall. It had never once been in a room with somebody it had happened to. It went on telling. It had said it would tell it once, and it was old, and it did. It believed it had said the next part, about the bronze and the warden at the door, rather well. It did not remember saying it at all.*

---

And then, four days ago, at noon, the thing on the lake had come up onto the hill.

The bird had felt it begin before anyone. The hum, which had been rising all morning to a note it had never heard, stopped. All of it. In one breath. Every lamp in the hall went out together, and the stone under the oak went from full to nothing between one beat of its heart and the next, so that the bird, which had stood on that stone in its sleep for three hundred years, nearly fell off its perch for the lack of it.

Then the hill shook.

The shelves leaned. Books came out of the high stacks in the dark and fell, all down the hall, a long soft roar of them hitting the floor, the old rolls and the bound books and the loose leaves going up and coming down. The oak swayed. The bird held on with both feet and its beak, and held on, and the oak swayed under it like a branch in wind, and it remembered, for the first time in three hundred years, a branch in wind. And something white. And cold.

And then, from under it, from under the floor of the hill end, far down, a breath.

Warm. It came up through the stone and through the oak and through its feet. It smelled of stone, and of something old under the stone, and it was like standing at the door of a bread oven. The archivist had written that. *Like standing in a bread oven. Like standing in the sun.* The bird had read it forty times and thought it a flourish.

Then it was gone. The breath, and the warmth with it, and whatever had been under the floor of the hill end, under the Undercroft door, under the oak, under the bird, for as long as there had been a hall.

*Footnote: sitting on a thing is not knowing it. It had sat on two things. It had read about one.*

That night the lamps came back white, and the hum came back wrong, and the frost came, and the ferryman's sister went up and down Candle Row telling everyone five days.

---

The fifth day came up grey.

It saw it come. It turned on the oak to look out of the great window behind it, the tallest in the hall, that looked south-east across the city to the lake and the mountains, and it watched the grey come up behind the Iron Fells and the snow on their tops go pink and then white.

The lake was ice. It had been ice for three days, a floor of it, grey-white, with the old broken plates frozen in at angles where the thing on the lake had lain. The lower city below the hill was dark under its white lamps. Smoke from a few chimneys. People moving on the steps of Candle Row, small, like ink-marks.

The cold came off the lake that morning so hard the window glass furred over from the outside, and the bird watched the frost grow across the panes in ferns.

It saw the ice move.

Mid-morning. Not at the shore. In the middle of the lake, where the island had lain. The ice lifted. It went up in a long low hump, as a page lifts when the binding's wrong, and cracked across, and something came up through the crack that was the colour of the ice and not ice. Pale. White and blue. Coil after coil of it, breaking up through the grey, each one as broad as a ferry is long, with fog coming off it so thick the bird lost the far shore. And a head. Long, and blunt, and lifting. The bird could not see eyes.

It had seen the drawing. The ferryman had copied it in the round room under the Ring, onto his silk. The serpent among the six.

*Footnote: in the summer, when he unrolled the silk, it had looked at the serpent a long time and touched only the lion.*

The water rose under the thing. It came over the shore. All at once, in one long wave, a grey wall of lake with ice in it, into the lower city, between the houses, and the bird heard it from the hill end through the glass and half a league of stone: not a roar. A long hiss, and then cracking, a great deal of cracking, like a hall full of readers all breaking their pens at once.

It watched for a long time. It couldn't see much. Fog, and the grey water in the low streets, and, once, down by the shore, behind the fish-market, a line across a street that the water leaned on and didn't come over. A wall where there hadn't been one.

*Footnote: it had no evidence for who had put it there. It did not need any.*

The thing lay on the lake in rings for an hour. Then it went. South, down the lake to the lake's foot, sliding, in its own fog, and the bird heard the lip of the lake go, far off, a deep long sound in the stone of the hill. And the water went after it, down the valley, and the lake was half gone, and the lower city was half gone under it.

It turned round on the oak, away from the window, and looked down the hall.

*Footnote: the ferryman would be at the wall. It knew where he would be as it knew where the Tervel was. And the ferryman had read the stir three times and called the hour and been right, again, which was his least attractive quality, and the under-clerk's mother was on Candle Row, alive.*

*It found that it had not been afraid all morning until then, and was afraid now, and could not think what of.*

---

The dragons came in the early afternoon.

It heard them first as weather. A sound over the hill like the wind getting up. Then a roar, close, over the roof, that came down through the slates and the vaults and set every lamp in the hall swinging on its chain, and the white light swinging with them, the shadows of the shelves going back and forth across the floor.

Then more. Fire, somewhere, on another roof. It smelled it through the lead of the great window: thatch, and something else.

Honey. Burnt.

It knew that smell. Everyone in Isvaren did. Hunters' smoke. Binders' smoke. It came in at the edges of the windows along the front of the hall, sweet and thick and wrong, and the bird's feathers went up all over its body at once, and stayed.

Something heavy hit the roof above the hall, a long way down toward the lake end. The slates jumped all along the hall, a rattle going away from the bird into the distance. Something sliding on the slates. A dragon's voice. Then shouting, and a man screaming, very high, and the screaming coming into the hall, in at the great door half a league off, and down it, a little way, and stopping.

*Footnote: dragons. It knew of a red, and a green, and a bronze, and a barley field. It had been waiting for them for longer than anyone would believe; it had said so to the knight, which was more than it had said to anyone. It noted that, if this were they, they were late. Everyone was late. It had been in a library three hundred years and never once seen anyone arrive on time.*

And under all of it, from the lake end of the hall, boots.

It had heard them come in at the great door before the roaring started, and taken them, for a little while, for Wardens.

---

It counted them.

A great many. More than ten. Heavy, in step and then not in step, as soldiers walk when they have stopped pretending, and with them a sound it didn't know at first and then did. Iron. Something iron, carried between two men, swinging. And under that, a dragging.

They were still a long way short of the hill bays. They would take, it knew to the moment, as long as it took a man to walk the rest of the half-league at a hunter's pace. A long time. Long enough to read a short letter. Long enough to decide something.

*Footnote: it had been asked, in three hundred years, by a great many readers, whether it could fly. It had said, Yes. They had asked why it didn't. It had asked them where they meant it to go. None of them had ever had an answer, and on this afternoon, for the first time, the bird thought it had one, and that it was the wrong one.*

It looked at the window behind it. Tall, white, the frost on it in ferns. Beyond the frost, sky. It could break that glass. It was old, and the glass was old. It could go up off the oak and through it and out over the drowned city and the half-gone lake and over the Fells, and nobody below would have a bolt fast enough.

It could not take the case.

It had looked at the case a long time, two centuries ago, the first night it drew it out of the oak, and weighed it in its beak, and known. Wood and silver, and the vellum. As long as a man's forearm. It could drag it. It could not lift it.

So: the window, and the hall without the case. Or the case, and the hall.

*Footnote: that is not quite right. The thing in the case was already somewhere else. In a ferryman. In a man's head, unwritten, where nobody could impound it. Whatever they took from this hall today, it would be a copy.*

*It found that it did not like copies going to such men. It found that it had been a keeper too long to stop on a technicality.*

It got down off the oak.

---

Its feet hurt on the stone. They had hurt for a long time now. It went round the foot of the oak, a hop and a hop, with its wings half open, through the frost, which formed on its feathers and went.

The panel. The crack at its edge. Its beak in the crack. The panel came open, as it had come open for Hew Corlis, and for the bird two centuries ago, and for the ferryman in the summer. The hollow. The case.

It took the silver at one end in its beak and pulled.

It was heavier than it had been. Or the bird was lighter. It drew it out a hand's breadth, and rested, and a hand's breadth, and rested, with the boots coming down the hall the whole time, nearer, not hurrying, and the hum knocking up through the floor under it, and its breath going in and out of its beak in small hard puffs of white. It got the case out of the hollow onto the stone. It dragged it round the foot of the oak into the open, in front of the perch, where the desk was, where anyone coming down the hall would see it.

Then it climbed up onto it.

It stood on the case. Its feet on the black silver at the ends and the old dark wood in between, warm, warmer than the stone, and the mark of the College burned into the wood under its claws, the wave over the glass. It folded its wings. It let its feathers stand, all of them, so that it was twice its size and looked it.

It faced down the hall.

*Footnote: it had occurred to it that it was standing on the thing it had been asked to keep, in full view, with the panel open behind it, so that the first man down the hall would know exactly what to take. It acknowledged the objection. It noted, against it, that it was done with putting things in the dark and sitting on them. If they wanted the case they could take it from under a reader, in the light. That at least would be a citation.*

They came out from between the last shelves in a crowd.

Men in grey and brown, in hunters' coats, wet to the knee with frost. A dozen with crossbows, and others behind. Two of them carrying between them, on poles, an iron cage, bars as thick as a thumb, the kind the Blue College kept its hawks in, but bigger. Big enough for a dog. Two more with a net, rolled, and lead weights at its edge clicking. And one, who was not with them so much as near them, dragged in by an arm and let go against a shelf, a man with his coat burned to rags down one side and his face blistered red and wet, who sat down against the books and rocked and smelled of hair and honey and fire.

*Footnote: a binder. It could smell the pots on him under the burning. Burned off something's neck, by the smell, and by the shake of him; ran in here to be away from whatever had done it, and been caught up. Nobody had asked him anything for a long time either, by the look of him.*

And in front of them, a little apart, a man with his hands behind his back.

Grey beard. A plain coat, good wool, not new. He smelled of the pass, of snow and goose-grease and a camp's smoke, and very faintly, old in the wool, of honey. He looked at the bird on the case for a long moment as a buyer looks at a horse, and then round the end of the hall, the oak, the desk, the dish with the perch's bones in it, the window. He didn't look at the books. He was the first man the bird had ever seen come to the hill end and not look at the books.

"Good afternoon," he said.

The bird said nothing.

"You'll be the keeper."

"This is a library," said the bird. "There are a great many keepers in it. Most of them are books."

The man with the grey beard almost smiled.

"I've come for a paper," he said.

"There are some hundreds of thousands. You'll want to be more particular."

"I can be," he said. "I've been told exactly." He didn't take a slip out. He didn't need to. He had it by heart, like a road he'd been paid to walk. "I've come for the College of Tides' contract..."

---

*Footnote: and there, at the third shelf from the end of the hill bays, on the far side, where the shadows of the shelves lay across each other, someone was standing.*

*It knew the step. It had heard it come down the gallery stair and along the hall, fast, a reader who did not run in the Library, and then running, a ferryman's careful walk gone to pieces, as if every flagstone might turn and he had decided not to care. It had heard him stop.*

*It did not look at him. It had one good eye. It kept it on the man with the grey beard.*

*Go back,* it thought, at the shelves, at the step, in a voice it did not have. *Go back down the hall. You have it already. You are the copy that matters. Go back to your wall.*

*It had never been able to make him do anything. It had been fond of him for that.*

---

"...the College of Tides' contract," the man with the grey beard was saying. Politely. As if he were asking for a book at the door-end tables. "With the lord of the river hill. And its appendix. That's all. Nothing else in the hall. You can keep the rest."

*Footnote: in three hundred and four years, the first person to ask for it by its right name. Party, counterparty, appendix. The citation was perfect. It was a hired man's citation, learned off somebody else's index. The bird had known since the morning of the breaking that someone would come and ask properly one day, and had always supposed, without evidence, that it would be a reader.*

"I know what you want," said the Old Reader.

"Then you'll know I've come a long way for it."

"Everyone has come a long way," said the bird. "Everyone who has ever come to this hall has come a long way. I have kept that case for three hundred and four years. In all that time, nobody has asked for it." Its feathers didn't settle. "You are the first."

"I'm asking."

"And the answer is no."

The man with the grey beard looked at it for a moment.

"It wasn't a question," he said. Almost gently.

He nodded to the two with the net.

---

They threw it well. It noted that. They'd done it before, to things that could fly. They threw it from either side at once, so that it opened in the air over the bird like a hand closing, and the lead weights at its edge spun.

The bird opened its wings.

All the way. It hadn't, in living memory. It didn't know, until they were out, how far they went: out and out from its body, past the edges of the case, wider than the desk, the long flight-feathers stiff and frayed at their ends and full, full of the hall and the flood and the white light, humming in their shafts, so that it felt them as it had not felt them in three hundred years, every one, to the tip.

The net came down on them.

And slid off. As frost had slid off them for four nights. It touched and went, slid off the feathers on both sides at once and fell in a heap round the case, and the lead weights rang on the stone.

It folded its wings.

One of the men swore.

"Old things," said the man with the grey beard, looking at the net. "In a flood. Everything's full today. Even feathers." He didn't sound angry. He sounded like a man noting the weather. "Bring the cage."

They brought the cage. They put it down in front of the case with its door open, an arm's length from the bird's feet, iron, cold, smelling of every hawk the Blue College had ever kept in it, and they stepped back from it, and waited.

The bird looked at the cage.

*Footnote: it had been, it reflected, in a cage of a kind for three hundred and four years, and a comfortable one, with a perch and a dish and a great many interesting books. It had gone in of its own accord, on a winter night, at a young man's word. It was not going into this one.*

It looked up at the man with the grey beard.

"No," it said.

Behind it, the great window stood tall and white with the frost on it in ferns, and the sky behind the frost. It didn't turn its head. It didn't need to. It saw two of the men see the window, and see the bird not look at it, and lift their crossbows toward the glass, ready, as men watch a door for a dog that will bolt.

It didn't go.

---

He walked out from between the shelves.

*No*, it thought.

He came across the open stone in front of the perch, past the crossbows, which turned to him, past the man with the grey beard, who turned to him, and he didn't look at any of them. He came to the case. And turned round. And stood in front of the bird, with his back to it.

It could feel him. The warmth of his legs through his wet breeches against the front of its feathers. He smelled of the lake and of the ferry-folk's boat-shed, tar and wet rope, and of blood, old, from his nose, and of stone dust, and under everything, as he always had, of his own black ink. His hands hung at his sides. They were red. Not the red of cold; raw, from the wrist down, swollen at the knuckles, the hands of a man who had held something too full for too long that morning. It could see them past his hips. They shook a little. He didn't seem to know.

*Footnote: one more thing it had not told him, in eleven years: that he was not to do this. It had supposed he wouldn't. It had supposed a great many things about the ferryman over the years, and been wrong about almost none, and it found, with its feathers against the backs of his legs, that it had kept its one error for last.*

"Reader Vael," said the man with the grey beard.

The ferryman didn't say anything.

"The tide-reader. The one who left the ring." The grey beard looked at him with something that might have been interest. "There's talk of you on the hill. They've been counting the dead up there since noon on the hundred-and-seventy-second, and they say your name between every one."

"Who are you?"

"A hunter," said the man. "Hired." He looked down past the ferryman's legs at the case under the bird's feet. "I was hired to fetch a paper. I didn't know till I came here that I'd been hired to fetch a reader too. But I think I have."

---

The bird understood it before the ferryman did.

It knew that, then. It was old, and it had been reading men's faces over the tops of books since before this one's grandfather was weaned, and it saw where the grey beard's eyes went. Not to the case. To the back of the ferryman's head.

"He'll need someone who can read it," said the man with the grey beard.

Reasonably. A man laying out the terms of a sale.

"My master. The man who pays for all this." A small movement of his hand at the hunters, the cage, the net, the crossbows. "He knows what's in that case. He was told. He's wanted it a long time. But he can't read it. I've been told that too. Nobody can read it but your oldest masters, and they won't. And one Second-ring reader of the College of Tides who was seen at a reading-desk at the foot of this perch for a whole night this summer with his palm on the vellum." He looked at the ferryman. "Who's read it. Who's got it in his head."

*Footnote: seen. By whom, it did not know. The under-clerk had gone home that night; it had sent him. But a library has windows and galleries and half a league of shelves, and is full of people who read over other people's shoulders. It ought to have known. It had been reading over shoulders for three hundred years.*

*Footnote: it had given him the roll so that the roll would be kept somewhere nobody could impound it. In a man. It had not, sitting on the floor that night in the white light, so pleased with the end of Hew Corlis's sentence, thought what men do with a thing kept in a man.*

*It had put the case in the dark for three hundred and four years and sat on it. And then it had put it in him.*

"My master pays better than your college," said the man. "He doesn't pay in rings. He pays in coin."

He took one out of his coat and put it on the reading-desk at the foot of the perch. A new silver coin. It rang on the wood once and lay still in the frost, bright, unworn, a ship on the face of it, and something small struck into the rim.

*Footnote: in three hundred years readers had left on that desk ink, sand, crumbs, blood once, a tooth, several love-letters, a great many corrections, and the bird's dish. Nobody had ever left coin. It had not thought the desk would hold it.*

"Corvin," said the ferryman.

The grey beard didn't say yes. He didn't say no.

*Footnote: a name. It had no citation for it. It filed it, under the coin, and found that it was the last new thing it would file, and that it would rather it had been almost anything else.*

"Come with us," said the man with the grey beard. "Read it for him. You'll never want for anything. You can measure every tide in Winshire."

"No," said the ferryman.

*There*, the bird thought, before it could stop itself, like a junior. *That's the right answer.*

---

The man with the grey beard nodded.

Not to the ferryman. Past him. To the side, to the end of the hall where the floor had broken open on the day of the hill, where the Undercroft stair came up out of the dark. And up it, slowly, a man.

The bird smelled him before it saw him. Burned wool. Burned skin. And something else, which it had smelled once before, at that desk, on a drawer of the Blue College who'd come up the hall one hard winter and asked it where the books on drawing were, and gone away again grey in the face: the smell of a man who has had too much go through him. Hot stone. Ash.

He came up the last steps onto the floor of the hall. Big, bald, his coat black from the cuffs to the elbows and his hands below it the colour of the ash it smelled. He stood with his feet apart on the old stone.

"Step aside, Reader," said the man with the grey beard. "He's only got one more in him."

The ferryman didn't step aside.

The bird felt his legs set against its feathers. No reader it had ever known stood like that. A ferryman's son, on a deck, with the lake coming over the side.

*Go*, it thought.

The big man put out his grey hands.

---

The air went.

That was all the bird knew of it. The air in front of it went, all at once, the whole hall's worth of it, and the ferryman went with it. One moment the warmth of him against its feathers, and the ink and tar smell of him, and his raw hands at his sides. The next, nothing. Cold, where he had been. The blow went past the bird and its feathers turned it as they had turned the net, it felt them turn it, a great push along every vane, and it rocked on the case and held.

And down the bay, thirty paces, between the shelves, the ferryman went through the air like a book flung down a hall by a Master in a temper. Back first. Not a sound out of him. Into the desk under the third window. Wystan Lusk's desk. It went over, and he went over with it, and the concordance went up in a white burst of pages, forty years of milestones in the order of the roads, and came down on him.

Up in the roof, all along the hall, the slates jumped.

The bird looked at the place on the floor of the bay where he lay among the pages and the broken desk.

He didn't move.

*Footnote*, it began, and couldn't think of one.

By the Undercroft stair the big man had gone down on both knees. His grey hands lay on the stone in front of him, palms down, and smoke came up off the backs of them, thin, grey, in the white light. His head hung. The bird looked at him and knew, as it knew where the Tervel was, that he had drawn on the floor of the hill in the flood, on stone fuller than anything he had ever stood on, and that it had gone through him and taken him with it, and that he would not be getting up.

It found that it was not sorry, and filed that, and found it did not care where.

Then, down the bay, the ferryman's elbow moved.

---

It saw the grey beard lift his hand.

It saw three crossbows come up.

Six paces. It counted them, from habit. The same distance as from the oak to the second table, where a Sarovel woman had sat for nine years and wept at the forty-first hymn.

It could open its wings.

It knew that, standing on the case. They were still full. It could feel them full, every shaft to the tip, humming. It could open them, and perhaps they would turn a bolt as they had turned a net and a blow, and perhaps not. It did not know. It noted that it did not know. Down the bay, among Wystan Lusk's pages, the ferryman had got onto his elbows and lifted his head and was looking at it, along thirty paces of frost and white light, and his mouth was moving, and no sound was coming out.

*No*, his mouth was saying. *No.*

The bird looked at him.

It did not open its wings. It was busy. It was looking at him with its one good eye, and it was counting, which was its oldest habit, older than *where*. Not the bolts. Its words. How many it had left, and which.

The bolts went.

A flat hard sound, three at once, like three doors slammed in a corridor.

It felt them. In the breast. Where the feathers were thickest, where it tucked its head at night. Heat, and a great push, that rocked it back on its feet on the silver of the case, and then forward again, and then a heaviness, coming down through it from the breast to the feet, as a book's weight comes down through your arms when you take it off a high shelf.

It sat down.

On the case. Slowly. Folding its legs under it, as it did on the oak at night. And it felt its feathers come down. All of them. Every feather that had stood up since the honey came in at the windows, settling, one after another, lying flat against its body, smooth, as they had not lain in days. It felt the frost touch them. And this time, stay.

*Footnote*, it thought, very clearly. *That's new.*

---

He was coming.

On his elbows. Over the pages. Over the frost. His legs didn't come under him; he left them behind him and pulled himself along with his raw hands and his elbows on the old stone, thirty paces, between the shelves, past the hunters, who let him. Someone laughed. Someone else told him to shut up.

The bird watched him the whole way.

It had watched him come up that hall a thousand times. At twenty, with a priest's letter. At thirty-one, with glass in his hair; that summer, with a forest in his coat. At night, with every lamp white, slowly, as if he wanted to remember it. It had never watched him come on his elbows. It did not think it wanted to remember this, and it did not think it would have to.

He got to the foot of the oak. To the case. His face was close to it now, closer than it had ever been, closer than he had ever come in eleven years, because he was a polite man and a reader and knew that you didn't put your face in a keeper's. His face was grey and wet. There was blood on his lip. His eyes were on the bird's good eye and nothing else in the hall.

It looked back.

*Footnote: it had one correction left, and it knew where it went. Against the last line on an arch at a bridge it had never seen. Against a College's fifth clause. Against a young man in a wet coat who said* don't show anyone *and never finished. Against three hundred and four years of a bird on an oak, answering* where.

"Ferryman," it said.

Its voice came out the same. It was glad of that. Dry. Exact. Like a page turned.

"Ask," it said.

He didn't understand. It could see him not understand. A reader with a word he didn't have a source for. His mouth opened.

It had enough for one more. It had counted.

"Ask, ferryman." It kept the eye on his face. It wanted him to have it from the front, where he could see where it came from, as you should with anything. "Ask everything."
