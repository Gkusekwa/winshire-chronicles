---

There is a saying among the binders of Hallowmere, in the narrow street behind the crown's record-house where the glue-pots smoke from dawn to dark, that a book is the only door in the world that can't be locked from the inside. You can chain a book to a desk. You can shut it in a press, or a chest, or a room, and turn a key on the room. But once somebody has it open in front of them, it's open, and it can't keep a word back from whoever is reading.

Osric Hale's father said it most weeks of Osric's childhood. He said it with a bone folder in one hand and his spectacles pushed up on his forehead, and he usually said it to a customer who had come in wanting something taken out. A page with a debt on it. A will with the wrong son's name. A parish roll with a birth on it that somebody's grandmother would rather hadn't happened.

"I'll bind you anything," his father would say. "I won't cut."

"Nobody'll know."

"Everybody'll know. A cut shows." And he'd open whatever ledger was on the bench at the gutter, wide, so the spine creaked, and show them the sewing: the thread going in and out of every fold, each sheet holding its neighbour. "Take a page out and you leave its stub behind. Sewn in. You can't get it out without unpicking the whole book, and then the book knows anyway. Anyone who looks will see a gap there, like a pulled tooth."

The customers went away and found a binder who would. There were plenty in that street. Osric's father stayed poor and honest, and Osric, who had stood behind the counter since he could see over it, learned two things from him: how a book is put together, and that the most interesting thing in any book is what somebody has tried to take out of it.

---

He went across the street to the record-house when he was nine, as a boy to sweep and carry. By twelve he was copying. By fifteen he could read the old script, the square hooked letters on milestones and temple walls that most of Hearthmere took for decoration, better than the clerk who was meant to be teaching him, and by twenty he had read everything in the record-house, or said he had, often enough that the clerks stopped arguing.

He was tall by then, and broad with it, and had already begun to stoop from going through doors that had been built for smaller men. He had a beard that wouldn't grow in properly at the sides. He knew the dates of every king of Hearthmere and the names of their horses. He thought a great deal of himself, and with some reason, and he knew it, and he thought it was charming that he knew it.

When he was twenty-three a letter came to the record-house from Greyhold. The archivist there was old and his eyes were going. He wanted a boy who could read the old hand and wouldn't mind the dark. The head clerk read the letter aloud in the copying room, and looked over the top of it at Osric, and everybody else in the room looked at Osric too, with an interest that was mostly relief.

He walked the King's Road in the spring. Seven days. Paved as far as the capital's last milestone, then gravel, then mud, and the inns every six leagues getting smaller and worse. He came down the last hill on the seventh evening and saw the river for the first time, the Wend, wide and brown and slow, with the far bank low and green and foreign, and on the near bank, on its hill where the river bent, the grey walls of the school.

He went in at the gate with his bag on his shoulder, and a knight on the gate with a hound at his knee looked him up and down and said, "You'll be the archivist's boy."

He was twenty-three. He had read everything in the record-house at Hallowmere. He said, "Under-archivist."

"Aye," said the knight. "The archivist's boy. Stair's under the north tower. Mind your head."

He didn't, and hit it.

---

The archive went back a long way under the north tower.

It was a single vaulted room, longer than the refectory above it, cut down into the rock of the hill so that the floor was a good way below the yard. You came down to it by a narrow stone stair from the yard, under an arch with a line of hooked letters cut in the lintel that nobody ever looked up at, and in at an oak door. Inside, a long table stood under a hanging lamp, and past the table the stacks went away into the dark, shelf after shelf, ledgers and rolls and boxes and bundles tied with ribbon gone brown, the duty books of three hundred years.

The lamp over the table was the first thing he had ever seen burn without oil. He stood under it that first evening with his bag still on his shoulder and looked up into it for so long that the old man at the table cleared his throat.

"It does that," said Warden Amos Tench. "It's done it for three hundred years. You'll get used to it. Sit down, you're blocking my light."

Tench was small and dry and bald, with a white fringe and very pale eyes that had gone milky at the edges, and he wore his spectacles on a ribbon round his neck and two more pairs in his coat. He couldn't read small print any more without all three. He had a ring of keys on his belt, and among them one that was longer than the rest, black and heavy and plain, as long as a man's forearm.

Osric saw it at once. He was a binder's son. He noticed the parts of things that didn't match.

He noticed the door the same evening.

It was at the very back of the room, past the last of the stacks, where the floor ran on bare and swept for a few yards and then dropped in three broad shallow steps. At the bottom of the steps, set into the old grey stone of the tower's foundation, was a door. Low. Black oak gone the colour of iron, with three iron bands across it, and no handle. Only an iron plate with a keyhole in it, and the plate as big as a man's hand.

Round the keyhole ran a ring of the old letters. Small. Square. Worn almost smooth.

He took the lamp off the end of the table, an ordinary brass oil one, and went down the room and down the three steps and held it to the plate and read them. It took him a while. The strokes were worn, and the hand was older than anything in the record-house, and the old tongue built its words from the back, so you had to read each one twice over before it sat still.

He came back up the room pleased with himself.

"It's a ward," he said. "Round the keyhole. It turns a word aside. Nobody opens it by saying anything to it."

Tench didn't look up from the ledger he was holding two inches from his face.

"Yes," he said.

"What's behind it?"

"Cellar."

"What's in the cellar?"

"Cellar things." Tench turned a page. "The ledgers for the year of the long frost want dusting. Third stack, top shelf. There's a ladder."

---

That was the spring.

He dusted the year of the long frost, and the years on either side of it. He learned the archive as you'd learn a town, by walking it: which stacks were sound and which had the damp in them, where the mice got in, which ribbons held letters that were only letters and which held letters that had been kept for a reason. He found three mistakes in the duty books in his first month and pointed them out to Tench, who said, "Yes," and didn't correct them. He found a ballad-sheet from Millbrook two hundred years old that somebody had used to wrap a cheese, and he read it, and laughed, and put it in a box.

He asked about the door four more times.

The second time Tench said, "Cellar." The third time he said, "Damp." The fourth time he took off two of his three pairs of spectacles and looked at Osric with his milky pale eyes, and said, "Ask me again and I'll send you back to Hallowmere with a note saying you drink."

"I don't drink."

"They won't know that."

Osric stopped asking. He didn't stop looking. He'd sit at the long table in the evenings with his back half to the far end of the room, and every so often, when he looked up from a page to rest his eyes, he found that he was looking down the room at the three steps, and had to look away.

Over the inside of the archive door, where you only saw it on your way out, somebody had carved a line in the old letters. *What is written here is true. What is true is not all written here.* He'd read it his first night, on his way up to bed, and laughed out loud on the stair.

"The first archivist," Tench had said behind him. "His little joke."

"It's a good one."

"I thought so," said Tench. "When I was your age."

---

At the noon bell in Greyhold you faced east, and said it.

He did it with everybody else, from his first day. It was the first thing they'd told him at the gate, after *mind your head*. When the bell went, you stopped where you were and turned toward the inner wall, the oldest stones in the place, grey and huge, and you said the words. The knights said them. The students in their lines said them. The grooms said them in the barn door with their forks in their hands, and the cooks in the kitchen door with their sleeves rolled, and so did Osric, at the top of the archive stair, squinting in the light.

*Keep the watch below, and do not ask.*

He'd looked it up, of course. In his second week. It was in the founding book of the orders, which lived in the Captain's room at the top of the north tower and which the Captain let him read after he'd washed his hands in front of her. *I will keep the watch below, and I will not ask what I keep.* Sworn by every knight of every order, every day, since the charter. Nobody had written down what the watch was. There was no gloss. There was no note in any margin. He went through every book in the archive that might have had one and none of them did, and he decided it was the kind of thing that grows up round an old place: a form of words, worn smooth by saying, like the good wishes at the bottom of a letter. Nobody expects the weather to be fair because you've written that you hope it will be.

He said it every day facing east and thought about what he'd have for supper.

---

In the autumn, on a day of frost, at the end of the noon bell, Tench was waiting for him at the bottom of the archive stair.

That was strange in itself. Tench didn't come up the stair at noon. Tench didn't come up the stair at all if he could help it. His knees, he said, had gone down in the year of the long frost and never come back up. He was standing at the foot of the steps in the cold with his hand on the wall and all three pairs of spectacles round his neck.

"Merrow's coming down tonight," he said. "After the last bell."

Osric knew Merrow. Everybody knew Merrow. A knight of the gate, past fifty, grey as a badger, who'd been a farmer somewhere in the south before the crown wanted bonded men for the gate and came looking for them. He had an ox-beast, a great grey hump-backed thing with a white face, that followed him round the yard like a dog and wouldn't settle in the Long Barn unless he was in it. He had a wife in the knights' lodging and four children and a laugh you could hear from the river wall. Osric had never said ten words to him.

"Why?"

"Wash your hands," said Tench. "Wear the other shirt."

"Why's Merrow coming down?"

Tench looked at him a moment. The pale eyes were hard to read. They always had been.

"To see you," he said, and went in, one step at a time, and shut the door.

---

He spent the afternoon not working.

He sat at the long table with a duty book open in front of him and turned a page now and then. He'd heard things. Everybody heard things, at Greyhold, about the inner circle: the old knights, and the wardens, and the ones who wore no mark of it at all but whom the Captain sent for at odd hours. There was a circle within the orders, the students said, as there was a circle within anything, and it kept old customs. He'd thought of it, when he thought of it, as a kind of club. Old men and old women who liked to know things other people didn't. He'd thought, a little, that he'd be good at it.

He looked at the door at the end of the room.

He thought of the long black key on Tench's belt. He thought of the ward round the keyhole that turned a word aside, which he had read on his first night when nobody else in the place had bothered to try, and he thought that perhaps somebody had noticed. Perhaps that was what this was. He was twenty-three. He'd been at Greyhold six months. He felt something rising in him that he would have called, if anybody had asked, a scholar's curiosity, and which was mostly vanity, and he knew that too, and didn't mind.

He washed his hands. He put on the other shirt.

At supper in the refectory he couldn't eat, and the student next to him took his bread.

---

The last bell went. He sat at the long table under the lamp that burned without oil and waited.

Tench sat at the head of the table in the archivist's chair, with a blanket over his knees and the black book closed in front of him. Osric had seen the black book before. It lived on a shelf of its own by the table, and Tench had let him look at it in the summer, at the list at the front of it: *Watch,* in square spare letters, and above that one line, *From the closing,* and then the names, one under another, page after page, each in a different hand, with a small mark beside each. A square with a line through it. Osric had thought it looked like a door. He hadn't said so. He'd asked what was closed, and Tench had said, "Yes," and taken the book back.

Now it lay under Tench's hands.

Above them, through the stone, something bellowed.

It was a long low sound, rising, and it came down the archive stair and through the door and along the vault so that the lamp's chain shivered. It stopped. Then it came again, nearer.

"That'll be Dun," said Tench.

Footsteps on the stair. Slow ones, and heavy. Behind them, further up, out in the yard, another voice, a young man's, pleading. "Come on. Come on, now. Come on, you great lump, he's only gone down the stair, he's not gone to Hallowmere…"

The door opened and Merrow came in.

He had to duck. He was nearly as tall as Osric and broader, with a face the colour of a brick and grey hair cut short as stubble, and he brought the cold in with him, and the smell of the Long Barn. He looked back up the stair as he came.

"Leave him, lad," he called. "He'll stand. He'll stand if he can hear me."

"He won't go in the barn, sir…"

"No. He won't. Leave him be and get to your bed, Grieve." He shut the door on the voice and turned round and looked at the room, and at Tench, and at Osric, standing by the table in his other shirt.

"Sit down," Merrow said. "You look like a heron."

Osric sat.

Merrow pulled out the chair across the table from him, the one with its back to the door, and lowered himself into it, and put his big red hands flat on the wood, and looked at Osric for a while without saying anything. Up in the yard the ox bellowed once more, and then went quiet, as if somebody had told it to.

"My wife thinks I'm on the night gate," said Merrow. "So I'll not be long."

---

"In the old days," said Merrow, "before the orders had a name."

He said it as some men say grace, with his eyes on his hands. Osric opened his mouth.

"Which old days?"

Merrow looked up.

"There's no date," Osric said. "There isn't for anything before the charter. Not here, not at Hallowmere. The record-house has the king lists back to the charter and before that it's songs. If you mean before the charter you'd have to say which…"

"Tench," said Merrow, without looking away from him, "is he always like this?"

"Yes," said Tench.

"Right." Merrow turned his hands over on the table. "In the old days. Before the orders had a name. Before Hearthmere was Hearthmere, near enough. There were men on this hill. The books call them knights, but they weren't knights, not like us. They were the first of us." He paused. "Will I go on, or do you want to tell me which year?"

"Go on."

"There was something under the hill. Asleep. A long way down. Something that had been put to sleep before there were men to write it down. And they found it." He looked at the table. "And they woke it."

"Why?"

"The books don't say. *For the realm's good,* they say. *For the keeping of the peace.*" Merrow shrugged. "I'm a farmer. I don't know why men do things. I know they woke it, and before it was awake enough to know what had been done to it, they chained it. And they built the inner wall over the way down. That's the old wall. The grey one. The one you face at noon."

Osric sat very still.

He didn't believe a word of it. He knew that clearly, sitting there. It was a story, and he had read a great many stories, and he knew the shape of them: the sleeper under the hill, the wakers, the chains, the wall. There was one like it in every kingdom. There was a cheese-paper in a box in the third stack with something very like it on it, in a tune every child on the river knew.

But he didn't say so. Because Merrow's hands were flat on the table, and Merrow's face was quite calm, and Tench at the head of the table had his own hands folded on the black book and wasn't looking at either of them. He was looking at the lamp. And Osric had stood behind his father's counter for fourteen years, and he knew what people looked like when they were telling you something they'd told before, and hadn't liked telling then either.

"The orders began as its wardens," Merrow said. "Everything else came after. The patrols, the river, the gate, the school. Me." He almost smiled. "Grew up round it like a farm round a well. And most of the farm's forgotten what's at the bottom of the well."

"What is?"

"The Watch has a name for it."

Osric waited.

"The Last Titan," said Merrow.

Osric laughed.

He didn't mean to. It came out of him short, through his nose, before he could stop it, and in the quiet of the archive it was very loud. Merrow didn't move. Tench didn't move.

"I'm sorry," said Osric. "It's… that's the lullaby. *The last great beast lay down below.* *Hush now, the last one's sleeping.* My mother sang it to me. Everybody's mother…"

"Aye," said Merrow. "Mine too."

"A lullaby isn't a source."

"No," said Merrow. "It's a lullaby."

---

He told him the rest.

He told it plainly, as a man reads out a list of stock, with no tune in it at all, and Osric listened and tried to keep his face still.

He told him about the lamps.

"That," said Merrow, and tipped his head back to look at the lamp over the table, white and steady with nothing in it to burn. "Where d'you think that gets its burning?"

"It's imbued. Crown's work, at Hallowmere. The licensing books say…"

"Imbued with what?"

Osric opened his mouth and found he didn't know. The licensing books said *by the crown's workers.* They said *according to the forms.* He had read them all and none of them said what the forms drew on, and he had never once noticed that they didn't.

"It comes up from below," said Merrow. "The lamps. The warming-stones in the infirmary. The words the surgeon's let say over a bone. Every lamp without oil from here to the southern sea. It comes up out of the stone, out of that." He didn't nod at the far end of the room. He didn't need to. "That's what the Watch is told, and I believe it."

"And the oath?"

"Ah," said Merrow. "The oath."

He put one big hand out flat on the table between them, palm down, as though he were laying it on something.

"Every voice that says it at the noon bell, facing east," he said, "is a hand on the chain. That's what the oldest books say. Every knight sworn holds a link. That's what it's *for.* It's not a promise, lad. It's a lock. *Keep the watch below.* That's the hand on the chain." He pressed his hand down on the table. "*And do not ask.* That's so the hand doesn't let go."

Osric looked at the hand.

"That's not how oaths work," he said.

"It's how this one does."

"An oath is a form of words. It binds the one who says it. It doesn't… it can't hold anything. Not iron. Not anything. There's no working in it, it isn't cut or drawn or said over anything, it's just *said.* By cooks. I heard a cook say it this noon with her mouth full."

"Aye," said Merrow. "And did you?"

"Did I what?"

"Say it."

"Of course I said it."

"Well, then," said Merrow, and took his hand off the table.

---

There was a silence. Up in the yard, very faintly, the ox shifted its feet on the frozen cobbles; they could hear it through the stone, a hollow knock, and then nothing.

"Nobody's seen it," said Merrow. "I'll tell you that now so you don't ask. Not in my time. Not in Tench's. Not in anybody's that's written down. The Watch goes down once. On the night it's sworn. You go down, and you see what there is to see, and you come back up, and you're Watch, and you never go down again unless you're the one holding the lantern for the next." He looked at Osric across the table. "I've held it twice. Tonight's three."

"And if I don't want to?"

He hadn't known he was going to say it. Merrow looked at him with something that might have been approval.

"Then you go up to your bed," he said, "and in the morning you go back to Hallowmere, with a good letter. Tench'll write it. And you never come down that stair again, and you never say a word about tonight to anybody, because nobody would believe you, and because if anybody did, you'd have to answer to me." He said it quite mildly. "Nobody makes you Watch. It's not that kind of thing."

Osric looked at Tench. Tench was looking at him now, with his pale clouded eyes, and his face had no expression in it at all. He looked like a man watching a binder sew a book he expected to come apart in a year, and who had decided not to say so, because it was the binder's book.

Osric thought of Hallowmere. The record-house. The copying room, and the head clerk's face, and the long street with the glue-pots smoking.

He thought that he didn't believe a word of it.

He thought that if he went up the stair now he would never know what was behind the door.

"I'll swear it," he said.

---

Tench took the long black key off his belt.

It took him a while. His fingers were stiff with the cold, and the ring was crowded, and the key was heavy, and Osric sat and watched him work it round the ring, key by key, until it came off with a small iron sound. Tench held it out across the table. Not to Osric. To Merrow.

"I'd come," said Tench, "but I'd not get back up."

"I know it."

"The year of the long frost."

"I know, Amos." Merrow took the key. He took the brass oil lamp off the end of the table, and a little shuttered lantern from the shelf by the door, and lit the lantern from the lamp with a spill, his big fingers careful, and stood up.

Osric stood up too.

"Bring nothing," said Tench. "Not a pen. Not a notebook. I know you, Hale."

Osric put the stub of pencil back on the table.

---

He'd never been past the last of the stacks. Not properly. Only that first evening, with the brass lamp, to read the ward. The air changed there. It was colder than the rest of the archive, and it smelled different: less of paper, more of stone, and of something under the stone that he couldn't name, a smell like the inside of a church in winter, old and cold and clean.

Merrow went down the three steps with the lantern and set it on the bottom one, to the side, so the light fell up across the door. The three iron bands threw three thin shadows. The hooked letters round the keyhole were only a ring of worn dents in the half-light, and Osric found himself reading them again, *turns a word aside,* as if they might have changed.

Merrow put the key in the lock.

It went in a long way. Further than Osric would have thought a key could go into a door. Merrow put both hands to the bow of it and turned, and the lock answered, like no lock Osric knew: a long, grinding, unwilling sound, iron on iron, and then the bolt went back with a crack like a stick broken across a knee.

Merrow took his hands off the key and left it in the lock.

"That's me done," he said. "I turn it. You go through. Going through's what does it. Not me. Not the key." He picked up the lantern. "You."

He pushed the door, and it swung inward onto the dark.

A breath came out of it.

Cold. That was the first thing. A breath of air out of the dark into Osric's face, cold as a cellar, colder, cold as water. And under the cold, so faint that he wasn't sure of it until it had gone, warmth. As if somewhere a long way below there was a room with a fire in it, and somebody had opened a door at the bottom of the house.

Beyond the door, a stair. Stone steps, going round and down into the dark.

It was a man's width. Barely. Osric looked at it and thought, absurdly, of the doors in the record-house that he'd stooped through for fourteen years, and of the knight on the gate. *Mind your head.*

"Go on, then," said Merrow behind him. "I'll light you."

Osric went through.

---

The stair went round and down.

He went first, with one hand on the wall, stooping, and Merrow came after with the lantern, so that Osric's own shadow went down the stair ahead of him, huge, bent, round the curve of the wall, always a few steps further on and always going down into the dark before he got there.

The steps were old. Cut in the rock, not built. Worn in the middle, every one, into a shallow dip, like the steps up to the chapel at Hallowmere where people had gone up to pray for three hundred years. He felt the dip with his feet. He thought: how many people? You'd need a great many feet to wear stone like this. You'd need more than a few hundred names in a book, each going down once.

He started counting the steps. He was a clerk. He counted things.

At a hundred the air had changed again. At two hundred the walls were wet, a cold sweat on the stone under his palm, and he thought of the river: the archive was under the tower, and the tower was on the hill, and the hill stood above the Wend, and by any arithmetic he knew they must be level with the river now, or under it. He thought he could smell it. Mud and cold. He thought of the whole wide brown slow Wend going over their heads in the dark, and how much it must weigh.

At three hundred he lost count.

He didn't know how. He was saying the numbers in his head, carefully, and then there was a step he wasn't sure of, whether he'd counted it, and he stopped, and Merrow stopped behind him, and the lantern-light swung and settled.

"All right?" said Merrow.

"I lost count."

"Aye," said Merrow. "Everybody does. Go on."

He went on. He didn't count again. The stair went round and down, longer than the hill could hold, he thought, longer than any hill. The wall under his hand changed. It had been wet and cold, and now it was dry, and he went on with his palm flat on it, and somewhere he noticed that it was less cold than it had been. Not warm. Only less cold, as the inside of a sleeve is less cold than the outside of it.

He took his hand away and went down without touching the wall.

---

Then the stair stopped.

It didn't come to a door. It came to nothing. The last step was there under his foot and then there was flat stone, a ledge, cut out of the rock, wide enough to stand on, and beyond the ledge the walls went away.

He felt them go. He couldn't see it. The lantern's light went out from the ledge into the dark and didn't come back off anything. No wall. No roof. Just dark, enormous, going away in every direction, and the sound of their breathing in it gone suddenly small, and a cold air moving on his face from somewhere a long way off.

Merrow came down the last step beside him.

"Stand still," he said. "Don't go near the edge till I've got the light out."

Osric stood still.

Merrow went past him, two paces, to the edge of the ledge, where the stone stopped. He opened the lantern's shutter all the way. He held it out at arm's length over the edge, into the dark.

Chain.

It came down out of the dark above them, past the ledge, close enough to touch, and went on down below the ledge into the dark under them, past where the lantern reached. Iron. Black. One link of it, the one in front of Osric's face, was taller than he was: an oval of iron as thick as his body, its inner curve higher than his head. The rust on it had gone hard and grey and cracked, like the bark of an old tree, and in the cracks it was black.

He thought: that's not real.

He thought it very clearly, looking at it. Iron didn't come that big. Nobody could forge it. There wasn't a smithy in Hearthmere with a hearth big enough to heat one link of it, nor a crane to lift it, nor a hammer to strike it. He'd read the guild rolls of every forge in the kingdom. It wasn't real.

Merrow moved the lantern, slowly, to the right.

Another chain. A little further off. The same. Coming down out of the dark above and going down into the dark below.

And another beside it.

Osric counted. He was a clerk. He counted things. Three. Four. Five, a long way off now, the lantern-light only just finding the curve of it. Six.

Merrow lowered the lantern.

The light went down. The six chains went up out of it into the dark above them, and the dark came in round the lantern, and Osric found that he had put out his hand, sideways, and found the rock of the wall behind him, and was holding on to it.

Then the dark below the ledge moved.

---

He didn't see anything. There wasn't anything to see. The lantern-light went a little way down the nearest chain, link under link, and below that was only dark. But the dark moved. Not the chain. The dark itself, far down, the whole of it, all at once, slow, heavy, as water heaves in the middle of a wide river at night when something big turns over under it.

And then he heard it breathe.

A long way down. Further down than the stair had come. It began so low that he thought, at first, it was the blood in his own ears. Then it went on. It went on and on. It went on longer than any breath he had ever heard, longer than he could have held his own, a long slow drawing-in of air through something so big that he couldn't think of the size of it; and while it went on, the chain in front of him moved.

All six. He saw the nearest one go. A hand's breadth. Up, against the rock of the ledge, with a sound of iron on stone like the grinding of the lock, but deeper, much deeper, a sound he felt in his chest and his teeth and the soles of his feet. And the ledge moved under him. The rock of the wall behind his hand moved. The whole hill, Greyhold, the archive, the tower, the walls, the yard above with an old grey ox in it, moved a hand's breadth on that breath, and settled.

The breath stopped.

The chain came down again, slowly, the hand's breadth, grinding.

Silence.

Osric stood on the ledge with his hand on the wall. He couldn't feel his hand. He couldn't feel his feet. He could hear his own heart, and Merrow breathing beside him, steady and slow, and nothing else at all, nothing below, nothing anywhere in all that dark.

Merrow didn't look at him. He stood at the edge of the ledge with the lantern down by his knee, looking at the chain.

"That's what you're keeping, lad," he said.

---

They went back up.

Merrow went first now, with the lantern, and Osric came after him, with Merrow's broad back in front of him and his own shadow behind him, going down the stair the other way, into the dark they'd left. He put his hand on the wall. Then he took it off.

He found, about a hundred steps up, that he was explaining it.

He didn't decide to. It started by itself, in the part of his head that read things and put them in order, and he let it, because it was something to do with his feet going up and up and his heart going too fast. Wind, he thought. The river runs over that place, or near it; there must be culverts, old ones, drains from the quarry days, Greyhold was a quarry once, everybody knew that. Wind in a culvert. The river pushing air ahead of it through a passage in the rock and letting it go. That would sound like breathing. It would sound like anything you liked, in the dark, if an old man had just told you a story.

And the chain. Iron settles. Iron moves in the cold; every smith knows it. The iron hoops on a cask creak in a frost. Chain that size, hung that long, in cold like that, would shift. It would shift on its own weight. You could read about it in any guild roll. The hill moving: the hill hadn't moved. His legs had. His knees had gone; he'd been frightened; a frightened man feels the ground move under him all the time. He'd read that too.

He had a footnote for all of it by the time the stair stopped going round.

He felt better. He felt almost himself. He went up the last of the steps behind Merrow with his hand off the wall and the cold coming down from the open door above to meet them, and the small square of yellow light that was the archive, and thought, quite clearly, that he would write it all down in the morning. Not tonight. In the morning, in plain words, and look at it in daylight, and see what it was.

Then he remembered that Tench had told him to bring nothing, and why.

---

Merrow stepped out through the door into the archive and set the lantern down on the bottom step, where it had been. He turned round.

The long black key was still in the lock. Merrow didn't take it out. He stood aside from it.

"You lock it," he said.

Osric looked at him.

"Your hand," said Merrow. "That's the way of it. I open it for you, you lock it after. It's turned once for each of us." He jerked his head at the key. "Go on. It won't bite."

Osric came out of the stair. He pulled the door to behind him; it was heavy, heavier than it looked, and swung shut slowly on its old hinges with a sound like a sigh, and the cold breath of the stair stopped on his face. He put his hand to the key.

It was cold. Colder than iron should be, even down here. He put both hands on the bow of it, as Merrow had, and turned.

It didn't want to. It was stiff, and it fought him, and he leaned on it, and felt it go, iron grinding on iron, slow, unwilling, and then the bolt went home with the same crack, a stick broken over a knee, and his hands came round with the key and stopped.

He stood there holding it.

It was done. He didn't feel anything. He'd thought, a little, he might. He had turned a key in a lock and that was all. He took it out. It came out a long way, as long as his forearm, heavy and plain, and he held it for a moment in both hands at the bottom of the three steps, and then he gave it to Merrow, and Merrow went up the room with it to Tench.

---

Tench had the black book open on the table at the list. There was a pen beside it, and a pot of ink, ordinary black ink from the store, and a little pot of sand.

"Sit," he said.

Osric sat. In the chair across from Tench's, where Merrow had sat, with his back to the door. Merrow stood behind him. Osric could feel him there, a big warm shape smelling of the Long Barn, not saying anything.

Tench turned the book round on the table and pushed it toward him with one dry finger and put his finger on the page.

The names went down it, one under another. All the hands of three hundred years. Some square and careful. Some quick and slanting. One so shaky it looked as if it had been written on a cart. One, a page back, that he'd always liked, a big round hand that took up two lines on its own, as if the person who'd written it had been proud of their name and wanted room. The ink of the oldest ones had gone brown. The ink of the newest was black. Beside every one, the same mark: a small square, with a line through it.

"Under the last," said Tench. "In the old hand. Your own name. And the mark."

"In the old script?"

"Every one's in the old script."

He knew that. He'd looked at the list in the summer. He'd wondered, then, why a list kept three hundred years after the old script went out of use should still be written in it, and had decided it was the kind of thing that grows up round an old place. Like a form of words said at noon.

He dipped the pen.

He'd written his name in the old script a hundred times, for practice, on scraps, in the margins of copying he shouldn't have been writing in the margins of. He'd never written it on anything that would last. His hand wasn't quite steady. He wrote it slowly: the square letters, the little hooks at the ends of the strokes, the old tongue's way of building it from the back so that you had to think of *Hale* before you could write *Osric*. Then, beside it, the mark. A square. A line through it.

Like a door, he thought.

He sanded it and blew on it. The ink went from wet to dull.

"There," said Tench. He didn't say anything else. He turned the book round toward himself again, and held it up to his face, two inches from the page, and looked at the new name through all three pairs of spectacles, and then put it down.

"Good hand," he said.

Above them, through the stone, the ox bellowed.

Merrow let out his breath.

"That's him," he said. "He'll have the whole school up." He put his hand on Osric's shoulder, once, heavily, and took it away. "I'd best go. My wife thinks I'm on the gate, and if the gate's heard Dun she'll know I'm not."

"Merrow," said Tench.

Merrow stopped.

"Thank you," said Tench.

"Aye," said Merrow. He looked down at Osric. His brick-red face was hard to read in the white light of the lamp. "You'll do," he said. "Get some sleep."

He went out. They heard him on the stair, slow and heavy, and then in the yard, his voice, low, and the ox's answer, a long rumble in the chest like a cart going over a bridge, and then their two sets of feet going away across the cobbles toward the Long Barn, together, the man's and the beast's, until there was nothing.

---

Tench sat with his hands on the closed book for a while.

"Well," he said at last.

Osric waited.

"You'll be wanting to know things," said Tench. "Everybody does. I did." He took off one pair of spectacles and folded it and put it in his coat. "Don't ask me tonight. I'm old and my knees hurt and I've sat in this chair since the last bell." He took the blanket off his knees and folded that too, slowly, corner to corner. "Put the book on its shelf when you're done with it. Don't read all night."

"I won't."

"You will," said Tench. "But put it on the shelf."

He got up. It took him a long time. Osric got up too, and Tench waved him off with one hand and went down the room to the door, one step at a time, and opened it, and stood in it a moment with his hand on the frame, under the carved line.

"Hale," he said.

"Yes, Warden?"

Tench seemed about to say something. Then he shook his head, and went out, and Osric heard him go up the stair, one step and then a rest and then the next, all the way to the top.

---

He sat down again.

The lamp burned over the table, white, steady, with nothing in it to burn. He looked up into it. *Where d'you think that gets its burning?* He looked at it until his eyes watered and there was a white blot on everything, and then he looked down at the black book.

He wouldn't read all night. He'd look at one thing.

He opened it at the front. Not at the list: before it. The list began a good way in, and he'd always known there were older pages ahead of it, and Tench had always turned past them. The vellum there was brown and soft as cloth, and the hands were strange, cramped and old, older than any hooked script in the record-house. He could read some of it. Not much. A word here and there, and where he could read, it was about a hill, and about a closing, and he went slowly, with his finger under the lines, leaning close to the page in the white light.

Then the pages stopped.

He turned the last of the old ones, and the next page was the first of the list, *Watch*, and the book went on as he knew it. But between them, as the leaf turned, he felt something in the gutter catch against his finger. He knew that feeling. He'd known it since before he could read.

He opened the book wide at that place, wider than Tench would have liked, so the old spine creaked, and bent his head over the gutter.

Stubs.

A row of them. Narrow brown strips of the old vellum, a finger wide, no more, standing up out of the binding with the thread still through them. Somebody had taken a knife to the pages there and cut them out close to the sewing. A sharp knife, and a careful hand; the edges were clean, cut in one stroke each, not sawn, not torn. Somebody who knew how a book is put together. Somebody who knew that you can't get a sewn page out without unpicking the whole book, and so hadn't tried, and had cut it as close to the thread as a knife would go, and left the stubs, because they had to be left.

*A cut shows.*

He held the book open with one hand flat across the bottom of the gutter, as he'd have held a ledger open on his father's bench, and bent down close over the stubs in the white light, and read what there was to read.

There wasn't much. At the edge of each stub, where the knife had gone a hair's breadth wide of the margin, a few letters were left. The ends of lines. The tail of a stroke, a hook, half a square. On one, three letters together that might have been the back of a word, if he'd known which word. On another, the curl of a large initial, cut through the middle. He went along them one by one. He knew the old tongue built its words from the back, and he tried every word he knew that might end so, and they all fitted, and none of them meant anything.

He sat back.

He knew exactly what he would ask. It was there in his mouth, whole, a sentence set in his head before he'd written it down. *Who cut them?* And after that, *When?* And after that, *What was on them?* He'd ask Tench in the morning. Tench would say *yes*, or *cellar*, or threaten to send him back to Hallowmere with a note, and he'd keep at him until he got it. He'd kept at the clerks in the record-house for years. He'd kept at his father, at nine, until his father told him what had been on the page with the debt on it, and whose debt, and why.

He sat with the book open in front of him and the question in his mouth.

He thought of the ledge.

He thought of the dark, moving, far down, slow and heavy and all at once. Of a breath that went on longer than he could have held his own. Of six chains going up a hand's breadth against the rock with a sound he had felt in his teeth, and the hill going with them, and his own hand on the wall.

Wind in a culvert. Iron settling in the cold.

If he asked, Tench might tell him.

He found that he had thought that, and he sat very still with it. If he asked, and Tench told him, then it would be written. Not in the book. In him. He'd have it, as he had everything he'd ever read, the king lists and the horses' names and the guild rolls of every forge in Hearthmere, and he wouldn't be able to unpick it. And the wind in the culvert would be gone, and the iron in the cold.

*And do not ask.*

He'd said it at noon. He'd said it every noon for six months, at the top of the archive stair, squinting, thinking about his supper. He'd said it tonight, too, in a way. He'd turned a key.

*That's so the hand doesn't let go.*

He closed the book.

He didn't do it quickly. He laid the old leaves down one on another over the stubs, and the list over them, and shut the cover, and squared it on the table with both hands, and sat with his hands on it as Tench had sat. Then he got up and put it on its shelf.

In the morning, when Tench came down the stair one step at a time and lowered himself into the archivist's chair and put on all three pairs of spectacles, Osric would look at him across the table with the question ready, and open his mouth, and ask him whether the lamp wanted trimming.

And Tench would look up at the lamp, and then at him, for a long moment, with his pale clouded eyes.

"It never has," Tench would say.

But that was the morning.

---

It was very late. The stacks went away into the dark past the table, and the archive was so quiet that he could hear the lamp: not a hiss, as an oil lamp hisses, but something below hearing, a kind of steadiness, as if the light were being held up from underneath.

He should go to bed. He picked up his pencil off the table and put it in his pocket, and went to fetch the brass lamp, and saw that the light at the far end of the room was still burning.

Merrow had left his lantern.

It stood on the bottom of the three steps where he'd set it down, with its shutter half open, throwing its yellow light up across the black door. The three bands. The plate. The worn ring of letters round the keyhole.

Osric went down the room to get it.

He meant to. He went past the last of the stacks, into the colder air, onto the bare swept floor, and down the three steps, and bent to pick up the lantern, and didn't. He straightened up instead, and stood at the foot of the steps, and looked at the door.

It was only a door. That was the thing. Black oak and iron. He'd seen a hundred doors in the record-house older than this one. It was a door, and behind it there was a stair, and at the bottom of the stair there was a ledge, and some very large old iron, and a draught. He had a footnote for all of it. He'd be fine in the morning.

He thought he'd like to know that it was a door.

He put his hand on it.

He put it flat, palm and fingers, on the old wood between the second band and the third, beside the lock, as he'd have laid a hand on a book's board to feel whether the damp had got in.

It was warm.

He hadn't expected that. He'd felt the cold of the stair come through it when it was open. He'd felt the key, colder than iron. He'd thought the wood would be cold as the stone round it. Instead it was warm all through, with a slow dry steady warmth that came up into his palm out of the grain. Warm as his father's glue-pot on the hob in the mornings, when it had been left there banked all night and you put your hand to the side of it before the fire was even lit.

And then something else.

He couldn't have said what. Afterwards, for forty years, he never found a word for it in any book, and he looked. There was no voice. Nothing came into his hand or up his arm. It was nothing like the knights in the yard with their hounds, who turned their heads together at the same sound. There was nothing between him and it, no thread, nothing at all.

Only, through his palm, very faintly, far off, a long way down, something noticed him.

Something very large. So large that he was nothing to it. He was a mark in a margin. He was one letter, in one line, on one page, in the whole of a library that went down further than the hill could hold. And somewhere, down there in the dark, the reader of that library had stopped, with its eye on that one letter. Slowly. Without hurry. As though it had all the time there was.

He couldn't take his hand away.

He stood at the foot of the three steps with his palm flat on the warm wood and his heart not going at all, and it was still there, the attention, the eye on the page, and it wasn't leaving. It was coming closer. Not up the stair. Nothing was coming up the stair. Only the attention itself, turning, slow as a tide, toward a hand on a door.

Behind him, on the archive stair, there were footsteps.

He heard the door open at the top of the room and somebody come in, a big man, heavy, fast. He heard the feet come down the long room between the stacks. He couldn't turn round.

A hand closed on his wrist.

It was a farmer's hand, broad and hard, and it closed on him so tight that the bones of his wrist ground together, and it pulled. His palm came off the door. The warmth came off it with a sound, or he thought there was a sound, like a page torn out close to the thread. He stumbled back against the step with his arm up and Merrow's hand still locked on it.

Merrow's face was grey. Not brick-red. Grey, all through, like the stones of the inner wall. Osric had never seen a man's face that colour. He'd never seen Merrow afraid. He'd only known him six months, and only to nod to, but he'd have said that the big grey knight with the ox and the laugh you could hear from the river wall didn't have it in him.

He did. It was all over his face. And it wasn't for himself.

He didn't let go.

"Don't," said Merrow. His voice had gone very low. "Don't you ever. Don't you ever put your hand on that door again."
