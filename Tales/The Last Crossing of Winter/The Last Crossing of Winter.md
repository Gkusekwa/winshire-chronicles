---

Ferry people have rules, and most of them are older than the people who keep them.

Liss Ashdown had hers from her mother, who had them from hers, on the same landing under the same grey wall, and none of them had ever been written down, because nobody writes down a thing they say every day. You put your hand on the rope before you do anything else. You let the passengers say their words over the water, because it does them good and does the river no harm, and you don't tell them the words have gone downstream with the weed before they're out of their mouths. You count them on and you count them off. A child in arms counts. If the count is short at the far side you don't tie up, you go back, and you don't go home until you've found what you dropped.

And you don't take the boat out on a dark night, not for any money.

That was the only one her mother had ever given a reason for, and she hadn't given much of one. Liss had asked at nine or ten, sitting on the jetty with her feet over the water, and her mother had looked at the river a while and said, *Because you can't see what's under you.* That was all. Years later Liss had said the same words to her own girl on the same jetty and been looked at in the same way, and had felt the rule go down the family like rope paid out through a ring, without anybody deciding it.

She had kept the rules thirty years. She had kept them through floods and two fevers and a husband buried with his hound at the end of the long field above the river. She had broken the last one in the thaw, more nights than she liked to think about, for the Garrows and the Pennicks and half of Harrow's End coming down to the Staithe with their bundles in the only dark there was, because the dark was the only time it was safe to come. She'd gone out for them on the rope with her eyes shut, since they were no use open. She'd come back and been sick off the end of the jetty, and told nobody, and gone out again the next night.

The river hadn't minded. The river had carried worse. That was what she told people, laughing. She didn't tell them she'd lain awake after, every one of those nights, listening to it go by under the house, the whole black weight of it, and thinking about what it had on its floor.

---

On the night Millbrook burned she stood on the stone jetty with a lantern and watched it.

Two leagues downriver the sky was red from the bank to the clouds. The river carried the red up to her, a long broken road of it across the water, so that the ferry rocked against its fenders in light the colour of a forge. Now and then something went up down there bigger than the rest, a barn or the mill or the Kettle's old thatch, and the road of light on the water jumped and shook and settled again. The wind had gone round at dusk and was bringing the smoke upriver. It smelled of wet straw and pitch and something else she didn't want to name.

Behind her the red door stood open. In it, with the firelight at her back, a thin girl with a great deal of hair.

"Is that the whole of it?" said Wren.

"Not the whole. The jetty road."

"Is your Tam in it?"

"Go in," said Liss. "Go in and mind the fire."

Wren didn't go in. She hadn't gone in anywhere she was told to since noon. She'd gone into Greyhold through the gap in the gate with her grandmother and come out again at dusk by the water-gate, with a groom holding her by the wrist as if she might bite. *She'll not stop in, mistress. She says she wants you. She says her gran said she might.* And Liss had looked down at the child on the bottom step of the water-gate stair, with her chin up and her bundle in her arms, and had found that she had no argument in her at all, not one, and had taken her hand.

So there were two of them on the landing, watching Millbrook go.

Liss had been on her feet since before dawn. She'd seen the camp at the meadows go up from this jetty with the first grey, and had the ferry off its rope and down the river in the slack under the bank before she'd thought about it, poling, and got who she could. Bel's two, crying. A woman with a burned arm. A man carrying another man. Then the road, and the gate, and the crush, and Ada coming through it like a barge through reeds, and her own girl in her shirt with a dead knight's sword in her hands. Then the water-gate all morning, in and out, under the wall, till her arms didn't belong to her. Then Pell. Then Tam.

*Stay here. I'll bring him.*

She hadn't stayed by the gate. The gate was shut, and wanted nothing from her. She'd gone home and put the kettle on, and been out on the jetty since dark. Nobody had told her to stay anywhere since Maren was twelve, and that had been Maren too, on this jetty, about a heron's nest.

The lantern was getting heavy. She shifted it to the other hand.

Then she saw them.

A small boat coming up out of the smoke under the bank, slow, close in where the current was slackest, with somebody standing in the stern poling, and something long and flat lying across the middle. Three of them. And a door.

She put the lantern down on the stones and ran.

---

She didn't remember the jetty, after. She remembered Tam's coat under her hands, wet through and stinking of smoke, and the bones of his back, which had got bigger since she'd last held him and hadn't got any older. He put his face down in her shoulder. He cried as he'd cried at six over a dead duckling, with his whole chest, and she held on to him with both hands and said nothing, because there was nothing in the world to say to it.

Over his shoulder she looked at her daughter.

Maren stood on the jetty with the punt's rope in one hand and the sword in the other and her father's brown cloak black from top to bottom. Her face was black too, except where her eyes had run. She looked at her mother, and Liss looked back, and Maren nodded once.

That was the whole of what was said. It was enough. It was more than Liss had been sure of an hour since.

Behind her, at the red door, Wren spoke up before anybody could ask.

"I wouldn't stay in," she said. "Inside the walls. With the knights. I wanted Mistress Ashdown. Grandma said go if I liked. She's with Aunt Bel and the greys." She looked at the door in the punt, and the man on it. "Is he dead?"

"No," said Maren.

"Good," said Wren, and stood aside to let them carry him in.

It took the three of them, and Wren holding the lantern. Odo Marl was the biggest man on the river and he lay on the door like a felled oak, with his burned arms crossed on his chest and his legs wrong below the knee, and he didn't make a sound the whole way up the steps. At the red door they had to tip him to get the door through the door. Liss had her hands under the head end and her face close to his, and so she was looking straight at him when the lamp went.

The old lamp over the red door. The one her grandmother had paid the lamp-tender for, that had hung dark on its hook since midwinter. It lit. White, all at once, so white it was like having a hand put over your eyes, and Odo shut his on the door and turned his face away from it. Then it was dark again.

Nobody said anything. They carried him in.

---

Later, when Odo was on the settle with a blanket over him and Tam on the floor beside him with his back against it, and Wren asleep at last in the net-loft, Liss sat down by the fire and took her daughter's hands.

Maren let her. That was how Liss knew how bad they were.

She unwound the rags. The palms under them were raw from the heel to the fingers, the skin gone white and wet and lifting. *A beam,* Maren said. *Hot. I didn't let go.* As if she were reporting the weather.

Liss looked at them. And because her mother had said it over every burned hand that ever came into this kitchen, and her grandmother before that, and because she'd said it herself over Tam's fingers and Maren's knees and every passenger's child who ever put a hand on a hot pot on this landing, she said the burn-word over them. The river one. Low, out of habit, as you'd say *mind the step.* It hadn't done anything since the turn of the year. Nothing had.

It took.

It took so hard that Maren cried out, and Liss felt it go. Out of her. Out of her own hands into her daughter's, a heat leaving her as fast as water out of a holed bucket, and under her fingers the raw skin drawing together, tight and shiny, in a breath, as if a week had gone by in it. Maren's hands jerked in hers. Liss let go of them as if they'd bitten her.

She sat and looked at her own.

They were white to the second knuckle. Cold. They ached as they ached after a winter morning on the ice with the rope, an ache all the way up into the wrists. She turned them over. They were her mother's hands, broad and spotted, with the rope-calluses gone yellow across the palms, and they had never in her life done anything she hadn't asked them to.

"That's not right," said Liss.

Nobody answered her. Odo, on the settle, had his eyes open, and was looking at the dark lamp-hook over the door.

---

Maren went up to the gate in the morning with the sword in her belt. Liss watched her go up the landing and didn't ask what for. Then she went up into the net-loft for laths and came down and set Odo's legs.

He let her. He lay on the settle and looked at the rafters and let her pull, and the only sound out of him was his breath going in and out through his teeth. When she'd bound the laths on with strips of old sail, he looked down the length of himself at the two stiff bundles and said, "There's dies under that beam."

"There's what?"

"Dies. Mine. Under the beam, at the back, where Tam couldn't shift it." He shut his eyes. "There's a man in Millbrook would have them out and sold by noon, if he's not burned. Little crossed hammers. Somebody'll want them."

"Somebody can want," said Liss.

"Somebody did," said Odo, and turned his face to the wall.

Tam sat on the floor by the settle all morning with his knees up and his hands hanging between them, and didn't eat, and didn't talk. On the third finger of his right hand there was a ring. Iron, small, plain, the kind a boy beats out for himself on a slack afternoon. He'd worn it on a string round his neck all winter, under his shirt, and thought his mother hadn't seen. She had seen. She saw it now on his hand and said nothing about it, and put bread by him, and took it away again at noon, and put more.

Maren came back after the bell.

She came in at the red door with her wrapped hands and the smoke still in her hair, and stood in the middle of the kitchen as if she'd forgotten what kitchens were for. Liss had seen that look on Edric, the days he came off the river watch after a drowning. She put the kettle on.

"They won't open," said Maren.

"No."

"I shouted it. Up at the wall. Somebody looked." She made a short sound that wasn't a laugh. "A second-year with a marten. He went away."

"They've a lot to look at," said Liss.

Maren took hold of her arm. Her wrapped hand was hot through the sleeve.

"The water-gate," she said. "Below the ferry jetty. Where you were taking them in yesterday. Is it still open?"

"For me," said Liss. "They know my boat."

"Then go to it. Today. Ask for Hale. The archivist. Warden Osric Hale. Nobody else. Tell him Callis has the seal. Tell him tonight, in the third hour, the river gate." Her hand tightened. "Tell him I said. Tell him I heard it from Tobin. Tell him I'm sorry I didn't come myself. They won't let me in."

Liss looked at her.

She thought: you're sorry. You were born in that room. You had the fever in that bed. And they won't let you in at a door you can see from my window, and you're sorry.

"And you?" she said.

"I'm going back to the knowe."

"Now?"

"Tonight." Maren looked west, through the wall, through the hill, at something Liss couldn't see. "It wouldn't come down with me. Yesterday. It's been up there on its own for two days." She stopped. She started again, and couldn't, and Liss watched her try as she'd watched her try at seven to say who had let the pig out. "I need to be with it. Tonight. I don't know why. I just do."

There were a good many things Liss could have said. She'd had a husband who needed to be with a hound, who went where it went and stopped when it stopped, and when poachers killed it he'd sat down by this fire and been dead within the week, with not a mark on him. *One life between two,* the knights said, as if it were a thing to be proud of. She'd buried the two of them in one hole at the end of the long field. She'd stood at the graveside with a girl of fourteen holding her hand, and thought: never again. Never in this house.

She looked at the girl holding her arm now, who was twenty-seven, and burned, and had sat by this fire all night with a dragon's scale in her lap.

She nodded.

"Wren," she said, without turning round. "Get her some bread."

She took the ferry off the rope after noon.

The rings at either end of the boat opened on iron pins as long as her hand, and the pins were stiff, because she took them out twice in a good year. She knocked them free with the heel of the mallet and lifted the rings off the rope and let the rope fall back into the water, and the river took the boat, gently, as it takes anything that isn't held. She poled it down twenty lengths under the wall, in the slack, to the foot of the water-gate stair.

It wasn't much of a stair. A dozen worn steps going up the foot of the river wall out of the water, green below the flood-line, to an arch the height of a man with an iron grille across it. Behind the grille, in the dark, the long low stores where they'd put what was left of the camp from the meadows. She'd carried a good many of those people in here herself, yesterday. She could smell them through the bars: wet wool and smoke and too many bodies, and somewhere a child being sick.

She made the ferry fast to the ring at the bottom step and went up and put her face to the grille.

"Hale," she said. "Warden Hale. Osric Hale."

A groom came. Old, bent, a lantern in his hand, a face she knew from the landing. Joby Marsh, who'd kept the servants' gate since before Maren was born, and bought eels off her every autumn, and always argued the weight.

"Mistress Ashdown."

"I want the archivist."

"He's in his books, mistress. He doesn't come down."

"Then go up and fetch him."

Joby went. He came back after a while without anybody. He stood on the other side of the grille with the lantern and didn't look at her.

"I went to the Captain," he said. "The Captain's on the river gate."

"Then tell the Captain."

"She's on the gate with Sir Callis, mistress." Joby looked at his feet. "I'll not say that name to her face, not with him stood there beside her. Fifteen years he's been on that gate. You know what she'd say. She'd say who told you, and I'd say Liss Ashdown off the ferry, and she'd say who told her, and I'd say her girl." He looked up. "And then where'd we be."

Liss held the bars.

"Then go and tell the archivist," she said.

"I did. He's not in the archive. Nobody knows where he is."

"Then I'll wait."

She waited. She sat on the top step with her back against the wall and her hands in her sleeves and the ferry knocking on the steps below her, and every time a face came to the grille she stood up and said the name. A student. A cook's girl. A knight she didn't know, who told her to go home, mother, it was no place for her. Joby again, twice, with nothing. She said it to all of them. *Osric Hale.* She said it the length and breadth of the afternoon until it had no more sense in it than a gull's cry, and some of the people in the stores came to the grille to look at the madwoman on the steps, and went away again.

Nobody believed her. Nobody even asked what she wanted him for.

The light went.

From the top of the water-gate stair you could see the River Road where it came round the foot of Greyhold's hill and went west. As the light went, a figure went up it. A woman, alone, walking fast, in a long brown cloak that had been a man's. She didn't look back at the river gate as she passed under it. She looked back once, further on, at the landing.

Liss turned her head.

On the landing, in the red door of the ferry house, Wren lifted her hand.

The figure on the road went on west. Liss watched it till the dusk took it. She didn't call out. It was too far, and she had nothing to call that the girl didn't know, and she was hoarse.

She turned back to the grille and put her face to it, and said, "Osric Hale," one more time, to nobody.

"Yes," said a voice.

---

He came down out of the dark of the stores with a stick, slowly, an old man, broad, bigger than she'd thought, with a white beard spread on his chest. She'd seen him go up and down the landing for twenty years and never heard him speak. He put one big hand on the bars and leaned on them, and got his breath.

"Nobody shouts for me by name," he said. "Joby found me. He said there was a woman on the water-gate stair calling it out like the price of eels." He looked at her through the grille. "You're the ferry."

"I'm her mother."

He was quiet a moment. "Ah," said Osric Hale.

So she told him. She told it as Maren had told it to her, word for word, as you tell the price of a crossing. *Callis. The seal. Tonight. The river gate.* The third hour. From Tobin, at the barricade at the foot of the jetty road, with the town burning. And she was sorry she hadn't come herself, but they wouldn't let her in.

He listened to all of it with his hand on the bars. He didn't say *are you sure*, or *who's Tobin*, or *Callis has been on that gate fifteen years.* When she'd finished he stood a while, looking past her at the river.

"Sorry," he said at last. "She would be. She'd apologise to the weather, that one."

Then he straightened up off the bars.

"Go home, Mistress Ashdown," he said. "Bar your door."

"What will you do?"

"Tell the Captain." He was already turning. "She'll not believe me either. But I'm a Warden, and it's my door they want, and she'll have to listen to me say it." He looked back once, from the dark. "Tell her I said she was right to send you. Tell her that, if I don't."

She heard his stick go away up the stores, slow, one step and the next, until she couldn't hear it.

---

The horns came while she was pinning the ferry back on the rope.

Upriver. A long way. Thin and frantic, the river watch's horns from Redwatch, three leagues up, and under them a long roar, rolling down the water toward her in the dark, like a barn going up. She looked up from the pins.

Over the island there was fire.

It came down out of the sky onto the place where the Old Ruin was. A long tongue of it, white at the root and orange-red at the end, so bright that the whole reach of the river lit up to her feet and showed her the rope lying across the water, every strand. Then it went out. Then another, from somewhere else, deeper, slower, gold and bronze, like the mouth of a bread oven when the door comes off, rolling over the trees and the stump of the tower in a great slow wave.

On the end of the jetty Wren was standing with her bundle in her arms.

"That's Kharrak," she said. "The red. The white at the start, that's him." Another light came down. "And that's Aurveth. That's gold." She watched. "There's two."

"There's two."

"There's three," said Wren. "There's Lisska." She watched the island a while longer, the colours coming and going over it, red, gold, red. "She's not there."

"She'll be somewhere."

"She can see a sparrow on a roof a league off," said Wren, as if that settled where she was.

Above them, behind the wall, a horn blew. Then hooves on the cobbles of the yard, a great many, and the river gate groaned open above the landing, and the riders came out. Knights on greyharts, on horses, with hounds loping at their stirrups, out of the gate and down onto the River Road and away north at a gallop toward the fire and the horns, so close above the landing that the jetty shook and the dust came down off the wall into the river. Liss saw them go by in the lantern-light at the gate. At the head of them, small and very straight on a tall grey, Rowan.

The gate shut behind them.

On the wall over it, in the light of the gate-lantern, one knight stood and watched them go, with his arm in splints and a sling.

Liss looked up at him from the jetty for as long as it took to tie the last knot.

---

She went in and told Tam.

He was where he'd been all day, on the floor by the settle. Odo was awake above him, looking at the door.

"You'll take him down to Millbrook," she said. "Now. In the punt."

Tam looked up.

"There's nothing in Millbrook," he said.

"There's the mill-house, if it's standing, and Peg Dowe's, and the Penners'. There's people. There's no gate." She was already getting the blankets. "And there's his dies under his beam, he tells me, and a man who'd sell them by noon."

"Ma…"

"They're opening that gate tonight," said Liss. "Up there. Over my house." She put the blankets in his arms. "I'll not have a man who can't stand on my settle when they do it. Nor you on my floor."

"And you?"

"I've a boat to mind."

He stood there with the blankets. He looked at her, and at Odo, and at the door. He opened his mouth and shut it again. He'd always done that, since he was small, when there were too many things to say and they'd all got stuck in the door together. She waited.

"I could stop," he said. "I could stop here, with you. I've a ring." He held his hand up. The little iron ring. "They'd not touch the house if I was in it."

She took his hand and looked at the ring.

"Keep it on," she said.

He stared at her.

"Keep it on in Millbrook," said Liss. "It'll get you through the jetty road. It'll get him through." She turned his hand over and closed his fingers on it, and gave it back to him. "And when you're through, you can do what you like with it. Throw it in the river with his hammers, if you want. But you'll keep it on till he's in a bed."

On the settle, Odo laughed.

It was the first time she'd heard it since he came. Nothing like the old bellows laugh you could hear from the Millbrook jetty across the water on a still day. Small and dry, and it hurt him. But a laugh.

"Your mother," Odo said to the rafters, "would have made a smith."

They carried him down to the punt on the door. Tam poled out into the dark under the bank and the current took them, and Liss stood on the jetty and watched until there was nothing on the water but the red of Millbrook's last embers down at the end of it.

---

"You'll go over to Greta's," she said to Wren. "On the boat. Now, while it's quiet."

Wren was sitting on the end of the jetty with her legs over the water, looking upriver at the island, where the fire had stopped coming down. She didn't turn round.

"No," she said.

"It's dry over there. There's eels. Greta's a terrible woman but she'll not let anything happen to you, she'd never hear the end of it."

"If I'm over there," said Wren, "Papa won't know where I am."

Liss looked at the back of her head.

Fisk's boy had come over the night before last, in his eel-boat, rowing like a mad thing, and stood in this kitchen dripping, fourteen and all elbows, and said it. *He's gone to the Ruin. On his own. He's left the dragons. My da took him over in the dark.* Liss had carried it down to the meadows at first light with the boat, into the smoke, and the child had had it out of her before she was over the side, and had asked, very calmly, which channel.

The island sat out there in the dark past the bend, three leagues up. Liss had looked at it every morning of her life and never once gone near it, and she had a husband in the ground who'd lain on its spit all night and heard it breathing.

"He'll know where you are," she said. "I'll tell him."

"You don't know him."

"I've bought his barley twenty years."

"That's not knowing him," said Wren. "That's barley."

Liss stood on the jetty with nothing to say to that, which hadn't happened to her often in fifty-three years. She thought of another girl who'd once stood on this jetty at twelve and told her mother to stay where she was, because of a heron's nest, and been obeyed.

She went in and lit her mother's lamp, the old one with the brass foot that you lit with a spill like anybody, and set it in the window. Then she came out and sat down on the jetty beside the child, and waited for the third hour.

---

She kept time by the bread.

She'd set it at dusk, out of habit, as she set it every night of her life, and it rose as it always rose, slow under its cloth on the table by the window, and by the time it had come up over the rim of the bowl it was the third hour. It always was. Her mother had kept time the same way and been right to the bell for forty years.

The bread came up over the rim.

The night was black. There was cloud, and under the cloud the smoke from Millbrook lying along the river, and whatever moon there was didn't come through either of them. The only lights were the lamps on Greyhold's wall, and those were dead. Up at the gate, a lantern. In her window, her mother's lamp. Out on the water, nothing.

She heard the boats before she saw them.

Poles. Not oars. She'd have known the difference asleep. The soft drop of a pole-foot into mud, and the wet suck as it came up, and the hush of a hull going along close under the bank where the water was deep, as Tolly had heard a barge go the night Upper Rush burned, all those months ago, with no light on her. One boat. Then the next, and the next, so many poles together that it sounded like rain starting.

They came up out of the dark from downriver, from Millbrook, close in under the wall. No lights. Long low shapes on the water, one behind the other, full of men. They went past the end of her jetty so close she could have leaned out and touched the gunwale of the first. Nobody in it looked at her. They ran their bows up on the shingle below the landing and on her slip, one after another, with a long grinding of keels, and the men came out of them over the bows into the shallows and up onto the landing, quietly, a great many men, in grey, with spears, with the ring of six on their coats.

Wren had her hand in Liss's sleeve.

Up at the river gate the lantern moved.

The little door in the great oak leaf, the wicket, opened inward, and light came out of it, and in the light a man stood. A knight, in mail, with one arm strapped across his chest in splints, holding the wicket open with the other. He stood aside. The men in grey went past him into the gate, two by two, fast, bent over, and he held the door for them as a good servant holds a door for guests.

Then he went in after them, and the great leaves of the gate began to swing.

"That's him," said Wren, very low. "Isn't it. The one."

Liss didn't answer.

The bell began.

Up on the north tower, fast, wild, the bell that gets men out of their beds. And under it, the noise. Iron on iron in the gateway. Shouting. Then a scream that wasn't a man's, huge, raw, going up and up, and she knew it. She'd heard it in the mornings across the landing for ten years, when Marta Thorne took her boar out to the river to drink. It went on, and on, and stopped.

The bell rang a while longer.

Then it stopped in the middle of a stroke. Not run down. Stopped, as if somebody had put a hand on it.

In the quiet after it she could hear the river.

---

They came down the water-gate stair a quarter of an hour after.

She heard the grille first, the scream of its bar drawn back. Then feet on the steps. Then Joby Marsh's voice in the dark, low and fast, *go on, go on, mind it, it's wet,* and the people from the stores coming down past him out of the arch onto the stair and along the foot of the wall toward the jetty in a long stumbling line, as many as the stair would hold. Children carried. Bundles. A woman with a bread-trough on her head, of all things. They came along the shingle under the wall in the dark, past the boats the men in grey had left, and onto her jetty.

The men in grey had left some of their own with the boats. A dozen, perhaps, on the shingle by the slip, round a hooded lantern. They stood up when they saw the line come along the wall. One of them came across the stones toward the jetty.

Liss got up.

She knew the walk before she knew the face. Long, loose, a little pigeon-toed. Peg Dowe's youngest, Peg who mended nets on the Millbrook jetty and had mended Liss's for twenty years and always charged too little. Merl. He'd been a fat baby. He'd got thin. He had a spear and a ring on his finger and a face like a boy who's been told to stand at a door and hasn't been told what to do if anybody comes to it.

"Mistress Ashdown," he said.

"Merl Dowe," said Liss. "Does your mother know you're out?"

He stopped on the shingle at the foot of the jetty steps. The line of people from the stores stopped behind her on the jetty and waited, packed in along it, breathing.

"There's nobody to cross," said Merl. "Nobody's to cross. We're to hold the landing."

"Then hold it," said Liss. "Nobody's taking it off you. I'm taking these off it." She turned her back on him. That was the trick with boys, and with beasts, and with most men: you turned your back on them as if it had never crossed your mind they'd do anything else but let you. "All of you. In. Little ones in the middle, where it's dry. Mind the step. Mind the rail, it's loose at the far end, I've been meaning to see to it since the Widow's Frost."

They got in.

She didn't look round to see what Merl Dowe was doing. She heard him not do anything. She heard him stand on the shingle a long while, and then his feet going away across the stones toward the others round the lantern.

Wren was in the stern before Liss had told her to be. She sat on the eel box with her bundle on her knees and her feet tucked up out of the bilge, and looked at the people coming in over the side as a farmer's child looks at stock coming into a pen: interested, not much surprised.

"You'll sit there," said Liss, "and you'll not stand up, and you'll not put your hand over the side."

"I know."

"All here?" said Liss. To the boat. To the dark. It was what her mother had said, at every landing, every crossing, every day of her life, standing in the stern with her hand up on the rope: *All here?* As if anybody ever said no.

Nobody said no.

She took the slip-knot off the post and put her hands on the rope, and hauled.

---

She went by feel.

You can't see what's under you. She couldn't see what was over her, or beside her either. She had the rope in her hands, wet, cold, thick as her wrist, coming up out of the dark ahead and going down into it behind, and the rings knocking on it at the bow and the stern, and the weight of the boat and twenty-three people and a bread-trough leaning back against her arms as the current took them. That was the whole of the world. Hand over hand. The rope was the only thing she could see, and she saw it with her palms.

Behind her the landing was a few dim shapes and the hooded lantern by the slip. Above it, the wall, black. Above that, the yard, with its noise and its red glow, and the gate standing open with light coming out of it.

They were a long bowshot out when the lamps lit.

All along Greyhold's river wall at once, the old lamps on their iron brackets that hadn't shown a light since the ninetieth night. White. Hard white, from one end of the wall to the other, so bright that the river round the ferry went from black to the colour of milk in a breath and every face in the boat came up out of the dark at her, white, with its mouth open.

Then dark.

Then white again. Then dark. Like a pulse in a wrist when you've a finger on it.

And on the shingle by the slip, somebody shouted, and something went past her head with a sound like a thrown stick.

She knew it. Every river woman on the Wend knew that sound, from the old smuggling days and from the knights' practice at the butts in the summer, when a bolt went wide over the landing and buried itself in the turf of her roof. It went past her ear and into the water on the far side with a small flat *chock*.

"Down," said Liss. "All of you. Down in the bottom. Heads down."

They went down. The woman with the bread-trough went down with it on top of her like a lid.

The lamps went white. Another bolt. It hit the stern board a foot from Wren's knee and stood there, humming.

Then from above, from the river tower, a crossbow answered. Not at her. Down, at the shingle, at the men round the hooded lantern, who'd stood up to shoot at the light on the water. She heard a man scream on the stones. Then the lamps went dark, and all of it stopped, and there was only the river and the rope and her own breath.

She hauled.

Every time the lamps came on she was the brightest thing on the Wend. She knew it. A flat boat full of people on a white river, in the middle of a bowshot, between men on a tower and men on a beach who'd both have liked to see what was coming across the water and neither of whom much cared. Bolts don't come with a name on. She'd told the Garrows that, in the thaw. She'd been joking then.

She hauled. The lamps went white. She hauled in the white and in the dark the same, because there was nothing else to do with the time, and her shoulders burned, and the rope went through her hands, and behind her a child was saying something under its breath very fast, over and over, and an old man at the rail was saying the Saltreach word for safe water with his eyes shut. She let him. It went downriver before it was out of his mouth. There was no call to tell him so.

The Staithe came up out of the dark.

---

Greta was on the timber jetty with a lantern and her arms folded.

"Liss Ashdown," she said, "what in the gods' name are you bringing me?"

"Hearthmere," said Liss. She threw her the bow-line. "The bits that'll fit."

"I've no room."

"You've a smoke-house. Put them in with the eels. They'll not know the difference by morning, and nor will you."

Greta caught the line and made it fast without looking, and looked at the people climbing up out of the bottom of the ferry with their bundles, and the bread-trough, and the bolt in the stern board by the child's knee. She stopped looking as if anything owed her money. She held the lantern up so they could see the step.

Liss stood at the end of the boat as they went past her onto the jetty, and counted.

She did it as her mother had. A hand on each shoulder as it went by, a touch, no more, so you counted by feel and couldn't be cheated by the dark. With her other hand she took the stub of chalk out of the tin in the stern locker and made a stroke on the stern board for each one. Her mother had chalked the fares there, and rubbed them off at the week's end, and the board was grey to the grain with forty years of strokes. Liss took no fares from people who had nothing. She'd chalked them anyway, all winter. It was what the board was for.

Twenty-two. Twenty-three. A child in arms counts.

"Twenty-three," said Wren, behind her.

Liss turned round.

"I counted," said Wren. "From here. Twenty-three." She thought about it. "Twenty-three."

"That's right."

"I know it is," said Wren. "I said it again so you'd know I knew."

Liss looked at her. Then she looked at the bolt standing in the stern board, and put her hand on it, and worked it out of the wood, and dropped it over the side.

"All here," she said, to nobody, and took the boat back.

---

She went back for them eight more times.

She learned the night as she'd learned the river, by going over it. She learned that the lamps on the wall came white in runs, three or four together, and then gave her a long dark, and that if she hauled hard in the dark and stopped in the white and lay down along the stern with her hand on the rope, the boat lay low and grey on the water and the bolts went over. She learned that the men on the shingle were not soldiers, most of them, and couldn't shoot, and that the men on the tower could, and were shooting at the men on the shingle, and that the ferry was between them every crossing, and neither side would stop on her account.

She learned, coming back on the second crossing, that the men in grey had been in her kitchen. The red door was standing open and the bread was gone off the table, bowl and cloth and all. They'd left her mother's lamp burning in the window.

"They might have left the heel," said Liss.

"They're hungry," said Wren.

"I'm hungry."

"You're not," said Wren. "You haven't eaten anything since yesterday. I watched. You're tired. That's different."

On the third crossing a stone came off the river tower.

She heard the engine before she saw anything. A long creak from the top of the tower, and a thump like a door slammed in a cellar, and then nothing, and then the river beside the ferry stood up. A great white wall of it, a length off the bow, that went up into the white light of the lamps and hung there and came down on them. It filled the boat to the ankles. A woman screamed. The rope jumped in Liss's hands as if somebody had struck it with a stick and something went *crack* along it, close, by the bow ring.

They'd meant it for the boats on the shingle. It had fallen short.

In the next white she saw a man in the middle of the boat sitting up with his hand clamped on his shoulder and blood coming out between the fingers. Gil Mossop, the Millbrook cooper, who'd once sold her a water butt that leaked from the first day. A bolt had done it, not the stone: one from the shingle, shot at the light, that had found the one man in the bottom of the boat who'd sat up to look.

"I'm all right," he said. "I'm all right, Liss, it's nothing, it's my arm."

"It's your shoulder."

"It's nothing."

"Lie down," said Liss, "and hold it, and don't argue with me on my own boat," and she went forward along the rail to the bow, because of the crack she'd heard.

The rope was cut half through. Bolt or stone, she never knew which. A hand's length of it beside the bow ring had been chewed, the strands sprung out of it like a broom, and the good strands left were singing under the current. She put her hand on it and felt it give.

She'd spliced that rope more times than she'd ever counted. There wasn't an inch of the first rope left in it. You couldn't splice in the dark in the middle of the river with your hands like ice, but you could bind. She pulled the scarf off her head. Her hair came down round her face, grey and wet, in the way. She wound the scarf round and round the cut, tight, tighter, and knotted it, and wound the rest over the knot, and knotted that. It would hold. It would hold for a night. It was a good scarf. Ada Fenn had brought it her from the Cotter's Cross fair in the year of the long drought, with a pot of honey wrapped in it, and she'd worn it every day since.

She went back to the stern and took hold of the rope above the scarf and hauled.

At the Staithe she put Gil Mossop down on Greta's floor by the smoke-house fire and looked at his shoulder. The bolt had gone in under the collarbone and out at the back, clean, and he'd been lucky, and he'd go on being lucky if it didn't turn. She had her hands on it, on the hot wet skin. The burn-word was in her mouth. It had been in her mouth since the stone. It was the word for burns, not for holes, but it closed skin, and it had closed Maren's in a breath.

She looked at her hands. They were still white at the knuckles from last night.

She didn't say it.

"Greta," she said. "You'll have honey."

"I'll have honey when it's paid for."

"Then put it on the board," said Liss, "with the rest."

Greta brought it. It was Ada's, as it turned out, a crock of last spring's that Greta had bought off Ada at the Staithe and never paid for either. Liss laid it into the wound with her fingers, thick and gold, front and back, and bound it with a strip of Greta's apron, which Greta tore off herself without being asked.

---

Mother Ketch wouldn't get in.

She was on the jetty at the fourth crossing, at the head of the line, with the people from the stores pressed up behind her. Eighty, and bent nearly double, from the lower lane at Millbrook where the river-flats began. Liss had known her by sight all her life and had never once carried her. Mother Ketch had never crossed. Nobody on the lower lane crossed. They went down to the water every day of their lives and fished it and washed in it and drowned in it, and never once went over.

"Get in, mother," said Liss. "You'll be over in a quarter of an hour."

"I'll not," said Mother Ketch.

"There's nothing over there but Greta, and she only wants paying."

"Your river took my man," said Mother Ketch. "And my two boys. Off the flats, one winter. I'll not go on it." She turned round, slowly, and began to push back along the jetty through the people waiting, who parted for her. "I'll go in the stone."

"Mother…"

"I'll go in the stone," said the old woman, and went. Along the shingle under the wall, small and bent, in the dark, back up the water-gate stair into the arch.

Liss watched her go.

*Your river.* As if she'd made it. As if she'd poured it out of a jug. She had carried two hundred people across it since the autumn, more, three hundred, and never once in thirty years had she thought of it as hers, any more than you think the road is yours because you walk on it.

"All here?" she said, to the boat, and hauled.

---

It was the fifth hour when the lion came.

She was on the jetty, loading. The lamps were dark. Wren was standing on the eel box in the stern, which she'd been told not to, looking up the landing at the gate.

"Mistress Ashdown," said Wren. "Look."

Up on the River Road where it came round the foot of the hill, out of the fields, something was coming. Fast. Low and long, the colour of old stone in the light that came out of the open gate, with a great dark ruff of mane. It went up the road below the hill in long flat strides that ate the ground like the river eats a bank in flood, without seeming to hurry, and the dust didn't have time to rise behind it.

It was the size of her ferry. Bigger.

There was somebody on its neck.

Lying flat along it, with her face in the mane and her elbows in and her knees locked, as nobody would ride a horse. A woman. Her hair tied back tight off her face as the river watch tied it. Liss knew the shape of that head against the light as she knew the shape of the rope in the dark. She'd washed that hair. She'd cut it with the eel-knife on this jetty when it got too long, with the girl wriggling.

The lion went up the road to the gate and in, over something lying across the threshold that Liss couldn't make out, and the light from the yard took it, and it was gone.

All the noise in the yard stopped.

All of it, at once. The iron and the shouting and the screams. As if a lid had been put on a pot. For the space of three breaths there was nothing from behind the wall but the crackle of the fires, and the river going by under the jetty.

Then out of the dark over the landing, so low that every head on the jetty went down, came something green. Long and slender, with its wings spread as wide as a barn roof, screaming, a thin high furious sound like a kettle left on the fire. It went over the wall into the light of the yard and the yard broke open in noise again, worse than before.

"*Lisska,*" said Wren.

She was up on the gunwale. Liss got her by the back of the coat and pulled her down onto the eel box and held her there.

"Let me go."

"No."

"That's Lisska. That's Lisska, and the lion-woman. She went to get her. She went to get her, that's where she was." Wren's voice had gone up into a squeak. "I *said* she'd be somewhere."

"You did."

"Let me go."

"No," said Liss.

Under her feet the stones of the jetty moved.

Not much. A shrug. As the ice shrugs on the first night of the thaw, before it goes out, a long slow lift under you and then down. Up in the yard something made a sound like a quarry, stone on stone, grinding, and a great many men shouted all at once in a way that she didn't know and didn't want to. Then the lamps on the wall went white, all together, and dark, and white.

She found she had let go of Wren and taken three steps up the landing.

She hadn't decided to. Her feet had. They'd gone up the jetty and onto the stones of the landing toward the gate, toward the light, after the shape on the lion's neck, before she'd had a thought in her head. She stood on the landing in the dark with her hair down and the people from the stores behind her on the jetty, and looked up at the open gate.

It was forty paces. Up the slope, past the boats on the shingle and the men round the lantern, and in.

And then what. And then stand in the yard of Greyhold at the fifth hour of the night with her bare hands, an old woman off the ferry, among three hundred men with spears and the knights fighting them, and a lion the size of a barn, and a dragon. And shout. She'd been shouting all afternoon.

Behind her, on the jetty, somebody said, "Mistress Ashdown?"

A woman, with a child on her hip. One of the camp. Liss had carried her over from the Staithe in the autumn, the Dunns, it would be. She was standing at the end of the jetty in the dark with the child and looking at Liss's back, not saying anything else, not asking. There were sixteen more of them behind her, waiting for the boat.

Liss turned round and went back down to the rope.

---

Joby Marsh's grandson came down the water-gate stair a little before midnight.

He came running along the shingle under the wall, a skinny boy of eleven with a man's coat down to his ankles, and out onto the jetty while she was handing the last of the sixth load in, and stood in front of her panting.

"Grandad said to tell the ferry," he said. "He said you'd want to know. About the lady."

Liss held the rope.

"Go on."

"She came in on the lion. Right in. Over Captain Thorne and her boar in the gate. They're dead." He swallowed. "And everybody stopped. And the stones came up out of the yard by the well like another lion, all made of stones, and stood in front of the barn, and the men daren't. And the Long Barn's held. Grieve's on the cart with a fork and there's a grey horse in the gap, a big one, a dray, and nobody'll go past him." He was talking very fast. "And the lion went across to the north tower. To the stair. The Haverly one was on the stair with his falcon, holding it. He's dead. They're both dead. And the lady went down."

"Down."

"Under. Down the stair, under the tower, where the old door is, where Warden Hale keeps his books. And the lion couldn't fit, Grandad says, it's only a man's door, so it put its shoulder to the wall and the wall *opened*. The stone. All round the door. Like a mouth. Grandad saw it from the barn door." He looked at her. "And they went down. Her and the lion. Under. There's a stair going down under, and they went down it. And the green one's lying over the top of the stair with its wings out like a roof and won't let anybody near."

Liss stood on the jetty with the rope in her hands.

"Down to what?" she said.

"I don't know," said the boy. "Grandad doesn't know. Nobody knows what's down there." He hesitated. "Warden Hale's not come up."

She looked at the wall. At the black bulk of the hill above it, Greyhold's hill, the grey stones and the old grey inner wall that they all faced at noon. *Keep the watch below.* Three hundred voices every day of her life, coming down over the landing at the noon bell while she was tying up or splitting eels or putting the bread in. She'd never once thought about what the words were for. You didn't. They were the school's words, like the bell was the school's bell, and you set your bread by them.

Below. Under the yard. Under the hill. Under the river, for all she knew.

She thought of Edric on the spit in the fog, with the water going by on both sides, hearing something breathe in the trees. *Liss, there's something on that island that isn't finished.*

"You'll get in the boat," she said to the boy. "You'll go over to the Staithe with the rest."

"No, mistress. Grandad needs me."

"Then go back to him," said Liss. "Go on. Mind the steps."

He went. She watched him run back along the shingle under the wall and up the water-gate stair into the arch, and the grille clang behind him.

"All here?" said Wren, from the stern.

Liss looked down at her.

"All here," said Liss, and took the knot off the post.

---

She was on the stone jetty, coming in from the sixth crossing, when the hill turned over.

That was what it felt like. She was standing on the end of the jetty with her hand on the post and the bow-line in her other hand, and the stones under her feet turned. Not up. Round. A long grinding under her, deep down, so deep it came up through her heels and her knees and into her teeth: stone on stone, a great flat weight going round on another, slow, as a quern goes round when somebody leans on the handle.

It stopped.

In the same breath, from far off, upriver, through the ground, she felt it again. Fainter. The same turn.

The people on the jetty had gone still. The men round the lantern on the shingle had stood up. Somewhere in the yard above, someone was crying in a high thin voice, a man.

Then upriver the noise came.

It came down the river at her from three leagues off, past the bend, in the dark. Not a crack and not a roar. A long falling, going on and on, like the river going out when the ice breaks, only all in one place, and getting louder, and going down. She'd heard the river go out a dozen times in her life. *You'll be asleep,* she'd told her children, *and it'll wake you, and you'll lie in the dark and think the world's ending.* This went on longer than the river ever had. It went down and down. And then, at the end of it, a long way off, a great soft sound like a sack of meal dropped on a floor.

Then nothing.

Up at Redwatch the lamps on the tower were lit. She could see them from here, three leagues upriver: a cluster of white points standing out of the dark on the bank, very bright, and not going out. They'd come on in the noise and stayed on. Steady. Burning white as she'd never seen them burn. Between Redwatch and the bend, on the dark water where the island sat, where it had sat every night of her life, there was nothing.

She couldn't see the island. She could never see it at night. But there was a darkness there that was a different dark.

"Mistress Ashdown," said Wren. Small, in the stern. "What was that?"

Liss stood on the jetty with the bow-line in her hand.

"I don't know, love," she said.

She'd never said that to a child in her life. Not to Tam, not to Maren, not to any passenger's child who'd ever asked her why the river was brown or where the eels went in winter. You said something. You said a story. Her mother had always had a story.

She hadn't got one.

---

After that the ground kept moving.

Not the turning. Something else, deeper, and further off, under the river or under the hill or under both, she couldn't have said. A shock. A long falling thunder through the rock, that she felt in the soles of her feet and in the boards of the boat and in the rope, which hummed after it, end to end, as if a giant had plucked it. Then quiet, a long quiet, long enough to load a boat and haul it over. Then another.

She counted them.

She'd always counted. Her husband had laughed at her for it. Eel boxes, steps, strokes of the pole, the bells, the people going past her into the boat. *If you hear it breathing,* she'd told Maren, the day they sent her to the island, *you lie still, and you count.* You counted because counting was something to do with your mind while your body did the rest, and because a thing you'd counted was a thing you knew the size of.

She counted the shocks. The first came as she took the seventh load out, halfway over, and the second as she bumped the Staithe jetty, so hard that Greta's lantern fell off its nail. The third and the fourth came on the eighth, one going over and one coming back, empty, in the dark, with Wren asleep under her coat in the stern. The fifth came while she sat in the boat at the stone jetty in the small hours, waiting for the last of them to make up their minds.

The sixth came a little before first grey.

She waited for the seventh. It didn't come. After the sixth there was a stillness in the ground that she'd never felt, a stillness all the way down, as if the whole hill under her had stopped holding its breath. She stood up in the boat with her hand on the post and felt it, and couldn't have told anybody what it was she felt.

Six.

The ring of six on the coats of the men on her shingle. She didn't know what she was counting. She knew she'd counted it.

---

The river was wrong.

She'd felt it since the falling, and not believed it, as you don't believe your own hand when it tells you the bread's cold. The current had gone slack. On the seventh crossing the rope had been easier in her hands than it had any right to be in the spring with the snow-water coming down. On the eighth it was easier still. The boat didn't lean back on her when she hauled. It lay on the water as if it were a pond.

In the small hours, waiting, she looked down from the boat at the steps of the jetty, and counted them.

Two more than there should have been.

Two steps showing that had been under the river since the thaw. Green, wet, with the weed lying flat and shining on them. And below them the river, black, lower than she'd seen it in spring in thirty years. Going down while she watched. She could see it go. The waterline creeping down the third step a finger's width at a time.

And the rubbish on it was going the wrong way.

A spar went by under the jetty. A wisp of burned thatch. An eel-box lid, somebody's, floating. Upriver. Slowly, turning, against all sense, past the end of her jetty toward the bend and the island and the dark where the island had been. The river was running backward. Not fast. But it was.

Wren had woken up. She was sitting up in the stern under Liss's coat with her hair on end, looking at the eel-box lid go by.

"The river's going backwards," she said.

"It isn't."

Wren looked at her.

"It is," said Liss.

She sat in the stern and waited on a river that didn't push at all, that lay under the boat as still and black as a cellar floor, with the rope going out across it from the post into the dark. The rope was the only thing on the Wend that night that behaved itself.

---

The last crossing went out at first grey.

The last of the stores. Fourteen of them, the ones who'd waited till the end because they were old or slow or had been sure till the last minute that they wouldn't go. And Merl Dowe.

He came across the shingle from the boats as she was loading. There was nobody round the lantern now. The men who'd been there were gone, some up into the gate, some lying on the stones where the tower had found them, one or two off along the River Road in the dark toward Millbrook without their spears. Merl had his spear still. He stood at the foot of the jetty steps with it and looked at the boat.

"Is there room," he said.

Liss looked at him.

He was very young in the grey light. Younger than Tam. He had the ring on his finger and a cut on his cheek and his mother's long upper lip, and he was shaking.

"There's always room," said Liss. "Leave the spear."

He laid it down on the jetty, carefully, as if it might go off. He got in. He sat in the bottom of the boat with his knees up, among the people from the stores, who looked at him and at the ring and didn't move away, because there wasn't room to.

Halfway across he took the ring off. He sat turning it in his fingers. Then he reached up and put it on the stern board, by her hand, among the chalk strokes, and didn't look at her.

She didn't pick it up. She didn't knock it off either. She left it where it was.

At the Staithe the timber jetty stood up out of the water on its legs higher than she'd ever seen it, with the mud showing black round its feet and a stink coming off it of things that hadn't seen air since before Tolly's grandfather. She had to hand the old ones up. Greta came down and took them one by one. Up at the top of the jetty old Tolly was sitting on his upturned boat by the weirs, as he always sat, with his pipe, looking at the river.

He looked at it a long time. At the mud. At the weed hanging off his weir-stakes, a yard clear of the water, dripping. At the eel-traps on their ropes, high and dry, with the eels in them thrashing in the air.

He took his pipe out of his mouth.

"River's lower than it was in my grandfather's day," said Tolly.

"It is now," said Greta.

---

She was halfway back, empty, when the sun came up.

There was nobody in the boat but her and the child, and the child was asleep again, under her coat on the eel box. Liss had stopped hauling. She didn't remember stopping. She was standing in the stern with both hands on the rope and her head turned upriver, and the boat lay still on the slack water in the middle of the Wend, between the two banks, with the light coming.

Past the bend, three leagues up, where the island had sat in the river all her life like a ship gone aground in another century, there was a hole in the river.

She could see that much from here. She'd always been able to see the island from the middle of the ferry: the line of the trees, the stump of the tower, the white water at the south end where the current split. There were no trees. There was no tower. Where they had been, the river went down. Steam came up out of the place in long grey columns into the grey sky, and spray, and under the spray the whole brown Wend was pouring in from both sides, from upriver and down, and falling, and going round. She could hear it, faint, three leagues off. A great slow roar, like a mill-race heard from the next valley.

That was where it had all gone. Her river. Down into whatever was under the island.

Then it stopped going.

She saw the spray settle. She saw the steam go thin. And then the place where the island had been lifted, all of it at once, slow, the whole width of the river humping up, brown and slow, like dough rising under a cloth, as wide as the Staithe was long.

And something came up out of it.

She would never get it right, after. She tried, once or twice, at her own table, and gave it up. A head, first, with the river falling off it in sheets, and the shape of the head was the shape of the beast she'd seen go in at the gate with her daughter on its neck, if you made that beast a hill. Then shoulders. A mane the water ran out of as out of a thatch in a cloudburst. A back, grey, laid in plates like a slate roof. It came up and up out of the water past the bend until it stood in the river where the island had been, as tall as Greyhold's hill, with the Wend breaking white round its legs as it breaks round Tolly's weir-stakes.

The ground moved under the river.

She felt it come. A long slow heave, out from the thing in the water in a ring, as the rings go out when a pike takes a fly. It came down the river toward her under the water. She saw it come: the whole surface of the Wend lifting in one long grey swell, from bank to bank. It came under the boat and lifted it, gently, as you'd lift a child out of a bath, and put it down again. The rope came up out of the water from end to end, the whole long bowshot of it from the post on her jetty to the post at the Staithe, streaming, and hung in the air in a long dripping curve, and fell back.

Above the landing, inside the walls, something cracked.

It sounded like a tree coming down in a hard frost, only it went on. A long splitting groan, up on the hill, and then a crash that went on and on, and dust going up out of the middle of Greyhold into the dawn, grey on grey. *Keep the watch below.* She didn't look. She'd see it soon enough.

It opened its wings.

Grey. From where she stood they covered the river from bank to bank and the fields on either side. They came up out of the water and spread, and their shadow lay down across the whole reach past the bend, and then it went up. Slowly. With the river falling off it in a curtain. Up over the Saltreach bank, over the hill at Harrow's End, over the Fellwood, going north-east into the sunrise, with something trailing from its legs that glinted. Getting smaller. Smaller than a haystack. Smaller than a gull. Then she couldn't find it.

On Greyhold's wall, the lamps went out.

They'd been white all night, on and off, on and off. Now they went dark, every one at once, and stayed dark. On the river tower. Up at Redwatch, three leagues off, the white cluster that had burned since the falling: gone. Over the red door of the ferry house, the old lamp her grandmother had paid for: dark. All of it. Every light in Hearthmere that you didn't have to light yourself.

In the ferry-house window a small yellow light was still burning. Her mother's lamp, with the brass foot, that you lit with a spill like anybody.

---

The river came back.

She felt it come under the boat before she saw it. A push at the stern. The current, finding its way again, out of the hole past the bend and down, the right way, toward Millbrook and the sea, slow at first and then stronger, until the ferry leaned back on the rope as it ought to, and the rope came taut in her hands, and the scarf she'd tied round the cut creaked and held.

Upriver, where the island had been, the water was going round and round in a slow brown eddy, carrying things. Reeds. A whole tree, with its roots in the air.

Liss sat down on the stern board.

The child was asleep beside her under the coat with her mouth open and her hair over her face. The sun was up over the Saltreach hills. On the far bank, at the Staithe, people were coming out of Greta's smoke-house to stand on the shingle and look upriver. On this bank, up on Greyhold's hill, she could see the dust still hanging over the walls, and nobody on the walls, and no lamp anywhere.

Her daughter was under that hill somewhere, or had been. Wren's father had been on that island, which wasn't there.

You count them on and you count them off. If the count's short you go back.

She put her finger on the stern board.

The chalk was grey on grey in the first light, and some of it had run where the stone's water came over, so that she had to go slow, and Merl Dowe's iron ring was lying on the fourth crossing. She moved it aside and found the first stroke, by the hinge of the locker, where she'd started.

She counted them. One stroke at a time, with her finger, moving her lips, as her mother had at the end of every week of her life before she rubbed them off. Twenty-three. Nineteen. Twenty-one, with Gil Mossop's among them, a little crooked, because the stone had come and the boat had jumped. Twenty-four. Eighteen. Twenty-two. Seventeen. Twenty. Fourteen.

A hundred and seventy-eight.

The river went by under the boat, brown, the right way, with the sun on it. The rope hummed in the rings.

She put her finger back on the first stroke, by the locker hinge, and began again from one.
