In the Wyldmarch they say that a Keeper owns nothing but a cord and a question. The cord is for tying the question into. The question is always the same one, asked of beasts and trees and water and stone in a hundred different words: will you? A Keeper who asks it well is answered. A Keeper who asks it badly is not, and goes home, and asks again tomorrow. There's no shame in it. The forest is old and it has been asked a great many things, and it is allowed to be deaf.

Hesper had asked it every morning for thirty years, and most mornings she had been half heard. That was the usual way. A finding-knot pulled a little, if you held it loose enough. A ward on a rail kept off the martens, mostly. A bear that didn't want to be moved could be asked to move and would think about it, and sometimes go. You lived inside the half. You learned to be glad of it. Her mother had said that the whole of being a Keeper was learning to be glad of the half, and the other half was patience, and that came to a whole if you didn't count too carefully.

For the last six days the forest had heard her every time.

She looked at the two red rings round her fingers while Elder Aun talked. They had gone yellow at the edges. The cord had done that, on the morning the grey went green: three turns, a twist, the word, and the knot had held, and then it had pulled, and kept pulling, until it was in her skin and she'd had to cut it off with her spear. Asked and answered and answered and answered. She thought of a child who'd been told to fetch water and wouldn't stop fetching it, and the floor of the house going under.

"Nobody will be told to go," said Aun.

Hesper said it after her, low, in the knight's ear.

---

The whole Moot had come down to the shingle to hear it. She'd never seen it like this, not in any year. Thousands, standing on the stones and the turf among the upturned boats, families together, beasts with them: wolves leaning on legs, elk with their heads up, a bear sitting in the shallows like a stone that had decided to be warm. Behind them the lake was flat grey. It had been flat since the lowlander came, as if it were listening too.

Aun stood in front of them all on the girl's arm. The girl was ten and had been leading Aun about since she was seven, and had a way of looking at people who came too close that Hesper liked. Aun's white eyes were on nothing. Her voice was dry as a seed-head and went all the way to the back.

"The lowlander has a glass," Aun said, and Hesper said it after her. "He says it tells him the ground is going to open. He walked two days to tell us."

The old woman turned her face toward where the lowlander stood, next to Hesper, with his box of glasses at his feet. She didn't see him. She found him anyway, as she found everyone, and he went pink to the ears.

Hesper didn't translate the pink. The knight could see it for herself. The knight stood very straight with the lion at her shoulder and her face shut. She stood at everything like that, as if she were on a wall somewhere being looked at by an officer. But the corner of her mouth moved.

"I believe he believes it," said Aun. "I don't know what his glass knows."

Nobody said anything. Hesper could hear the water touching the shingle, very small.

"So. Whoever believes the glass, take to the water. Today. Now." Aun's hand went up toward the lake and stayed there a moment, an old brown hand with the bones showing, and came down. "Whoever doesn't, stay. Nobody will think less of you either way." A breath. "The elders stay. Elders stay. That's what elders are for."

Hesper said it all, every word, a breath behind, in the knight's own tongue. It was a flat tongue, the lowlanders'. Everything in it lay down on the same level, like boards on a floor. You could say *the elders stay* in it and it sounded like *the bread's on the table*. She'd learned it from a pedlar out of Saltreach when she was a girl, and then from the smugglers, who were worse, and from talking to herself in it on the platform for twenty years because there was nobody else to talk to who'd understand it and she didn't want to lose it.

Aun turned round on the girl's arm and went back up the shingle, slowly, and the crowd opened for her.

"That's all?" said the lowlander.

"That's all," said Hesper.

"She didn't say what she thinks."

"She did. She said she's staying." Hesper picked up her spear. "Go and find a boat, glass-man. Find a river woman with a big boat and be very polite."

"I'm always polite," said the lowlander, worried, and she laughed, and the lion turned his great head at the sound and looked at her, and she bowed to him a little, because she always did. *Grandfather.* Every time she said it to him, in her head or out loud, something in her chest went over, like a stone going over in a stream when the water's high. She'd stopped expecting it to stop.

---

By noon half the Moot was out on the lake.

She went down to the shingle to watch, because she couldn't help it. The river clans went first. They'd come in boats and they knew boats. The round hide boats went out three and four at a time, light as baskets, with whole families folded into them, children in the middle and grandmothers at the back with paddles, steering as if they were stirring a pot. Then the horse clans' rafts, poles lashed with rawhide on the shingle by men who had never in their lives stood on anything that moved unless it had four legs under it. They pushed them out and stood on them holding each other's belts, very serious, with their red-clay bars on their cheeks, and the rafts tipped and they all leaned the other way at once, and on a boat nearby a river woman laughed so hard she dropped her paddle.

Then doors. Tent-poles bundled. Somebody's loom. A whole roundhouse roof lifted off its wall by forty people and turned over and walked down the shingle like a great grass beetle on its back, and floated, and the forty climbed in and sat down in it, and it turned slowly round once on the water and stopped.

They went out a bowshot and waited there. A long line of boats and rafts and roof and people the whole length of the Moot's front, on the still grey water.

She looked for the glass-man. He was sitting on a rock at the water's edge with his glasses set out in a row in front of him, and a river woman in a boat was telling him something sharp in her own tongue, and he was nodding politely and not moving. Well. He'd go when it mattered.

She looked at the half who'd stayed. The old, who wouldn't. The sick, who couldn't. The stubborn. The horse clans' riders, who wouldn't leave their elk, and the elk who wouldn't go near water deeper than their knees. The Keepers, who wouldn't leave their beasts, and the beasts who couldn't be put on a raft. And along the top of the shingle, the elders, in a row, sitting down with their backs to the lake and their faces to the forest. Aun, with her girl. Tolly-Bright, small and dark and for once not laughing. Morwen in the middle, Hesper's own, broad as a door, with the brown bear lying behind her like a hill that had come to sit.

Hesper went along the row to Morwen and squatted down in front of her.

"You'll be in the way here," she said, in their own tongue.

"I'm usually in the way," said Morwen. "That's what elders are for." She looked at Hesper's fingers. "Show me."

Hesper showed her. Morwen took the hand in both hers, which were warm and dry and enormous, and turned it over and looked at the rings, the yellow and the red.

"You'll go up the slope," Morwen said. It wasn't a question.

"Somebody will."

"Somebody will," Morwen agreed. She gave the hand back. "Your mother was the same. Stupid, and always going up the slope."

"She came down again."

"She did." Morwen looked at the forest over Hesper's head, the dark line of it at the top of the rise. "Mind you do."

---

In the afternoon she walked the Moot with the knight, to show her the shape of it.

The lion came too, a little behind, as he went everywhere. People got out of his way without seeming to know they were doing it. Children stopped and stared until somebody pulled them by the arm. He paid them no attention, and once, when a little boy didn't move, he stepped round him, carefully, as you'd step round a sleeping hen.

The knight looked at everything. It was her way. She looked at a slope as if she were counting it, and at a row of tents as if she might be asked later how many there'd been, and Hesper thought that somewhere a long way off in the lowlands, on some wall, some officer had once asked her exactly that and she'd known.

The north shore went up from the water in a long gentle rise of turf, half a league wide along the lake. The roundhouses stood on the lower part of it in their rows, thick near the water and thinning as the ground climbed, and the tents between and above them, and above the tents the turf went on rising, empty, a league from the shingle to the edge of the Thornwood at the top, where the forest stood along the whole sky in a black wall. The Moot had trodden lanes up the slope between its rows. Seven of them, broad as drove-roads, bare earth and flattened grass, worn there by a month of feet and hooves and every Moot before this one, going down from the edge of the trees to the edge of the water.

"Anything that comes out of the forest," Hesper said, "toward the water, comes down those."

The knight looked at her.

"You've seen it," the knight said.

"A fire," said Hesper. "When I was a girl. In the dry year. The Thornwood burned in the north and everything in it came south and came down here to the water, down the lanes, through the Moot. Deer, boar, wolves. Bears. All at once. We stood in the lanes."

She stopped. She hadn't meant to stop. It had come up into her mouth whole, as if it had been waiting there, and she found she was standing at the bottom of the third lane, where she'd stood that day, with her toes in the same dust.

She'd been nine. Her mother had stood across the third lane with a spear in one hand and the other hand held out flat, palm down, the Keepers' asking, as if she were pressing on the air to keep it from rising. Hesper had stood behind her mother's legs. She could still see the backs of them, the calves brown and scratched, and her mother's heels, which were cracked from the summer, and she'd held on to her mother's skirt with both fists, and the beasts had come down the lane. She remembered the noise more than the beasts. She remembered that her mother had sung under her breath the whole time, so low that only someone holding her skirt could have heard. *Hush, now. Hush. Like the six who sleep.* Over and over. To the beasts, Hesper had thought. Years later she'd understood her mother had been singing it to her.

"You stand in a lane," she said to the knight, "so the beasts go down it and not through the houses. That's all you can do. You can't stop them. You make them go where the houses aren't."

The knight nodded. She looked up the lane at the trees, and then back down it at the water, measuring.

The lion stood beside them with his nose a little lifted, smelling north.

*There,* said Hush, from somewhere behind Hesper's knees. She didn't need to look. Hush had been against the backs of her legs all day. *Under. Coming up. Like a mole under a bank.*

"I know," said Hesper.

The knight looked round at her.

"The cat," said Hesper. "She says the same as your glass-man. Without a box."

---

Hush had come out of the dark in her ninth winter alone.

Hesper had come back to the oak one evening in the first snow and there had been two lamps at the edge of the firelight, and she'd said what you say, *will you come to the fire*, and the lamps had stayed where they were. They stayed where they were every night until the thaw. Every night Hesper put a strip of meat on the snow halfway between, and every morning it was gone and there were prints round the place where it had been, and none nearer. In the spring she came down the ladder one morning and the duskcat was lying on the bottom rung with its chin on its paws, and it looked at her, and didn't move, and she understood that it had decided.

It was the quietest thing she had ever known. It made no sound walking, or eating, or landing from a branch, or washing. Even its breathing was quiet. So she called it what her mother used to say over cradles, and it seemed to agree, and after a while it began to talk to her. Not much. It had never been much of a talker.

*Here,* it said, when it came back from hunting. *Wet. Deer, old. Fox, young. The owl is cross.*

That was a long speech, for Hush.

They said in the forest clans that duskcats bonded to people who had been alone a long time, because they could tell when someone had got used to quiet and wouldn't fill it up. Hesper thought it was more that a duskcat had been alone a long time too, and didn't see why it should stop just because there were two of them. They had been alone together eleven years. It was the best company she'd had.

---

They set the lanes at dusk.

Nobody told anyone. Nobody needed to. The Moot did it as it would have for a fire, and Hesper watched it happen all along the slope with something in her throat that wasn't quite pride. The horse clans brought their tame stonehorns down, the herd they'd brought to trade, sixty of them: great grey plated beasts, short-sighted and short-tempered, as big as haywains, driven by riders on elk into the two western lanes and turned to face the forest. They stood three deep across the lanes, shoulder to shoulder, horns forward, and when they'd settled they put their heads down and grazed the bare earth as if there were anything on it.

The Keepers took the middle lanes. Wolves sat down in rows with their tongues out, looking north. Bears stood up. Hesper counted eleven, and one of them the great pale bear, which she'd seen once before, years ago, and which stood up in the third lane on its hind legs and was taller than the roundhouse beside it and looked at the forest as if it meant to have words with it.

The third lane. Her mother's lane.

On every ridge along the slope a duskcat went up and lay down for the night watch, black against the evening, lamp-eyes. There had been a duskcat on every ridge every night of every Moot for as long as there'd been a Moot. Hush watched them go up. Hush should have been on a ridge too. She looked at the nearest one and the nearest one looked back, and Hesper felt something go between them, not words, a long flat look of the kind cats give each other across a yard.

*Not tonight,* said Hush. *Tonight, the ground.*

The children who'd stayed and the old went down into the big roundhouses at the bottom of the slope by the water, the ones with stone footings. Hesper saw them go in. Small ones carried, bigger ones holding hands, two old women counting them in at each door and starting again when they lost count.

At the east end of the slope there were two lanes with nobody in them.

"Those," said the knight.

Hesper looked at her. She knew what was coming and wanted to hear it said anyway. That was the clans' way. You ask what you know, out loud, so it's been said.

"The near one," said the knight. "And the one past it. We'll take those."

She didn't look at the lion when she said it. She didn't have to. Hesper had watched the two of them for seventy days and she knew what it looked like now when they agreed: it looked like nothing. The knight said a thing and the lion was already standing where she meant.

Two lanes. One lion. Hesper looked at the knight's face and saw that the knight had counted it too and had decided not to say the number.

---

There was a seventh lane.

Past the two the knight had taken, at the very east end, where the Moot ran out against the forest. Not the main edge, which stood a league off at the top of the rise. Here the Thornwood came down to meet them. A long tongue of it, thorn and black pine and a few old oaks, ran down the slope from the edge nearly to the water, as if the forest had put out an arm to drink. The forest clans called it that. The Drinking Arm. Between the Arm and the last row of roundhouses there was the seventh lane: narrow, half a drove-road's width, the houses close on one side and the trees close on the other.

Anything running under the trees, anything that didn't like open grass, would keep to the Arm and come down inside it, and come out at the seventh lane first, before anywhere else. She'd known that since she was nine. In the dry year the first deer through the Moot had come out of the Arm.

"That one's mine," she said.

"Hesper..."

The knight said her name as if there were a great deal behind it. There probably was. Hesper didn't wait for the rest.

"It's the one nobody else wanted." She shrugged. "Somebody has to stand in it. I've stood in a lane before. Hush has."

She looked down at the cat. Ten summers back, at a Moot, a thunderstorm had gone over at midnight and put the horse clans' whole elk herd through the eastern lanes in the dark, and Hush had been a year bonded and had stood with her in the fifth lane with her ears flat and her tail like a bottle-brush and hadn't moved one paw. Afterwards she'd been sick behind a tent and wouldn't speak to Hesper for a day.

"She doesn't like it," Hesper said. "She'll do it."

Hush looked up at the knight with her lamp-eyes and didn't blink. Hesper felt the look go out of her, through the bond, and felt what was in it, which was not unfriendly. *The lion's one,* Hush said. *Iron and river. She has the lion's hair in her coat.*

She did. Hesper knew she did. The knot from the Ring, tied with a hair of the grey-maned's mane, in her inside pocket, where she'd put it on the eighty-fifth day with her left hand because she couldn't feel the right.

"The asking," said the knight. "Your knots. In the surge. They take too hard."

"I know."

Hesper looked north, at the dark line of the trees along the top of the rise, and the darker line of the Arm coming down beside her lane.

"I'll ask them anyway," she said. "That's what we do. You ask. If they hear you, they go round." She looked at the knight. "If they don't, they don't."

She hadn't said the other half. The knight had seen her fingers. The knight could work out the other half for herself: that they would hear her. That was the trouble now. For thirty years she'd been half heard, and she had made a whole life out of the half, and it had been a good life. Now there wasn't any half. Whatever she asked would go all the way in.

She turned and went up the seventh lane in the dusk, barefoot, with the cat behind her, toward the trees.

---

First she went to Aun's.

She'd nearly forgotten. She went back down the slope by the tents instead of the lane, so the knight wouldn't see her turn round and think it meant something, and found Aun's big roundhouse by the water with its door open and its fire low and nobody in it, because Aun was on the shingle with the others in their row.

She took the hide bag off her shoulder and hung it on the door-post, on the peg where Aun's girl hung the old woman's shawl. The moss in it, what was left. The green cord. Her flint and steel, for the days when nothing would light with a word. It wasn't anything. But the moss didn't like being stood on, and it didn't like being looked at, and a lane was a bad place for something that didn't like either.

She stood a moment with her hand on the bag.

She thought of the green dragon on the Plains, stretching her neck down so the bag could go round it, and sniffing Hesper's hair, and saying through the lion that it smelled of the woods. She thought of the lowlander captain on her pallet in the knights' tent, with the bone through her shin and her jaw like a trap, saying *yes* to a barefoot woman she'd been told her whole life was a savage. Four days ago that leg had begun to knit. It would be knitting now, somewhere in the Thornwood, under the moss, under a bandage nobody was allowed to look under.

The moss was the same moss. Her grandmother had gathered it from the same stones. It was the ground that had changed, and changed back, and might change again tomorrow and leave the moss only moss.

She took her hand away and went back up the slope.

---

The seventh lane had eleven roundhouses down its west side, and ten of them were empty.

She went into each one. She called at the door first, as you do, and then went in anyway. Cold hearths. A cradle-board left behind, which she picked up and put down again. In one, a dog asleep on a bed, which wouldn't get up for her, and which she asked, and which heard her all the way in and got up so fast it fell off the bed and ran out of the door and down the lane to the water without stopping, and she stood in the doorway with her hand over her mouth and couldn't decide whether to laugh.

In the eleventh, the third from the top, there was Sedge.

He was sitting on his own doorstep with a half-made basket on his knees. He was older than Aun, nearly, and had been making baskets at the Moot since before Hesper was born, and the whole Arm end of the slope smelled of his willow, soaking in tubs by his door.

"Go down, Uncle," she said.

"No," said Sedge.

"The lowlander's glass says the ground's going to open."

"Lowlanders have been saying the ground's going to open since I was a boy. Usually it's their mouths." He pulled a withy through and tapped it down. "I've stood in a lane. In the dry year. You were up your mother's skirt."

"I remember."

"So." He tapped the withy again. "I'll stand in this one. It's my lane. It's my house."

She sat down on his step beside him. Hush sat down in the lane in front of them and looked at Sedge without blinking, which was a thing duskcats did to people who were being stupid. Sedge looked back at her for a while and then went on with his basket.

"Uncle," said Hesper. "I've got the cat. I've got a spear. And I've got a lion two lanes over, with a lowland knight next to him, who'll come if I shout."

"A lion." Sedge snorted. "The grey-maned."

"You've seen him."

"I've seen him." He worked a while. "Your mother would have liked to see him."

"Yes."

"She said they'd come back. When I was a boy. She was a girl. She said one day they'd come back out of the west and we'd better be civil." He set the basket down. "Nobody listened to her either."

Hesper thought of Rhun. Of the Keepers' fire in the spring, the night after the great beast went over the Thornwood like a hill flying, when nobody had slept, and she'd dozed off at last against Rhun's shoulder, and woken with her heart going and said: a lion. Out of the west. A grey lion, and a woman on its back, and we should listen to her. And Rhun had looked at her and asked what she'd been putting in her pipe, and the others had laughed, and Hesper had laughed too, because she didn't smoke and Rhun knew it.

She'd got it wrong, a little. The woman didn't ride him. The woman walked beside him, with her hand on his shoulder, and sometimes with her hand on her sword, and never in front.

"If you stay," Hesper said to Sedge, "I'll have to stand in front of your door instead of in the lane. And the lane will go through somebody else's house. And they'll say Hesper of the Keepers let a lane go because an old man wouldn't put down a basket."

Sedge looked at her.

"That's cheap," he said.

"It is. My mother taught me."

He sat a while longer. Then he got up, slowly, his knees cracking, and picked up the half-made basket and went inside and came out again with a bundle of withies under his arm and his good knife in his belt, and pulled his door shut behind him.

"I'm taking the willow," he said.

"Take the willow."

"I'll be back for the rest." He went off down the lane toward the water with his withies, and stopped after ten paces and turned round. "Hesper."

"Uncle."

"Mind the Arm. There's that bull in there."

"I know," she said. "I'll ask him nicely."

---

The light went off the lake and the stars came.

She sat down in the lane at the top end, where the roundhouses stopped and the turf went on up the slope toward the forest, with her back against Sedge's willow-tub and her spear across her knees. The Arm was a black wall on her right. The roofs ran down on her left to the water. On every ridge a duskcat, lamps in the dark, all down the slope, like a row of lanterns somebody had hung along the roofs and forgotten to light the bottom of.

Hush lay across her feet. That was where Hush lay when she was worried. When she wasn't worried she lay a body's length off, where she could see everything.

*Under,* Hush said. *Coming up.*

"I know."

*You keep saying.*

"You keep telling me."

The cat put her chin down on Hesper's ankle and was quiet.

Far down the slope she could see a stub of candle at the water's edge, on a rock, and two shapes beside it. The glass-man, with his glasses. And the knight. The knight had gone down to him in the middle of the night, Hesper thought, because she couldn't sleep either. They'd be watching the little silver beads climb. Hesper had watched them climb, by the sink. It had made her feel sick, like watching a child climb a tree that was too high, when you can see where it will have to stop and it can't.

She took a cord off her belt.

She hadn't meant to. Her fingers did it. Thirty years of mornings. She had the cord in her lap and her hand reaching for the turns before she'd thought about it, and then she did think about it, and looked at the rings on her two fingers, and thought: well. Once more. Loose.

"Give me a whisker," she said.

Hush lifted her head.

*Why.*

"To ask with."

The cat looked at her a long moment with the lamps. Then she put her face up, and Hesper took hold of one of the long stiff whiskers by the root, gently, and the cat let her, and it came out, and Hush sneezed, which was the loudest thing she'd done all year.

Three turns, with the whisker in them. A twist. The word on the twist, the water-over-stone word, the one she'd taught the knight at the edge of the Ring with the grey all round them and the great dead breath going past, eleven in and nineteen out. The knight had got it wrong the first time. Most people did. She'd said it wrong and looked at Hesper as if she expected to be sent out of the yard, and Hesper had said it again for her, slower, and the second time it had been nearly right, and the knight had tied it with a hair from the lion's mane as long as her arm, and it had lain in her palm like any string in the world.

And then the next morning the grey-maned had put his chin down on the knight's wrist, without anyone asking him, and the string had come alive.

Hesper had seen that. She'd been sitting at the foot of the south stone with Hush miserable beside her, and she'd seen the knight's hand jerk, and the cord go tight across her palm as if a fish had taken it, and she'd seen the knight's face. Hesper hadn't expected a lowland knight to have a face like that: a child's, a child who has been told her whole life she can't swim, and is swimming.

Keepers taught their way mother to child. That was how it went. Her grandmother to her mother. Her mother to her, on the platform in the oak where Hesper still lived, in the mornings, with the cord going round Hesper's small fingers and her mother's big ones over them. Hesper had no child. She'd thought the knot would end with her and had made her peace with it, mostly, as she'd made peace with the half. Then she'd taught it to a lowlander, a knight-that-was, in a dead place, and it had worked through a lion.

She didn't know what that made it. Whose it was now. She thought her mother would have laughed until she had to sit down.

In her head, while she tied, the asking: the shape of it, with no words. *Where should I stand?*

She held the knot loose in her open palm.

It pulled. At once, hard, the cord drawing tight across her hand and dragging her hand down, not north, not toward the water, not toward anything. Down. Straight down into the turf of the lane between her knees, as if something under the ground had taken hold of the other end and wanted her hand in the earth.

She laughed out loud. She couldn't help it.

"Here, then," she said.

It was already biting. She slid the spear's edge under the turns and cut it before it could get into her skin, and the two halves lay on the turf and moved a little, slowly, and were still.

*Told you,* said Hush. *Here. Under.*

"You did."

---

Some time after midnight Hush stood up.

She stood up off Hesper's feet all at once, and the cold came where she'd been, and she looked north. Up the slope. Up the long empty league of turf to the edge of the forest, which was only a blacker black under the stars.

*Men,* she said.

Hesper got up.

*There. The edge. Men and iron. Cold iron, cold like snow.* The cat's tail went once, side to side. *The ones who smell of snow. Their cats.*

The Wardens. The lowland colleges, out of the north, with their white lynx and their tall iron lamps. Hesper had smelled them at the Ring, a smell like a pot that has been scrubbed with ash until there's no smell left in it, which is a smell of its own. She looked and couldn't see anything. She was a Keeper and not a cat, and the edge was a league off in the dark.

"What are they doing?"

*Waiting.* Hush sat down again, but not on her feet. A body's length off, where she could see. *Like us.*

Hesper stood with her spear and looked at the dark where the edge was, and thought of the tall iron lamps in their sacking on the mules, coming past the Speaking Stones, and the glass-man walking beside the last mule with his head down. She thought she ought to go down and tell the knight. And she thought: tell her what? That the cat smells snow on the forest's edge. The knight had two lanes and one lion to think about. Hesper had one lane and one cat and a whisker's worth of answer. She sat back down against the tub.

She didn't sleep. Some time in the long dark before dawn she found she was humming, under her breath, and stopped, and then didn't see why she should, and went on.

---

At dawn the birds went.

She heard it first as wind. A great rushing roar out of the north, out of the whole length of the forest's edge at once, and out of the Arm beside her, close, so close that the black pines over her lane shook and the needles came down on her like rain. Then the sky over the trees was black with them. Every bird in the northern Thornwood. Rooks and pigeons and finches and owls and hawks, all together, owls in daylight and hawks beside the birds they ate, nobody hunting, nobody minding anybody, one cloud, pouring west over the Moot and over the lake and over the line of boats, so many that the dawn went dark under them.

She stood in the lane with her face up and watched them go, and the sound of their wings was the whole world.

Then they were gone, west, and it was very quiet, and the lake was so still she could see the boats twice, once on it and once upside down in it.

Hush was flat to the turf with her ears back.

"I know," said Hesper, before the cat could say it.

The light came. Grey first, then gold along the top of the rise. Down at the water's edge the candle had gone out. She saw a small figure get into a boat with a box on its knees, and the boat go out onto the lake, and she thought: good. Be polite.

She saw the knight come up the slope. A long way down at first, small, walking fast up between the tents with the lion beside her, the grey of him paler than the grey of the turf. They came up to the bottom of the fifth lane and turned into it, and went on up. Hesper lost them behind the roofs. Then she saw the lion's back again, over a ridge, as he stopped. The fifth lane. The near one.

The sixth lane, between them, had nobody in it. Through the gap between Sedge's house and the next one down, she could see a strip of it: bare earth going down the slope, and at the very bottom of it, by the water, the biggest of the stone-footed roundhouses with its door shut. Thirty children in it. Forty. Two old women. She'd watched them go in.

She looked at that strip of empty lane for a while.

Then she went to the middle of her own, where it was narrowest, between the third house and the trees, and stood there with her spear, and Hush came and lay down beside her, low, flat, with her tail going.

---

The ground opened at the full of the morning.

Not here. North. She'd known it would be north. She felt it first in her feet, through her bare soles, before there was anything to hear: a sound too low for sound, as if the whole hill had swallowed. Then she heard it. A long deep noise out of the earth a league off, under the forest's edge, like a great beast clearing its throat before it speaks.

Then the ground heaved.

It lifted under her. The whole slope, all at once, like a blanket when somebody turns over under it. She went down on one knee with the spear-butt in the turf. Sedge's house beside her swayed on its posts and the thatch hissed. A tent somewhere went down with a crack of poles. Up and down the slope the duskcats on the ridges crouched flat to the thatch, every one, as if a hand had pressed them. Behind her, far down, the lake slopped up over the shingle in one long grey wave and slid back, and out on the water somebody screamed, and stopped.

She stood up.

And over the Thornwood, to the north, at the forest's edge a league up the slope, there was a flash.

Three of them. Three white sparks, cold white, small and far, in a row at the edge of the trees. They flared once, all together, too bright to look at. And went out. She didn't know what they were. She'd find out, or she wouldn't. Hush hissed, a long low sound like a kettle, the first time Hesper had heard her hiss since she was young.

And over the place where the sparks had been, something rose.

---

Antlers.

She saw them come up over the black line of the trees, going up into the morning, bare, grey and brown, branching and branching. A great crown of them. Going up past the tops of the pines and on up, branching, until they stood over the forest like another forest, a winter one, standing on its head in the sky.

Then the head under them.

A stag's head. Long, grey-brown, with a great dark eye, wet, that caught the light. The size of a hill. It came up out of the forest's edge slowly, with the trees falling away from it on both sides like water off a bear coming up out of a pool, and lifted, and the crown went up with it, and the morning came through the branches of it in long gold bars.

Then it stood.

She knew it. She'd had her hand on it, under the Ring, on the warm stone, in the grey glow, with her torch gone out: the stag lying down with its legs folded under it and the forest lying down round it, the carved trees leaning in, their branches folded over like arms. *That one's ours,* she'd said. *That one's the forest.* She'd said it as if it were a thing in a song.

The trees round the stag were getting up.

She'd thought the carver meant it as you'd carve leaves round a door. Every tree round the great stag was pulling up out of the earth, roots and all, the roots coming up wet and black and reaching like legs that had been folded a long time, and the trunks leaning, and straightening, and moving. A forest, walking. With it. Round it. As if they'd lain down with it when it lay down, a thousand years ago, and had been waiting all that time for it to get up, and it had, and so had they.

The sound of it came down the slope a moment later. A long tearing groan, a thousand roots coming out of the ground at once.

Hesper's mouth was open. She shut it.

*Old Stag,* she thought. That was the clans' word for it, the word you said to children when the ground shook in the north: the Old Stag turning over in his sleep. You didn't ask what stag. You didn't ask who he was. *Like the six who sleep.* Nobody had ever asked.

He wasn't asleep now.

On the shingle, far behind her, she knew without looking that Morwen was sitting with her back to the lake and her face to the forest and her bear behind her, and wasn't moving. That Aun was sitting there with her white eyes open on it, seeing nothing, knowing everything. Elders stay. That's what they're for.

"Grandmother," Hesper said aloud, to nobody. She didn't know which grandmother. Her own, who'd planted the sleepwood. Or the Grey Mother, who keeps the stones, whom you call on when the ground shakes. Or something else entirely, older than either, standing up a league off with a forest on its feet.

*Coming,* said Hush.

---

Then the beasts came.

They came out of the Arm first.

Before anything showed at the main edge she heard them in the trees beside her lane: a crashing, close, coming down the Arm, branches breaking, the whole tongue of forest shaking along its length, and the smell. Fear has a smell. Every Keeper knows it. Sour and sharp, like a cut onion and a wet dog together, and this was so thick it stood in the lane like smoke.

The first of them broke out of the trees on her right a dozen paces up the lane. A hind. A red hind, alone, her eyes white all round, her tongue out. She came out of the Arm sideways, at a dead run, and hit the lane and turned down it toward the water, and saw Hesper.

Hesper put her left hand out flat, palm down, her mother's hand, pressing on the air.

*Go round,* she asked, in the old tongue. Small. As small as she could make it. *Go round.*

The hind heard her.

She heard all of it, every bit, all the way in. Hesper felt it go in, like a peg going into green wood under the mallet, all the way to the head. The hind turned. She turned as if she'd been struck, all at once, at a run, in the space of a stride, her legs going one way and her body the other. Her shoulder went into the corner-post of the house below Sedge's, and Hesper heard her neck go.

She lay at the foot of the post with her legs still running.

Hesper stood in the lane with her hand out.

*Too much,* said Hush, very low.

"Yes."

She'd known. She'd told the knight she knew. Knowing it was a different animal from seeing it lying at the foot of a post with its legs going.

There was no time. More were coming out of the Arm. Three roe deer together. A pair of foxes. A badger, astonishingly fast, low, like a stone rolling. Martens. Behind them something heavier.

She closed her hand halfway.

That was what you did, with a child who heard too much. You didn't stop talking to it. You said less. You said it softer. She shut her hand to a loose fist and held it down by her hip instead of out, and made the asking so thin she could hardly feel it go out of her, a thread of it, a hair. *Round,* she asked. Not *go*. Just *round*, and the shape of the lane in her head, the bare earth going down to the water between the houses and the trees, the shape of where the houses weren't.

The roe deer came at her. She asked. They heard. They swerved, all three, hard, too hard still, and one went down on its knees and was up again, and they went past her on the tree side, so close she felt the wind of them, and on down the lane toward the water. The foxes didn't need asking; the foxes went where the deer went. The badger went under Sedge's step and stayed there, which she thought was sensible of it.

It worked. If she held it thin enough, it worked. Like carrying a full pot without spilling it. You could do it if you thought about nothing else.

She thought about nothing else.

---

The flood came out of the main edge a little after.

She saw it over the roofs, up the long slope to the north: the whole edge of the forest emptying at once along its length. Brown and grey and black, coming out from under the trees as if somebody had lifted the forest by one side and everything in it was sliding out. Deer, more deer than she'd known there were. Elk, wild ones, with their antlers laid back. Boar in a mass, a single dark thing with a hundred legs. Wolves, wild, tails down, running among the deer and not looking at them. Bears. Everything small in a moving brown skin across the grass. And away at the far west end, a grey wave with a sound like a landslide in it: the wild stonehorn herd, coming out of the trees at a run with their heads down.

All one way. South. Away from the stag. Toward the water.

They came down the league of empty turf toward the Moot, and the turf shook, and she watched them come and kept her fist loose by her hip and her asking thin as a hair, because the Arm was still emptying beside her, and every few breaths something came out of it into her lane.

The front reached the top of the roundhouses.

She heard the western lanes before anything else. A crash like two cliffs meeting: the wild stonehorns hitting the tame ones. Then the horse clans' riders yelling, high and fierce, like hawks. Then a long changing roar as the wild herd broke on the wall of plated backs and went round, sideways, and down, and a sound like the lake being hit with a mountain as a hundred of them went into the water at once.

Somewhere in the middle of the Moot the great pale bear was roaring. She knew its voice now.

And from the near lane, the fifth, two lanes west of her, the lion.

She'd heard him roar before. On the knowe, the knight said, and at a gate, far away in the lowlands. Hesper had never heard it. It came across the roofs and went through her and through the ground under her feet, and she felt the ground answer it. The turf of the fifth lane came up. She saw it over the ridges: a long bank of earth heaving up out of the ground across the lane like a wave rising, with the grass still on top, and curving. She saw the flood hit it and turn.

*Grandfather,* she said, under her breath.

*Grandfather,* said Hush.

She'd never heard Hush call him anything.

---

Then the sixth lane broke.

She saw it through the gap between Sedge's house and the next. The strip of empty lane, going down to the big roundhouse by the water. Something came into the top of it. Then a great deal of something. Boar first, the dark mass with a hundred legs, and behind them deer, elk, a pack of wild wolves running in the middle of everything, the whole flood that had come down on the knight's bank and been turned east by it, turned into the sixth, where nobody was, and coming down it. Toward the big roundhouse at the bottom. The door shut. Forty children behind it.

Hesper took one step toward the gap.

She couldn't. There was her own lane. There was the Arm, still emptying beside her, and behind the flood in the sixth there'd be more, and if she went to the sixth her lane would go through Sedge's house and the next and the next. And what could she do in the sixth, with her hand and a hair's worth of asking, against that? You stand in a lane so they go down it and not through the houses. You don't stand in two.

She stood still and looked through the gap at the children's door.

The ground in the sixth lane heaved.

She saw it. The turf of the lane, just above the big roundhouse, lifted. Not in a bank. In a shape. It came up out of the earth like a swimmer coming up out of a lake, the turf sliding off its back, grey, enormous, with a mane like iron-grey cloud and pale eyes. It shook itself. It stood. Across the lane, with the children's door at its back.

A lion.

The same lion. The same size, the same weight, the same mane, the same way of standing. She looked through the gap between the houses at it, and then over the roofs to the west, where the other one was, where the grey back still stood over the ridges in the fifth lane holding its bank. Two. One in the near lane and one in the far.

It roared. She heard it twice, from both of them, in the same breath, from the same throat. The turf of the sixth lane came up in a bank. The flood hit it and turned.

Hesper laughed. It came out of her before she could stop it, a crack of sound, half a sob. Out here in the seventh lane with a dead hind at the foot of a post and her hand in a fist and the Arm crashing beside her, she laughed, because she'd stood at the Speaking Stones and told four hundred people and three elders that the knight was the one he let sit with him, and that had been true, and it had been the smallest true thing she could find to say.

The knight was in that one too. She knew it as surely as she knew the cat at her feet. Hesper had felt Hush in her own body for eleven years, a second set of feet, a second nose, a warm weight somewhere at the edge of her. She knew what a bond was. The knight would be in both lanes now, both banks, both bodies, stretched across the two of them like a hide over a frame.

*Hold on,* she thought at the knight, who couldn't hear her. *Hold on, girl.*

---

The seventh lane filled.

There was less of it than the others. It was narrow. But the first of everything that came down off the slope on the east side and didn't want the open turf swung into the Arm for cover, and the Arm was full and spilling, and it all came out at her. She stopped seeing it as beasts. It was weather. A run of deer and she asked, thin, *round*, and they went round, hard, on the tree side, and one hit a pine and went down and got up. A sow with half-grown young and she asked and they went. Wolves, three of them, wild, and one stopped dead in front of her, a stride off, and looked her in the face with its eyes white all round, and Hush was up and in front of her, flat, with her mouth open and no sound coming out of it at all, and the wolf heard Hesper's asking and Hush's open mouth together and went round so fast it went over on its side.

Her arm was shaking. Her fist. The beasts didn't do that; holding the asking thin did. Every time she asked, the ground under her wanted to give it more than she'd asked for, as a river in spate wants to take the bank, and she had to hold it back. She felt it in the bones of her hand. In her two ringed fingers. The ground was so full. It had been held down for a hundred days and now it was let go and it wanted to answer everything, everyone, all the way, at once, and the only thing standing between it and the lane was a fist.

She started singing it. The asking. Under her breath, the old tongue, the shape of the lane in her head. *Round. Round. Not this way. Round.* You could hold a thing thinner if it had a tune. Her mother had known that.

*Hesper,* said Hush.

The cat hadn't said her name in a year.

*Heavy. In the Arm. Old.*

She knew before she saw him. She heard him in the trees above her, a different noise from the deer, slower, a breaking and a breaking, pines going over, an old oak cracking like a shot. She smelled him. Stonehorn bull. Dung and wet stone and old anger.

Leans-Left.

He'd lived in the Arm alone for longer than Hush had been alive. The Keepers knew him. Every year at the Moot somebody went up into the Arm to look at him, from a long way off, and came back and said he was still there. An old bull, too old for the herd, too cross to die, with a horn that had grown crooked and swept off to the left of his face like a branch somebody had bent and let go. The children were told: don't go in the Arm, Leans-Left will have you. He never had anybody. He wanted to be left alone, and he was.

He came out of the trees at the top of her lane as big as a haywain, plated, grey, the crooked horn first. He'd been running a league. His sides were going in and out like a bellows. His eyes were white all round and there was nothing in them. Nobody at home. Only the surge, the whole wild flood of it, in his legs.

He put his head down and came down the lane at her.

Behind him, the rest of the Arm. Everything that had been sheltering in it.

She opened her hand.

She didn't decide to. Or she did, and it was the same thing. There was too much. A thread wouldn't turn him; he was too big and too far gone and there was too much behind him. She opened her hand and held it out, flat, palm down, her mother's hand, and asked him as her mother had asked in the dry year, with all of it.

*Go round,* she asked. *Old one. Go round. Not this way. Please. Round.*

He heard her.

---

She felt it go in.

All of it. All the way. The ground took her asking and gave it to him and gave it to him and gave it to him, full, and she felt him hear it to the bottom of himself, where there had been nothing, and something was. For one breath the old bull's eye wasn't empty. She saw him in it. She saw him hear a Keeper ask him, by his name, with all her heart, to go round. Nobody had asked him anything in thirty years.

He went round.

He turned as the hind had turned. All at once, at a run, as big as a haywain, in the space of a stride. Too hard. Too fast. Away from her, away from the tree side, into the side of the lane, into the house. Sedge's house. The third from the top, with the willow-tubs at the door and the step where they'd sat. He went into the wall of it with all his weight and the crooked horn first.

The wall went in like a basket stepped on.

She heard the posts go. She heard the roof-tree crack above her, a great dry crack, like a tree splitting in frost. She looked up and the thatch was coming over. The whole roof, leaning, tipping, coming off its posts and down over the lane toward the narrow place where she stood, slowly. Very slowly, it seemed. She could see each bundle of reed. She could see a bird's nest in the eaves with nobody in it.

She could still have run.

She didn't think of it, and then she thought of it, and the thought was a small one, a long way off. Behind Leans-Left the Arm was coming, all of it, and it had heard her too. She felt it. The whole front of the flood in her lane had heard the Keeper ask the old bull to go round, every beast of it, all the way in, and it was turning. Going round. Down the lane on the tree side, past the place where she stood, away from the houses, toward the water. Where the houses weren't.

You make them go where the houses aren't.

Under her the ground was singing back to her in its full voice, answering, answering.

Hush was against her legs.

*Hesper,* said the cat.

She stopped asking. She didn't need to; it was asked. And because something in her mouth still wanted a tune, and because Hush was against her legs shaking, and because she'd sung it to other women's children when they were teething and nobody had ever sung it to her except her mother, behind the third lane, in the dry year, she sang the other thing.

*Hush, now. Hush. Like the six who sleep.*

In her own tongue. Low, rising and falling, a tune to rock something with. To the cat. The roof came down over the sky.

*Hush, now. Hush.*

Through the gap between the houses, still standing, she could see the grey shoulder of the far lion in the sixth lane, holding. Stretched. And in him, she knew, the knight. Listening, maybe. A lowlander who'd never had anyone sing it to her at all.

So the last of it she sang in the knight's flat tongue, where everything lay down level like boards on a floor, so somebody would know what it had said.

*Like the six who sleep.*

Hush came up off the ground.

Not away. Up. Across her. The cat came up and over her in one long black leap, without a sound, as she'd come down off a thousand branches, and was across her chest and her shoulders and her face, warm, all of her, as the roof came down.

Hesper put her arms round her.
