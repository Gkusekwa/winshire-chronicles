On Glasswater they say the lake has no bottom, and that the ferry-folk know it better than the colleges do, because the colleges have only measured it and the ferry-folk have crossed it.

They say a lot of things on Glasswater. That you don't whistle on the water after dark. That a boat built by a man who died in her will never sit straight. That a ferry owl knows the lake's mood better than the ferryman, and that if your owl won't sleep you should stay ashore. That the Lady under the Water takes one boat in a hundred years and you'll know it's yours because the owl will look at you. The colleges on the hill have charts for the lake, with its depths written in, and lines for its currents, and none of these sayings are on them. The ferry-folk have no charts. They have their sayings, and their owls, and a little tin serpent nailed to every prow, and they have been crossing the black water under Vael's towers longer than there have been towers.

Nessa Vael didn't hold with most of it. She whistled when she liked. She'd stopped throwing salt for the Lady the first week she had the *Heron* to herself, because salt was dear and the Lady could buy her own. But she touched the tin serpent when she pushed off and touched it again when she came in, because her mother had, and because you didn't stop doing a thing just because you'd stopped believing it.

And she watched the owl.

---

It was the last crossing of the day, and the snow was still on the roofs of the city, and the light was going.

Spring came late on Glasswater. It came up the valley from the south a long time after it had come everywhere else, and it came first to the water, so that there were weeks when the lake was black and open and moving and the city above it was still white to the eaves. This was one of those weeks. The fish were rising again off the far-shore reeds. The ice had gone out of the foot of the lake, down toward the valley, where it went every year. Up on the hill the towers of the colleges stood in their snow like old men in shawls, with the covered bridges between them, and the porters were going round lighting the lamps.

Nessa had a load of firewood, a pedlar with a pack, and a woman from Tern End going to her daughter's lying-in. The woman talked. Nessa let her. She had the sweep, and the brown lugsail was drawing, and there was nothing to do but stand and lean and look at the water.

Then she looked at the owl, because she always did, and stopped leaning.

It was the ferry's owl. Every boat on Glasswater kept one, in a wicker cage on the prow, and theirs was the one their mother had kept, an old grey hen-owl with ear-tufts like a pair of torn sails and eyes the colour of honey held up to a candle. A lantern owl's eyes glowed. Everybody knew that. Faintly, a warm small light, enough to see on a prow in a fog, not enough to read by. The students on the hill rubbed the owls' cages for luck before their examinations. The ferry-folk kept them because a boat with an owl on it was a boat you could see.

The owl's eyes were white.

Not honey. White. Bright, hard, like two holes knocked in a lantern. The light came out of them and lay on the wicker and on the tin Lady beside the cage, and on the water ahead of the prow, a little white path on the black. The owl stood on its perch with all its feathers drawn in tight, thin as a stick, and it had turned round to face south, down the lake, toward the foot and the valley and the mountains, and it was quite still.

"Look at that," said the woman from Tern End. "Is she sick?"

"No," said Nessa.

She looked at the city.

The lamps were coming on up the hill. They came on every evening in their colours, the Blue College blue, the College of Stars a thin silver, the Physicians green, the Scribes' amber, so that the hill looked like a hand of cards somebody had thrown down on the dark. Their father had said so to every passenger he ever had.

The lamps were coming on white. Every one. Blue College, white. Stars, white. The amber stair of the Scribes, white, the whole crooked length of it. Down the hill, the lower city, the fish-market, the dyers' yards, the little colleges in their cheap halls by the water: white. On the quays, the lanterns on their poles. On the boats at their moorings along the shore, the riding-lamps, white, white, white, all the way along.

The woman from Tern End had stopped talking.

"Is that the colleges?" said the pedlar. "Is that something they're doing?"

"I don't know," said Nessa.

She put her weight on the sweep and brought the *Heron* round for the ferry steps, and as they came in past the end of the fish-quay the owl began to make a sound.

---

She'd never heard it. Thirty-five years on the water, more or less, counting the years she'd sat in the bottom of the boat at her father's feet, and she'd never heard a lantern owl make any sound but the little dry click they made when they wanted feeding, and the hoot they gave the dark at midnight, once, as if to tell it they were there.

This was a scream. High and long and flat, like a hinge being forced. It went out over the water. Along the shore, from the other boats coming in to their moorings, other owls answered it, and she heard ferrymen she'd known all her life swearing at them in the dusk.

She touched the Lady coming in. She made the rope fast. The woman from Tern End got out without paying and Nessa let her go, and the pedlar paid twice over and went up Ferry Street fast with his pack bumping. She unshipped the cage from its bracket on the prow, as she did every night, and the owl screamed at her, a hand's breadth from her face, with its eyes blazing, and she held the cage out at arm's length and carried it up the steps like that, like a pot off the fire.

Mother Talley was out on the corner of the fish-market.

She was sitting on a chair under her lamp. The lamp hung from an iron bracket on the corner of her house, and had hung there forty years, and her father's forty before that, and it was a lamp of the old Isvaren kind that burned without oil on a word somebody had cut in its foot, and every night since Nessa could remember it had burned the colour of butter. Tonight it burned so white the whole corner was lit like noon. You could see every scale on the cobbles. Mother Talley was sitting under it in her shawl with her hands folded on her stick and her face turned up.

"Will you look at that," she said. "Forty years. Never seen her so bright. Never." She looked at the cage. "What's the matter with your bird?"

"Same as your lamp."

"Mine's not screaming." She looked up at it again, fond, as you'd look at a child who'd done well at something. "I'm stopping out. I'm not missing this."

"You'll freeze."

"Then I'll freeze in the light," said Mother Talley.

---

The house on Rope Street was narrow and high, three rooms one above the other, with the kitchen at the bottom and the street door opening into it, and the rope-walk at the top of the street creaking all day as the cord came off the wheel. Their grandfather had bought it with the money from the first *Heron*. It smelled of tar and fish and the river-weed they burned in the grate when the wood was dear.

The kitchen lamp was burning white.

It was a cheap one, from a stall at the bottom of the College stair, half the price of Mother Talley's and a quarter the age, a little brass dish with a word stamped in the foot that made it give a yellow light you could peel potatoes by. Tonight it filled the kitchen like snow. Every pot on the shelf threw a hard black shadow. The boy was sitting at the table with his hands flat on it and his eyes screwed half shut against it.

"It won't go down," Tib said. "I tried. I turned it. It won't."

He was twelve, a cousin's son from the far shore, and had lived with her two winters, since his mother married a man in Tern End who didn't want him. He was thin and he ate like a mule and he was frightened of nothing on the water and a great many things ashore.

"Leave it," said Nessa.

She hung the cage on its hook by the hearth. The owl screamed. Tib put his hands over his ears.

"What's wrong with it? What's wrong with her?"

"Nothing."

"She's screaming."

"I know she's screaming. I can hear she's screaming." She took the bucket from under the sink and emptied the potato water into the yard and came back and put the bucket upside down over the lamp. The kitchen went dark except for the owl. The owl's light lay on the walls in two white rounds that swung as the cage swung. "There. Get the blanket off my bed."

"Why?"

"Because I said."

He went. The owl screamed. Nessa stood in the middle of her kitchen in the white light coming out of a bird and thought about the hill.

---

When he came down with the blanket she put it over the cage, all of it, tucked round the bottom, as her mother had done on nights of thunder. The scream came through it. The light came through it too, at the edges, a thin white seam right round where the blanket didn't quite meet, and through the weave itself in a hundred tiny points, so that the cage hung by the hearth like a lantern somebody had tried to put out and failed.

She sat down at the table across from Tib.

"Are you frightened?" he said.

"No."

"I am."

"Well, that's one of us," said Nessa. "Have you eaten?"

He hadn't. She got up and cut him bread and the end of the cheese and put it in front of him, and he ate it in the dark with his shoulders up round his ears, and the owl screamed, and the light came through the blanket in its hundred points and lay on his face.

She went to the window.

From the kitchen on Rope Street you could see a slice of the hill between the houses opposite, and tonight it was all white. The towers. The covered bridges between them, every window. The long grey roof of the Library on the hill's shoulder, which on ordinary nights showed only a few amber lights along its length like a boat's portholes, was white from end to end. And high up, on the round tower of the College of Stars where the wild lantern owls nested, there was a row of small hard points of light along the ledges, very small at that distance, very bright. Dozens. All in a line. All still.

All turned south.

She knew which window was her brother's. You couldn't see it from Rope Street; it was round the far side of the College of Tides, under the roof, and looked out over the lake. She'd been up there once, the year he got his ring, and had stood in the middle of his room because the ceiling sloped too steep to stand anywhere else, and had looked at his bed, and his table, and the ledgers on it, and his ink, and told him he'd die of cold up there and nobody would find him till spring.

He'd be at his glasses. He'd be at them all night. He kept the night bells, he'd told her once, because nobody else would.

She didn't go up. She thought about it. She thought about walking up the College stair at night in the white light with a screaming owl on Rope Street behind her and a frightened boy, and knocking on the porter's lodge, and the porter's face. Then she thought about her brother's face if she did, and sat down again.

---

"Did Idony ever do this?" said Tib. "When he was little? Sit up with her?"

"Not with her. With the one before. He was always sitting up with something."

She got up and lifted the corner of the blanket. The light came out at her, hard, so she had to narrow her eyes. The owl was on its perch with its feathers drawn in and its face to the south wall, toward the lake. It didn't look at her. She held out a sprat from the dish on the shelf, between finger and thumb, and it didn't look at that either.

She let the blanket fall.

"Come here," she said.

Tib came. She took him by the shoulder to the door-post, the inside of the street door, where the wood was old and greasy and dark, and held up the corner of the blanket again, so the owl-light fell on it.

There were marks on the post. Chalk. Old, gone grey, worked into the grain, a little ladder of short white lines with tiny numbers beside them, from about the height of Tib's chest to about the height of his nose. Hundreds of them. Some had words by them in a child's round letters. *Fog.* *Very bright.* *Dim, Mam says thunder.*

"What's that?"

"Your cousin," said Nessa. "Every night. Five years. How bright her eyes were." She meant the one before; it didn't matter. "Our mother'd have washed it off if Da had let her. He wouldn't. He said it was the only book in the house."

Tib looked at the marks. He put his finger on one.

"What's the top one?"

The top one was near his nose. It said *Highest yet* in the round letters, and a date in the old ferry way, so many days after the ice went.

Nessa took the chalk from the shelf where they kept it for slates. She reached up, with the blanket held back and the owl's light pouring on the wood and the scream going on and on in the cage, and drew a line on the post a foot above the boy's head. Higher than any of them. She stood back and looked at it.

She didn't write anything beside it. She didn't know what he'd have written. She thought he'd want to know, that was all.

"He'll laugh at that," said Tib.

"Let him," said Nessa.

---

Some time in the night she went out to the step.

The street was white. Rope Street went up from the fish-market in a long crooked climb toward Candle Row, and every lamp on it was blazing, and every door had somebody in it. The dyers' wives. The cordwainer and his daughters. Old Mags Penner in her nightcap with a poker in her hand, for some reason. Nobody was talking much. Down at the corner Mother Talley was still sitting under her lamp with a second shawl over the first, and somebody had brought her a cup of something, and she was holding it in both hands and looking up.

Behind every door, screaming. Every ferry family on Rope Street had its owl in.

"It's the colleges," said the cordwainer, across the street. "It'll be the colleges. Some working."

"What working?"

"How would I know what working? It's always the colleges."

"It's not just here," said Mags Penner. "My son-in-law came down off the Pass road at dusk. He says every lamp in the toll-house at the top was the same. White. A day off. That's not the colleges."

Nobody said anything to that.

Nessa looked up the hill. She thought, for no reason she could have given, of the night her father had taken her out on the lake in a thunderstorm when she was eight, to fetch the midwife back across for a woman on the far shore, and how he'd stood at the sweep with the rain going sideways and his face perfectly calm, and how, when they got in, she'd seen that his hands were shaking so hard he couldn't untie the rope.

She went back in.

---

It came at midnight.

She was at the table with her head on her arms, not asleep. Tib was asleep on the settle under her coat. The owl had been screaming so long that she'd stopped hearing it, as you stop hearing the wind.

Then it stopped.

She lifted her head.

And up the hill, far off, came a sound. Not loud. Like a dresser of crockery going over in somebody else's house, down the street, behind a closed door. One long crash made out of hundreds of small ones. Then a kind of rustle after it, very faint, that might have been something falling, a great deal of something, small. Then nothing.

The owl was silent. Under the blanket its light had gone up so bright that the seam round the edges was a white line drawn on the dark, and the hundred points in the weave had run together into one glow, and the blanket itself looked thin.

Tib sat up. "What was that?"

"Something broke."

"What?"

"Something up the hill." She was standing. She didn't remember standing. "Go back to sleep."

The owl drew breath. She heard it, a small dry sound under the blanket. Then it screamed again, louder than before, and went on.

Nessa stood in her kitchen and looked at the wall, toward the hill, through it.

She'd known her brother thirty-one years. She'd carried him about this kitchen on her hip when he was a year old and she was five and he screamed at everything. She'd taught him to scull. She'd seen him take the back off Da's compass when he was six, and lose the little screw, and sit under the table all afternoon with the pieces laid out in a row in front of him, not crying, waiting for them to tell him something. The compass was on the shelf above her now. Its back had been on crooked for twenty-five years.

Something had broken up there. Something made of a great many small pieces.

She knew where he'd be standing.

---

The light went out a little before dawn.

She'd been watching the window. The sky over the houses opposite had gone from black to the colour of slate, and she was thinking about the first crossing, and whether there'd be one, and who'd want it. And behind her the scream stopped again.

She turned.

The cage hung by the hearth under its blanket. There was no seam of light round the edges. There were no points in the weave. The kitchen was dark, properly dark, the grey dark of a room before morning, for the first time since dusk.

She went and lifted the blanket.

The owl was on its perch, upright, with its feathers loose now, fluffed out so that it looked twice the size it had all night. Its head was sunk into its shoulders. Its eyes were shut.

It was asleep.

She stood with the blanket in her hand and looked at it. She had never in her life seen a lantern owl asleep with the dark coming up. They slept in the day, after the morning crossings, on the prow in the sun. At dawn they were wide awake, and their eyes were the first light on the water.

She put her hand to the bars. The owl didn't wake. Its breast went in and out, slow, under the fluffed grey feathers. She could see its eyelids, thin and pale and a little blue, and nothing coming through them at all.

Tib was awake on the settle, watching her.

"Is she dead?"

"Asleep."

"Is that bad?"

"I don't know," said Nessa. "Get your boots on."

---

She took the first crossing anyway, because the far shore had a man waiting for the market with a cart of hay, and because the *Heron* had always taken the first crossing.

The lower city was coming out of its doors as she went down. Grey-faced people, in their coats. On the corner Mother Talley's chair was empty, with the second shawl folded on the seat, and her lamp over it was burning butter-yellow again in the grey light, small and ordinary, as if it had never done anything else. Every lamp in the street was its own colour again. Up the hill, the colleges were blue and silver and green and amber, and the porters were going round putting them out.

The lake lay flat and black.

She shipped the cage on its bracket on the prow with the owl asleep in it. Tib took the sheet. She touched the Lady pushing off, two fingers on the cold tin, and put her hand back on the sweep, and the *Heron* went out past the end of the fish-quay into the still water with her owl on the prow, eyes shut, and no light on the water ahead of her at all.

It felt like going out blind. It felt like going out with no lantern in a fog. It was full grey daylight, and she could see the far shore and every reed on it, and it felt like that all the same.

"There," said Tib.

He was pointing up.

They were coming down off the High Crown. Out of the snowfields above the city, into the cold air over the roofs, sliding. Stormkites. Two of them, then two more, great grey fork-tailed shapes as wide as a barn roof and light as paper, not beating, falling and catching and falling, as they came down before weather. She'd seen them all her life. She'd never seen them come down so early, before the sun, so low over the city that you could see the long grey feathers at their wing-ends spread like fingers.

"Weather coming," said Tib, because everybody said it.

Nessa looked at the sky. It was clear from the Fells to the snow.

She looked back at the city. At the College of Tides on the hill, the old grey pile of it, the oldest and the poorest and the smallest, with its long east wing. Somewhere round the far side of that roof was a little window under the eaves that looked out over the lake. If he was at it, he'd see the *Heron* going out. He'd know her sail. She didn't wave. You didn't wave at a building.

Then the sun came over the Fells, and the lake went gold all down its length, and the owl slept on the prow with the light on its shut eyes, and didn't wake for anything.

---

Nobody on the water that morning talked about anything else.

The hay-man from the far shore talked about it all the way across. His wife had sat up with the lamp. Their dog had howled. Over the far-shore villages there'd been a white glow from Vael all night, he said, as though the whole city had caught fire, and men had stood on the shore and watched for flames and seen none.

At the College quay a Scribes' porter got on with a basket, going across for eggs, and he knew. He was full of it. It wasn't the Scribes, he said. It was the Tides. The College of Tides. They had a gallery up there, the old east wing, three hundred glasses in it, for measuring something, and at midnight every one of them had gone at once. Every single one. You could hear it from the Scribes' stair. The Tides' porters had been in the lodge this morning white as milk saying there was quicksilver running in the floor of the place from one end to the other like a stream after rain.

"Every glass?" said Nessa.

"Every one. Three hundred. Old ones. Older than the colleges, some of them, the Tides' porter said." He shifted his basket. "Glasshouse says it'll be a year blowing new. And the Tides can't afford it in ten."

"Was anybody in there?"

The porter looked at her. "In where?"

"The gallery. When they broke."

"How would I know?" He shrugged. "Some reader, I suppose. They keep the bells all night up there, the Tides. Poor sods."

Nessa looked at the water.

Some reader. Who kept the bells all night, because nobody else would. Who'd stood in a long room with three hundred glasses round him when every one went at once.

"You've a brother at the Tides, haven't you?" said the porter. "The Vael boy. The one with the hands."

"I have."

"Well," said the porter, kindly enough. "I expect he swept up."

---

She sent Tib up the College stair the next day with bread.

She told herself it was because the boy needed something to do and was underfoot. She wrapped half a loaf and a piece of smoked whitefish in a cloth and gave it to him at the bottom of the stair and told him to give it to the porter at the Tides' lodge for Reader Vael, and not to say who from.

"He'll know who from."

"Then you don't need to say."

Tib came back down at noon with the cloth folded small and nothing in it.

"The porter took it," he said. "He says Reader Vael's asleep. He says he's been asleep since last night and nobody can wake him and they've stopped trying."

"Asleep in the day."

"That's what he said."

"Then he was up all the night before," said Nessa. "And the night before that." She took the cloth and shook it out. "Did the porter say anything else?"

"He said there's glass all down the Gallery still and the readers won't sweep it because it's beneath them, and he gave me this." Tib opened his hand. There was a tiny thing in it, a sliver, bright as a fish-scale, curved. "He swept it off the lodge step. He said it's in everything. He said they're all finding it in their coats."

She took it. It was glass. Very fine, clear as water, thinner than a fingernail. She held it up to the light and it caught the sun.

She put it on the shelf by Da's compass.

---

The owl woke that afternoon, the day after the night, in the sun on the prow, and opened its eyes, and they were honey.

She'd been watching for it. She was standing in the stern at the College quay with the sweep in her hands and three passengers aboard, and the owl stirred, and shook itself, and the fluffed feathers went down sleek, and it opened its eyes. Two small warm lights. The colour they'd always been. It looked about at the water, and the quay, and her, as if it had just come in from somewhere a long way off, and it clicked its beak for feeding.

She gave it a sprat. It took it and swallowed it and clicked for another.

Nessa found that her hands on the sweep were not quite steady, and was annoyed with them, and put more weight on.

Pell was one of the three. Pell the glass-blower's apprentice from the east shore, who was not an apprentice any longer, though everybody still called him one, because he'd been one for so long. He'd owed her money four winters back, more than he could pay, for a crossing every day for half a year to a girl in Tern End who in the end had married somebody else. He'd paid it off in work. She'd never asked what work. She'd sent her brother to him.

He was going up the hill with a sack over his shoulder and his hair singed at the front.

"Glasshouse wants every blower on the lake," he said. He was pleased. "Three hundred and twelve. Fine glass, crystal. You'd not believe what they're paying." He looked at the owl, which was taking its third sprat. "She all right? The whole east shore was screaming all night. Every boat."

"She's all right."

He nodded. He rubbed his singed hair. Then he looked at her sideways, as he'd looked at her when he owed her.

"Your brother's," he said. "His ones. They'll have held, I'd bet."

"His what?"

He stared. Then he laughed. "You never knew? He never told you? What I made with him, for what I owed?" He shook his head. "Glasses. Same as the College's, only ugly. Eleven of them. Green bottle-glass, thick as your thumb, I'd not have sold a pickle in them. He cut the words in them himself with a cobbler's awl. Sat on my bench three winters cutting." He shifted the sack. "Thick glass doesn't go like fine glass. Fine glass goes to dust. Thick glass cracks and holds. I'd bet my sack his held."

Nessa brought the *Heron* in. She touched the Lady. She made the rope fast.

"Where would he keep them?" she said.

"Where would he keep them?" Pell laughed again and stepped up onto the quay. "Under his bed, if he's got the sense he was born with. Where else would you keep eleven things the College would have your ring for?"

---

He came down the morning after that.

It was mild. The snow was going off the roofs. All down the hill it was coming off in slabs and sliding and dropping into the lanes, and the gutters were running, and the light came off the wet slates so bright you had to squint. The lake was flat, black, very still. She was coming in to the College quay with a load from the east shore, and she saw him before she was halfway in.

He was standing at the bottom of the long College stair in his winter coat.

The heavy one. Their mother's work, grey frieze, cut down from their father's, too big in the shoulders, which he'd worn up the College stair the day he went up with his letter at twenty and had worn every winter since. He had it buttoned to the neck. On a morning like this, with the snow coming off the roofs and the passengers on the *Heron* sitting with their coats open, he stood at the bottom of the stair buttoned to the chin like a man on the ice in midwinter, with his hands in the pockets and his hair standing up.

She brought the *Heron* in. She touched the Lady without looking. The passengers got off.

He didn't move. He stood on the quay with the sun on him, looking at the owl.

"She's all right," Nessa said. "If that's what you're looking at."

He looked at her.

"Get in," she said. "I've a crossing."

He got in.

---

He sat in the bottom of the boat, against the side, with his knees up. She took the sweep and pushed off, and the *Heron* slid out from the quay into the flat water. He watched the owl. It was awake on its perch in the sun, clicking at nothing.

"It screamed," he said. "Didn't it. All night."

"All night. From dusk. Its eyes went white. Dusk to dawn." She leaned on the sweep. "I sat up with it in the kitchen. Put the lamp out. Put a blanket over the cage. It wouldn't eat." She thought about the chalk on the door-post, and didn't say it. "Then a bit before dawn it went to sleep. Slept through the first crossing with its eyes shut on the prow. I've never seen one do that."

"No," he said. "No, you wouldn't have."

"It ate yesterday. Four sprats."

He nodded. He put his head back against the side of the boat and shut his eyes, and the sun lay on his face, and she looked at him. He was thin. He was always thin; he forgot. There was something fresh-scraped about him this morning, as if he'd been rubbed down with sand. There was a fine glitter in his hair still, at the back, where he couldn't see it.

"Were you frightened?" he said, with his eyes shut.

"No."

He smiled a bit, at that, with his eyes shut, as if it was what he'd thought she'd say.

---

The *Heron* went out across the lake. Tib was ashore today; she had the sheet in one hand and the sweep in the other, and she managed. Idony didn't offer to help. He'd never been any use with a sail. He could scull, because she'd taught him, and he could read the lake's colour better than anybody but their father, and he'd once told a whole boatload of farmers, at the age of ten, why the fog came off the water in spring and not in autumn, at length, until a farmer had offered him a penny to stop. But he'd never been any use with a sail.

Halfway over, he said, "They took my glasses."

He said it to the sky, with his eyes shut.

"Who did?"

"The Blue College. A magister. With Wardens. And lynx." He opened his eyes. "This morning. They came to the Gallery and took my ledgers. All of them. Eleven years. Then they came up to my room and took the crate from under my bed." He looked at her. "The glasses. My own ones."

"The ones I paid for," said Nessa.

He looked at her a long moment.

"Pell?" he said.

"Pell."

"Pell," said Idony, to the sky, with feeling.

"He said you cut the words in yourself. With an awl."

"I did."

"He said they'd have held. When the others went."

He was quiet.

"They held," he said. "Three cracked. They held." Then he stopped, and she didn't ask, and the *Heron* went on across the flat water with the snow sliding off the city behind them in the sun.

---

She knew what she'd paid for, now. Eleven ugly glasses, green as pickle jars, that the College would have his ring for. She knew that much. She didn't know what they measured, and she didn't ask. She'd never asked what anything he did was for. When he was six it had been the compass, and when he was ten it had been the fog, and when he was fourteen it had been the old script on the milestone at the top of the Pass road, which he'd walked up to in his stockings in the snow one night without telling anybody and come back from with his feet blue and a page of copied marks. You didn't ask Idony what a thing was for. He'd tell you. He'd tell you until the farmer offered him a penny.

He wasn't telling her now. That was the thing she noticed. He sat in the bottom of her boat in his buttoned coat in the sun and told her nothing, and it wasn't like him at all.

She looked at the coat.

He'd sat with his knees up, and the skirt of it had fallen open over his boots, and she could see the hem. The lining at the hem. Their mother's lining, grey linen, gone soft, and all along the bottom of it, for the length of her forearm, new stitches.

Big ones. Each one long as a barleycorn, and crooked, and pulled too tight so the linen puckered between them. The thread was the wrong colour, the black the colleges used for binding ledgers. She'd have known those stitches anywhere. Their mother had sat at the kitchen table with his mending when he was twelve and held it up to the window and laughed until she cried and said he sewed like a man stitching a sail with a boathook.

Something was in the hem. The coat hung stiff there, over his boot. Something flat, folded, sewn up in the lining that morning or the night before by a man who couldn't sew and didn't trust anybody else to.

She looked at it. Then she looked at the water ahead.

She let it be. She brought the *Heron* in to the far shore and made the rope fast, and took on a man with a pig and two women with baskets, and took them all back across, and Idony sat in the bottom of the boat the whole way with his coat over his knees and his eyes shut, and she thought he'd gone to sleep.

At the College quay he got up and climbed out. He stood on the stones.

"You'll boil in that," she said.

"I'm cold."

"It's the warmest day since the ice."

"I'm cold, Ness," he said, and went up the stair.

---

The days went on.

They went on as days do on Glasswater in the thaw, a crossing and a crossing and a crossing, the snow going off the city a little more each morning and the black water coming up a finger's breadth on the ferry steps as the streams came down off the mountains into the lake, and the ice long gone out of the foot of it down the valley toward the Wend. The fish rose. The far-shore villages wanted their salt and their flour and their midwife, and the market wanted the far shore's eggs and hay and cheese, and the *Heron* took them, back and forth, as she had every spring since there'd been a *Heron*, and the one before her, and the one before that.

The owl ate. Its eyes stayed honey. At midnight it hooted once at the dark, as it always had, and Nessa, who'd heard that hoot every night of her life and never noticed it, lay in her bed on the floor above the kitchen and listened for it, and slept after.

Up on the hill the College glasshouse had its fires lit, day and night. You could see the glow from the lake after dark, a red mouth low down on the hill's east side, and the smoke going up from it straight in the still air. Pell was up there. Every blower on the lake was up there.

Idony didn't come down again. She sent Tib up with bread and fish every second day, and every second day Tib came back with the cloth folded small and said the porter had taken it, and once that Reader Vael had come to the lodge himself and taken it out of his hands and said *thank your mistress* as if Tib was a servant, and then laughed, and said *thank Nessa*, and gone back up the stair. *He looks bad,* Tib said. *He looks like he's not been to bed.*

She didn't go up.

She thought about the hem.

She thought about it at the sweep, and in the kitchen at night, and lying in bed listening for the owl. She didn't think about what was in it. That was his. She thought about him unpicking his own coat, by a lamp, under that sloping roof she'd stood under once, with the College's black thread and a needle he'd have had to borrow or buy, and sewing something up in the hem with those big crooked stitches, and then putting the coat on, in the thaw, and buttoning it to the neck, and walking down the stair to the quay to stand in the sun and look at an owl.

She thought: he's frightened. Then she thought: no. He was frightened as a boy, at night, of the dark under the stair. He'd come into her bed. This was something else. This was a man carrying something about with him because he thought somebody would come for it.

On the nail inside the street door hung their mother's salt-bag. A little bag of grey cloth with a string. It had hung there seven winters, since the lung-fever came down the lake and took their mother, and then, a month after, their father, who hadn't been ill a day in his life before that winter and didn't seem to see the point of being ill after. There was still salt in it. Nessa had never emptied it and never thrown it. She'd hung it on the nail the week she took the *Heron* and hadn't touched it since.

She didn't touch it now. She looked at it, going out in the mornings. That was all.

---

The talk went on too. The talk always went on, on a ferry.

It wasn't only Vael. That was the first thing. It came across the lake on the *Heron* in pieces, from the far shore and the east shore and from the Pass road, from carters and pedlars and drovers and a priest. At the toll-house at the top of the Pass the lamps had burned white all night. At Tern End. At the villages up the eastern streams, under the snow. In every place anybody had come from that week, the same night, the same white, the same owls screaming, and by morning, the same: everything back to its colour as if nothing had happened.

"Everywhere," said a drover from the Pass road. "My cousin's at the foot of the Fells, the Kessmoor side. Same. Same night."

"What is it?"

"Ask your colleges," said the drover. "It's their trade."

The colleges said nothing. The colleges were saying nothing so loudly that you could hear it from the lake. The porters on the *Heron* had stopped gossiping; somebody had told them to. The Blue College's Wardens were out on the College stair at every bell with their lynx, the great white cats sitting at the turns of the stair with their pale eyes half shut, watching who went up.

"Something flew," said a carter from the far shore, six days after the night. "That's what they're saying on the Pass road. At sunrise. Out of the south. Big as a hill. Going east."

"Who saw it?"

"Nobody here. Somebody down the valley. Somebody's brother." He shrugged. "That's what they're saying."

Nessa looked at the owl on the prow, which was asleep in the sun with its honey eyes shut, and thought about the night, and how all night every one of the wild owls on the College of Stars had stood on their ledges in a line with their blazing faces turned south.

Out of the south.

---

The boatman came over nine days after the night.

He was on the far-shore landing at Tern End in the late afternoon, waiting, a broad short man in a sheepskin with a river-man's oilskin rolled on his pack and a river-man's way of standing, his feet apart as if the ground might tip. He'd walked up the valley, he said. From Wendhead. Up the valley from the river by the old track along the outflow, with the meltwater roaring down beside him the whole way, and he'd not seen a soul between the mill at Wendhead and the foot of the lake but goats. He wanted Vael. He had letters for a merchant on the Scribes' stair from a merchant in Hallowmere, and he'd been paid to carry them and paid again to carry them fast.

"Hallowmere," said Nessa.

"Hearthmere's. The capital." He got into the *Heron* as a man gets into a boat who's been getting into boats since before he could walk, without looking and without touching anything, and sat down in the middle where the weight should go. "Ruddock. I run the upper Wend, Wendhead down to the Greenwold ford. Two boats. Had two boats." He looked at the owl. "That's a fine old bird."

"She was my mother's."

"We don't have them on the river. Wish we did." He was looking at its eyes. "Wish we had something."

She pushed off. She touched the Lady. Ruddock watched her do it, and said nothing, and put his own hand flat on the gunwale beside him, and she thought it was for the same reason.

It was a long crossing in the evening light, with the wind dropping. She had two others, a girl with a goose and an old man asleep. Ruddock talked. She let him. She thought, afterwards, that he'd been waiting to talk since Wendhead, and that the goats hadn't been enough.

---

"It's dark down there," he said. "Hearthmere. Did you know?"

"Dark how?"

"Dark. Their lamps. All of them." He leaned forward with his elbows on his knees. "You know what they had down there. Lamps that burned with nothing. Not a drop of oil. Every street, every house, every inn on every road, three hundred years, and the cheapest little cot on the Greenwold had a lamp over the door that'd never gone out since the old king's time. I've tied up under them. Tied up at the Greenwold ford a hundred nights under the ferry-house lamp, a lamp you could read by from the middle of the river." He looked at the water. "Out. All of them. The whole kingdom. That morning. The same morning as your white lamps here, they said, near enough to the hour."

The girl with the goose was listening.

"Their wells have gone bad," said Ruddock. "Sweet wells. Wells that had words on them, so they ran sweet. Gone brackish. Their surgeons, the licensed men, can't stop a nosebleed, I saw one try on the ford landing, on a boy, saying his words over it, saying them again, louder, and the blood just going on running down the boy's chin. Their heralds. You know they had words to carry the voice, the crown's heralds, so they could stand in a square and be heard at the back? They're shouting. I heard one at the ford, reading something from the crown, and he was just a man shouting with a paper, and nobody could hear him past the third row."

"What about the bread?" said the girl with the goose.

He looked at her.

"Their bread won't rise," he said. "A woman told me at the ford. They bake with a word down there, for the leaven, every baker in the kingdom, every kitchen. She said she'd said it over her dough four mornings and it lay in the trough like a stone. A whole kingdom," said Ruddock, "of flat bread."

The girl laughed. Then she saw his face and stopped.

Nessa leaned on the sweep. She thought about her own crock of leaven on the shelf in the kitchen on Rope Street, that had been her mother's, and her mother's mother's, that she fed every night with a handful of flour and a splash of water and that rose on nothing but itself and the warm. She'd never said a word over it in her life. She'd never needed to.

---

"What did it?" she said.

Ruddock didn't answer at once.

"They say," he said, and stopped. He looked round the boat. At the girl with the goose, at the old man asleep, at the owl. "The boatmen say. The ones that came up from below. From the Marches." He rubbed his face. "There was a war down there, you'll have heard, at the border. On the river. Some lord's war. That's all over now, they say. Over that same night." He shook his head. "And at sunrise, after, something came up out of the ground. Under the river hill there. Where the knights have their school. Came up out of the ground, the boatmen say, and went up into the sky, and flew off north-east. And the lamps went out behind it."

"Came up out of the ground."

"That's what they say. I didn't see it. I've not been below the Greenwold ford since the autumn." He looked at his hands. "But there's a man on the ford landing who did. A boatman from the Marches who came up with a load of folk running from the war. He said it was the size of a hill. He said it ate them. The lamps. Ate them right out of the ground and flew off with them in its belly, and left the place dead behind it." He looked up at the city across the water, at the colleges on the hill, their lamps coming on in the dusk, blue and silver and green and amber, ordinary. "That's what he said. Dead. He said you could feel it. Walking on it. He said it was like walking on a floor where the fire's gone out."

Nobody said anything.

The *Heron* ran on across the black water. The owl on the prow had turned round on its perch, at some point, and was looking south, down the lake, toward the foot and the valley and the Fells. Its eyes were honey. Ordinary. It was just looking.

Nessa looked at it.

*Out of the south. Big as a hill. Going east.*

She thought about her brother sitting in the bottom of the boat in his winter coat in the sun, with his eyes shut, telling her nothing. She thought about eleven green glasses under a bed that had held when every glass in the College went to dust, and a College that had come for them the next morning with Wardens and lynx.

She thought: he knew. Before the boatmen. Before anybody. Whatever it is, he knew it was there, and he made glasses to watch it, out of pickle-jar glass with Pell, three winters, on my money. And he watched it go.

She brought the *Heron* in to the quay at the foot of Ferry Street in the dark. She touched the Lady. Ruddock got out, and turned, and put a coin in her hand, too much, and closed her fingers over it with his.

"Feed that bird," he said. "Keep it fed."

---

She didn't sleep, that night.

She sat at the kitchen table with the lamp burning its cheap butter-yellow and the owl on its hook by the hearth, awake, honey-eyed, watching her. Tib was asleep upstairs. The chalk mark she'd made on the door-post was still there, a foot above the others, grey now where her sleeve had brushed it.

She got up and fed the leaven. A handful of flour, a splash of water. She stood with her hand on the crock afterward and felt it, faintly warm, alive, working on nothing but itself.

Then she sat down again and looked at the owl.

She'd been on the water all her life. Her father had been on it all his, and his father. She knew what the lake did. It took boats. Not often. One in a hundred years, the old ones said, for the Lady, and the old ones were wrong about the number but not about the thing. She'd seen two go in her own life, both in fog, both men she'd known, and she'd been out on the water the morning after each time with the others, in a line, with poles, not finding anything. You lived with it. You put a tin serpent on your prow and touched it and you lived with it, because the lake was the lake, and it didn't want anything. It was water. It just did what water did.

What the boatman had told her about wasn't water.

A thing the size of a hill that came up out of the ground and ate a kingdom's lamps, and its wells, and the words in its bread, and flew away fat with them. Something that ate. Something that had been down there under the ground a long time, eating, and was up now, and had gone off somewhere north-east, at sunrise, with all of that in its belly.

And her brother, who'd watched it for three winters on her money, was sitting up under the College roof with something sewn into his hem.

She looked at the owl.

"Well," she said to it. "What do you think?"

The owl clicked its beak, once, for feeding. She got up and gave it a sprat.

---

The day after was wet. Rain came down the lake from the north in grey curtains, and the snow on the city went to slush and then to nothing, and she made four crossings in oilskins with the owl's cage under a sack, and at the last of them, at the College quay, a Scribes' porter she knew got on dripping and said, before he'd even sat down, "Your brother's been called."

"Called where?"

"The Council. The Council of Colleges. The Hall of Rings." He wiped his face. "The day after tomorrow. He's to wait in the corridor. Nobody gets called to the Hall of Rings from a Second ring. Nobody." He looked at her as if she'd know. "What's he done?"

"Kept the night bells," said Nessa.

She took the porter across. She didn't send Tib up the stair that evening. She went up herself, in her oilskins, all the way, past the Wardens at the turns with their white lynx looking at her with their pale eyes, to the Tides' lodge, and gave the porter the cloth with the bread in it and the fish, and a second cloth, with the sliver of fine glass from the shelf folded up inside it, and said:

"For Reader Vael. From his sister. Tell him that's one of his."

The porter looked at the cloth.

"Tell him to eat the fish," Nessa said, and went back down in the rain.

---

After that, for a while, there was only the water.

He went to the Hall of Rings and came out again. She heard it on the *Heron*, two days after, from a Physicians' porter: the Vael boy had stood up in front of the seven masters and told them something, nobody knew what, and old Quell of the Tides had said something back to him, nobody knew what, and he'd come out white. That was all anybody knew. The colleges went on saying nothing. The Wardens went on sitting on the stair. The glasshouse fires went on.

The snow went off the hill entirely. The spring came up the valley and onto the water and then into the city, late, as it always did, all at once, with the cherry on the dyers' walls coming out white in a night and the lake going from black to green at the edges where the weed came up. The owl moulted a little and was cross about it. Tib grew out of his boots.

Then, nineteen days after the night, it was all over the market by noon.

The Blue College was sending men east. Wardens. Twelve, with their lynx. Into the Wyldmarch, over the Crown, to find the thing that had flown. Mule-loads of something heavy. A magister in command. And a tide-reader, because there was only one man on the hill who could follow the thing on his glasses, and the College had given him back his glasses for the road.

Nessa heard it from three people before she'd made her second crossing. She heard it from Mother Talley, from her chair on the corner. She heard it from Pell, coming down from the glasshouse with his hair singed again.

"He'll be pleased," Pell said. "Eh? Gone off to measure it. He'll be like a dog with two tails."

"Yes," said Nessa. "He'll be pleased."

---

He was on the quay below the College hill at dusk.

She saw him from halfway in. She'd the day's last crossing aboard, low in the water, a farmer and his cart and two goats and a woman with a basket of eggs, and the light was going, and he was standing at the bottom of the College stair in his winter coat. In spring. With the cherry out on the walls behind him.

She brought the *Heron* in without looking at the quay stones. You didn't look, coming in to a quay you knew; you felt for it, as you'd walk across your own kitchen in the dark. Their father had come in like that, and she'd learned it at his feet before she was tall enough to see over the gunwale. The farmer led his cart off, and the goats, and the woman with the eggs. Nessa made the rope fast and looked up at him.

"You'll have heard, then," he said.

"Everybody's heard."

He was lit up. She could see it from the boat. He was trying not to be and it was coming off him like heat off a stove. He was thirty-one years old and he was standing on the quay in a coat he didn't need, with his hands in its pockets, and he looked six, and as if somebody had given him the back of the world to take off.

"The College's sending you east with the Blue College's men to look at the thing that flew," she said. "It was all over the market by noon." She coiled a rope. "Get in. I've one more crossing."

He got in.

---

It was a still evening. The lake lay flat and black under the mountains. The last light was going pink on the snowfields of the High Crown, and behind them the lamps of the city were coming on up the hill in their colours, blue and silver and green and amber, a hand of cards laid down on the dark. She took the sweep and pushed them off from the quay, and the *Heron* slid out into the black water without a sound.

The owl blinked at him from the prow. Honey-eyed. Its ordinary light.

She took one hand off the sweep as they came out past the quay-end and touched the tin serpent with two fingers, and put her hand back. He didn't say anything about it. He'd stopped believing in the Lady when he was nine, and had told her so, often, at length, from that day to this. She'd told him he was a fool. She'd gone on touching it.

The water was quiet along the sides. He sat in the bottom of the boat with his knees up and the hem of his coat over his boots, and she didn't look at the hem.

"So," she said, halfway across. "Tell me."

---

He told her.

Not all of it. She could tell it wasn't all of it. She'd known him thirty-one years, and she could hear where he went round things, as you can hear a sweep catch weed. He told her about the glasses breaking, which everybody knew. About the thing that had moved, which everybody knew too, more or less. About the expedition, the Wardens, the magister, the east road. About the glasses. His glasses. The ugly green ones. About the magister giving them back to him in their crate, for the road, because nobody else in Vael could read them.

He didn't tell her what was in the mule-loads. She noticed that. She noticed the place where it would have gone, and how he went round it.

And he was smiling. He couldn't help it. She watched him try, and fail, and give up. He was going to measure it, he said. Nobody had ever measured one. Nobody, in the whole history of his college. He'd be the first to stand in front of one with a glass in his hand and write down a number.

"A Titan," he said.

He said it. The word out of the old songs, that their mother had sung to them in this boat on the long night crossings when they were small and couldn't sleep, and that nobody said in daylight, and nobody on the hill said at all. It came out of his mouth on the black water as easily as *fish*, and he heard it come out, and she saw him hear it.

She heard it too.

She kept her face as it was. She let the *Heron* run, with her hands off the sweep, and the water went along the sides, quiet. She looked at him. Their father had looked at a stretch of bad water like that, before he crossed it. She knew she was doing it and didn't stop.

She thought about Ruddock's hand closing hers over the coin.

"Idony," she said.

"What?"

"You know what's in the market, about Hearthmere? From the river-boats that came up the Wend? About what happened down there?"

"Some."

"Their lamps are out," said Nessa. "All of them. In the whole kingdom. The ones that burned without oil, for three hundred years, every street, every house. Out. Their wells have gone bad. Their surgeons can't stop a nosebleed. Their heralds have to shout." She looked at him across the dark boat. "A boatman told me their bread won't rise. The leaven words won't take. A whole kingdom of flat bread."

He didn't say anything.

"That thing," said Nessa. "The one you're going to measure. It did that. It ate a kingdom's lamps. Ate them right out of the ground and flew off with them in its belly, the boatmen say, and left the place dead behind it." She took the sweep again. "And you're going after it with your glasses to write down how big it is."

"It's important to know..."

"I know it's important." She put her weight on the sweep, and the *Heron* swung. "I'm not stupid. I know it's important, and I know you're the only one who can do it, and I know you've wanted to do something like it since you were six and took the back off Da's compass to see where the north was kept." She looked at him. "I'm asking you something else."

---

The owl was looking at her.

It had turned on its perch, at some point, and it was looking at her, not at the water. Two small warm lights in the dusk, steady, honey, the colour they'd always been. Her mother's owl. It had screamed all one night for something nobody could see, and then gone to sleep at dawn with its eyes shut as if it had been told it could stop, and in the morning it had eaten four sprats.

On the hill behind her the glasshouse was a red mouth in the dark, blowing three hundred and twelve new glasses to put back on the shelves.

At home on the nail inside the door their mother's salt-bag was hanging, with the salt still in it.

Nessa took one hand off the sweep and laid it on the little tin serpent on the prow, cold under her palm, its tail in its mouth, and left it there.

"You're going to measure the thing that ate a kingdom's lamps," she said. "Have you thought about what it'll eat next?"
