---

In Solenne's low city they say the king's olifants are the oldest family in the kingdom, older than the king's own, and they say it on the quays, where the king's men don't come down.

It is true, in its way. The first of them were brought out of the Sunward Hills as calves, small enough to lead on a rope, in the years when the first Bond-King was new and wanted something large to stand behind him. Nobody wrote down who brought them, or how many died on the road, or what the hill herds did when they found them gone. The calves were put in a yard under the landward wall. They grew. They bred, and their daughters bred, and the yard is there still, and the herd in it, nine mothers deep.

Grey, and nearly hairless, with ears like the sails of the fishing boats and trunks that can lift a cask or a child. In war they stand in front of the crown's army, and on the great feasts they walk at the back of the crown's procession, painted, and the rest of the year they haul the crown's stone and the crown's wine up the harbour road from the quays. Three of them, the best, live on the cliff in the royal stables with the chosen beasts of the court and have riders, chosen men of the court. The rest live in the yard, and the low city keeps them, as it always has: olifant-boys, the sons of olifant-boys, who sleep in the yard's loft and know every cow by her walk before she comes round the corner.

An olifant remembers, the hill people say. Where the water is, and which way the road goes, and where her mother lies. The king's olifants remember nothing of the kind. They have been in the yard too long. They have kept the old tongue, the low one you feel through your feet more than hear, and they talk in it half the night. Nobody in the low city has ever known what about.

---

She talked with her feet.

That was where the yard was. Not in her eyes, which were small, and saw the gate and the wall and the shapes of the others well enough by day and not much else. In her feet. The yard was a floor of brick laid by men long ago and worn into hollows where cows had stood, and under the brick was packed earth, and under the earth the rock of the hill, and everything that happened in the yard came up through all of it into the soft pads of her feet, and up her legs, and into her chest.

The boys on the loft boards over the stalls. Cart wheels in the lane. The young bull in the bull-yard next door, moving from foot to foot all night because he wanted to be somewhere else. And the herd. Every cow in the yard, standing, lying, leaning on the wall with her calf against her, and every one of them speaking into the ground, so low that the boys asleep above them only turned over and dreamed of thunder.

*Here,* the herd said, all night, one and then another. *Here. I am here.*

It was most of what they said. It was enough. You said it, and the others said it back through the brick, and you knew where each of them stood without turning your head.

---

At dusk the eldest said the lines.

She was the oldest cow in the yard. Her ears had gone to rags at the edges and one tusk was worn short from leaning on the wall. When the light went out of the sky over the wall and the boys hung the lantern on the gate, she set her four feet square on the brick and spoke, and the yard went still in the ground to listen.

*I am here,* she said. *Of my mother. Of hers. Of hers.*

On, back, each mother a long note and a breath, mother after mother. Then the last.

*Of the small one who came down.*

Then nothing. The line stopped there, as the harbour road stopped at the water.

One by one the others said theirs. Each cow her own mothers, back and back, and each line came at last to a small one who came down, and stopped. There had been several small ones. The lines went back to them like the lanes of the low city going down to the shingle, many lanes, one shore.

She said hers when it came to her. Her mother. Her mother's mother, who had leaned on the gate-post until it went smooth. On, back. And the small one.

Her calf was against her foreleg. He was two winters old and could say *here* and not much else, and he said it now, into the brick, too high, so that it came out of him as a squeak and the boys in the loft laughed.

She put her trunk over his back.

*Here,* she told him, low, to show him where the sound should be.

---

There was one road.

It went out of the yard gate and down the lane between the mud-brick houses, where the walls were so close her sides brushed them and the low city's children ran under her belly shrieking. Down through the low quarter, turning and turning, to the harbour road. Along the harbour road under the white walls, with the sea on the other side slapping the stones. To the quays at the sea-gate, where the casks were, and the stone from the ships, and the men who shouted.

Then back.

The full sledge behind her going up, hauled on the chain from her breast-strap. The sledge empty and rattling behind her coming down. Her boy at her ear on the way up, and the long pull at the lane's turn where the ground leaned, and the smell of the sea going behind her and the smell of the yard coming down the lane to meet her: dung, straw, wet brick, the bull. Up and down. Morning, and again after noon.

She knew every stone of it. She knew where the harbour road dipped under the sea-gate and held water after rain. She knew the drain-cover in the lane that rang. She knew the place by the old fishing-stair where the road came onto the shingle through a gap in the low cliff, because they turned there, every time, and went back.

It was a road that came back. She had never thought there was another kind.

---

Her boy smelled of fish-oil and onion, and brick dust, and the black root he chewed that made his breath sweet and his teeth brown.

He had come to her when he was small. She remembered him small: a voice down by her knee, a hand slapping her leg, an older boy laughing and lifting him onto her neck for the first time and him gripping her ears so hard it hurt. He was longer now. His legs hung down behind her ears to her shoulders. He still gripped too hard.

He had sounds for her. A short one that meant walk. A long one, held, that meant stand. A falling one that meant down, and she knelt for that, front legs first, so the boys could load her back or climb on. And a sound that was only hers, two notes, the first heavy and the second lifted, which he said when he came into the yard in the morning before he said anything else.

*Bruna.*

She knew it meant him coming. She didn't know it meant her. She turned her head for it anyway.

He talked to her all day. On the road, on her neck, in the stall at night with his back against her side and his feet in the straw. Long streams of sound, up and down, fast and slow. She didn't know any of it. She liked the hum of it through his back. She liked that it never stopped.

---

On still nights the hills talked.

Not often. A few times in a season, when the wind dropped and the ground was dry and the sea was flat. Then, late, when the boys were asleep and the lantern on the gate had burned down, it would come. Out of the hills behind the city. Far. Up through the rock under the yard, faint, so faint you felt it in the bones of your feet and nowhere else.

The old tongue. Their tongue. The same.

*I am here,* the hills said. *Of my mother. Of hers.*

She understood that. Every cow in the yard did. A line, said into the ground by a cow a long way off, back and back through her mothers. Longer than any line in the yard. So long that the eldest would shift on her feet before it was done.

Then the hills said other things.

Not *here*. Other words. Long ones, slow, made of many parts. She could feel that they were words. She could feel the shape of them come up through the rock: a slope, going up and leaning to the left. A stone under the feet, broken into steps. A cold, a particular cold, of water coming out of rock into a hollow. Steps between one thing and the next, counted in the ground.

Places. That was what the hills talked in. Places, said by saying the ground of them.

She had never stood on any of that ground. Nobody in the yard had. So the words came up into her feet and had nothing to stand on, and went out of her again, and she was left with the shape and nothing in it. It was like a boy holding a fig under her trunk and then eating it himself, and her left with the smell.

The eldest answered them. Every time. She set her feet and said the yard's lines up into the rock, toward the hills, one after another, all the mothers back to the small ones who came down.

The hills went quiet a long time after that, each time. Then they said a place again. Slowly, as you'd say it to a calf.

The eldest had no place to give back. She said *here*. The hills said *where*. She said *here*.

After a while the hills stopped asking, and only talked among themselves, far off, about water and slopes and the cold of stone, and the yard stood in its hollows in the dark and listened, and didn't understand, and listened.

---

Her mother had gone up the hill.

That was how the yard said it. Up the hill, to the stables on the cliff, to stand in a marble stall with gold on her tusks and the bright people's beasts all round her. *She went up.* It was the highest thing the yard could say of anyone.

It happened like this. A man came into the yard when she was a calf, a young one, with a black beard. Not a boy. A man from the white city, in a coat with gold thread on it, who smelled of horses and scent and something else that she had no name for and the herd had no name for, a brightness, as if he'd been standing in the sun. He came to the gate every day for a season and stood. He didn't talk to them. He stood.

One morning her mother walked across the yard to the length of her chain and put out her trunk over the gate and laid it on the man's head.

He stood very still. The boys stopped what they were doing. Somebody ran for somebody.

After that her mother went up the hill.

She came down on drill days. Painted, sometimes, red and gold on her ears, with the tips of her tusks gilded, and the man with the beard on her neck. She smelled of marble dust and sweet oil and of some heavy furred beast that lived in the stall beside hers. She stood in the yard and said *here* into the brick, and the yard said *here* back, and it was her mother's voice.

But there was something new in it.

A word. One the yard didn't have. She said it often, low, between her other words, and it meant him. The one on her neck. *The one who sits.* And after it, sometimes, she said things that weren't the old tongue at all, or were the old tongue bent round some other thing, as if what the man thought came into her and out through her feet before she could make it fit. The yard didn't follow. The yard waited for her to finish.

The beard went grey. Then white. The man got thinner, and sat her neck more carefully, and on drill days he talked to her the whole time, quietly, as her boy talked to her. She leaned her head round to him when he did.

She never came back to the yard to stay.

---

On drill days they made the stand.

Her mother and the other two came down the harbour road from the cliff, and the boys opened the yard's gate wide, and the whole herd came out into the open ground below the wall, between the yard and the last houses, where the earth was beaten flat. The three went to the front. The herd lined up behind them. Side to side. Shoulder to shoulder. And then her mother said it.

Not *here*. The other word. The one under all the others, so low it was barely a sound at all, held, and held, and held, until it was in the whole herd's feet at once and there was no telling whose it was.

*Stand.*

They stood.

Trunks up. Ears out wide. Feet planted. A wall of them. And the men came at them from the white city, shouting and banging pans and running with flags and drums, and the boys ran at them screaming, and somebody threw a pot of fire on the ground in front of them once, and nobody in the wall moved.

That was the stand. It was the one thing the yard did that the hills didn't. She was proud of it, as a beast is proud: it went into her chest and stayed there. When it was done and the men went away laughing and slapping each other, the herd turned and went back into the yard and said *here* to each other all night, louder than usual.

She had never stood in war. Nobody in the yard had. Her mother hadn't. The stand was a thing they had always practised for a day that never came, like the boys practising the long sound for stand on calves that only wanted to lie down.

---

The hills went quiet in the dry cold after the long nights.

It was a still night, the first in a long while. She was asleep standing, with her calf asleep against her, and something woke her, and she didn't know what. She put her feet out.

The hills were talking. Far, faint. A line, a long one, back through mothers.

It stopped in the middle.

Not at the end, where a line stops. In the middle of a mother. As if the cow saying it had put her foot down on a stone that turned.

Then nothing from the hills. All night. The next night nothing either.

Instead, from the west, from the edge of the land where the sea was, there was something walking.

---

She didn't know it was walking at first.

It was a pressure. In the ground. Coming up through the rock from very far, from the west, a push and then a long time and then another push. She stood with her feet out and felt it come. Every cow in the yard did. The bull next door stopped moving from foot to foot and stood.

A push. A long time. A push.

It came nearer over days. Up out of the west, along the edge of the hills. The pushes got bigger. They got so big she felt them in her chest, and the brick under her trembled with each one, very faintly, and the water in the trough shivered in rings.

Feet. They were feet. She knew it the third day. Something walking, a long way off, with feet so heavy each one was the ground moving.

Then, one morning, it lay down.

She felt it go down. On the ridge above the bright people's house, on the cliff, behind the city, where the hills came down to the sea. A long slow settling, like a cow kneeling, that went on and on, and then stopped, and then didn't push any more.

It lay there.

After that the ground leaned. All of it. Toward the ridge. Not so you'd see it. So you'd feel it, standing still: a pull in the rock toward that place, always, day and night, as if the ridge were a trough and the whole hill were draining into it.

The calves wouldn't lie down. The eldest turned to face it and stood facing it day and night, and after a while the others did too, without being told, and the boys came in the mornings and found the whole herd turned toward the west wall of the yard, and laughed, and then stopped laughing.

---

The sweetness came up after that.

It had always been there. She had never known it was anything. It was the ground: the warmth in the brick on a sunny afternoon, the something under the warmth that made the yard a good place to stand. She had been born on it. Her mothers had been born on it.

Now it grew.

Every day a little more. It came up through the brick into her feet like warmth, and then more than warmth. Sweet. It was sweet. It was like the carob pods the boys left in the trough in the rain until they foamed, and the calves got into them and went silly and butted each other and fell over. That sweet. Then sweeter.

The calves were silly all the time. Her own butted her leg and squealed and ran in circles at the end of his chain until the chain caught him short and sat him down. The young bull next door trumpeted at night, on and on, for no reason, and the boys threw things at the wall. The eldest stood with her trunk curled tight against her chest and her eyes shut.

The lantern on the gate burned white.

It was a tin thing with oil in a dish and a wick, and it had burned yellow every night of her life. Now it burned white. Every night whiter. So white it threw the shadows of the gate-bars across the yard as sharp as lines cut in the brick. One night it cracked its glass with a little bright sound and the boys took it down and stared at it.

Her boy came in the mornings now with his face drawn tight. He talked to her faster than usual. He kept looking up the hill.

---

There was a smell at the end of the street.

It came up the lanes from the edge of the low quarter one morning about when the thing lay down on the ridge. Faint, then every day. Stone. Old stone, cold. And under it a beast, a cat, but not any cat the yard knew from the bright people's processions. Bigger. Older. A smell like the bull's musk if the bull had been made of rock.

The eldest lifted her trunk to it every morning and held it up and said into the brick, *What.*

Nobody answered. Nobody in the yard had a word for it.

It didn't come near. It lay somewhere at the end of the street, and the smell of it lay over the lanes, and the low city's children went down there every day, and came back up past the yard chattering, with mud and ochre on their hands.

---

At noon one day the thing on the ridge stood up.

She was in the lane with the sledge, coming up from the quays with stone, her boy on her neck. She felt it in the middle of a step. The pull in the ground that had leaned toward the ridge since it lay down rose up and stood. All at once the rock under the whole city tilted the other way and back. Her foot came down on the brick of the lane and the brick wasn't where it should have been.

She stopped. She planted her feet.

Her boy shouted at her. The short sound, walk. She didn't walk.

It was coming down.

Off the ridge. Toward the cliff. Toward the bright people's house. She felt every foot. A push that came up through the lane into her and shook the sledge on its chain behind her, and the doors of the houses on both sides banged in their frames, and a pot fell off a roof and broke. Then another. Down the hill. Onto the cliff.

A bell began in the white city. Then many bells.

Then it was still. It had stopped. It was standing on the cliff, on top of the bright people's house, and everything under it was bearing it up, the whole rock of the hill, and she could feel the rock bearing it, straining, as her own legs strained under a full sledge on the turn.

Then, somewhere deep, a long tearing. Far down under the cliff. As if the rock had been holding something for a very long time and had let it go.

And then it was gone.

Not walked. Gone. The whole of it lifted off the cliff at once, and the rock under the city sprang up under her feet, light, so light she staggered, and the pushes didn't come again. Nothing walked away. It had gone up. She felt it go through the air instead of the ground, a long pressure passing over the yard like the shadow of a cloud, going away north and west over the hills, and then not even that.

The sweetness came up behind it.

---

It came up out of the ground like water out of a cracked cistern.

All of it. Everything that had been growing since the thing lay down, and more, from somewhere under the cliff where the tearing had been, up through the rock and the earth and the brick and into her feet, so much that it went past sweet into a roaring in her legs.

She trumpeted.

She didn't mean to. It came out of her. And the herd in the yard trumpeted too, all of them, up the lane and over the wall, and the bull broke something next door, and her boy on her neck was shouting and hitting her ear with his open hand and she didn't feel it.

Every lamp in the low quarter went white. She saw them through the doorways in the lane, in the houses, in the dark backs of the rooms at noon: dishes of oil, rush-lights, the stub of a candle on a sill, all burning white, too bright, and cracking their holders.

Her boy got down off her neck. He took her by the ear and put his face against her face and talked. Fast. On and on. His breath sweet with the black root. She felt his voice through her skin, shaking.

She stood for it. After a while the roaring in her legs went on but she could walk on top of it. He took her up the lane to the yard, the sledge still on, stone and all, and left her in the gate with it and ran.

---

For three days the white city burned.

She smelled it before she saw it. Smoke coming over the wall, and in the smoke everything: straw, and timber, and the sweet oil the bright people put on their beasts, and gilt going black, and beasts. Beasts burning. A cat. A hound. She knew the smells from the processions, the bright people's chosen beasts in their collars, walking ahead of the yard on feast days. She smelled them burn.

Ash came down in the yard. Grey, soft, on the brick, on her back. The calves tried to eat it.

She stood with her feet out and felt for her mother.

The stables on the cliff were a long way off, across the white city, at the far end, east of the bright people's house. Too far to hear. Not too far to feel. She put her feet down hard and said into the brick, *Here.* Up, toward the cliff. And waited. And in a while it came back, faint, through the burning, through all the stone of the white city between: her mother.

*Here.*

And under it, the other word. *The one who sits.* Close. Her mother was saying he was close.

Morning and evening she called, and her mother answered. Morning and evening, under everything.

The beasts in the cliff stables were screaming. She could feel that too: a jarring in the ground from that end of the city, hooves on marble, something big throwing itself against a wall. Her mother wasn't one of them. Her mother stood.

---

The sea heaved.

She felt it in the yard, up at the top of the low quarter, a long way above the water. A slow lift in the rock. Then a slow settling. Bigger than any wave. The whole bay rising and falling once, like a cow breathing in her sleep, with no wind behind it.

Then again, an hour later.

Each time the boys in the lane stopped what they were doing and looked down toward the shingle. Each time the low city went quiet and then loud.

Somewhere down there, under the cliff, under the bright people's house, something was turning over.

---

Two mornings after the thing went up, the light came.

She was facing the west wall. They all were. The sweetness was so thick in the ground by then that she had stopped feeling her own feet in it, and the eldest stood with her eyes shut and her trunk curled up against her chest and hadn't eaten for two days.

The sky over the west wall went white.

Dawn was behind her, over the eastern end of the bay, grey. This was in front of her. Over the wall, over the white city, over the cliff where the bright people's house had been. The sky went white and then gold, and the shadows in the yard turned round and lay the wrong way, toward her, and the brick under her feet grew warm, at dawn, in the winter.

And something stood up out of the sea.

She couldn't see the sea from the yard. She saw the light. Rays of it, going up over the wall and out across the sky in every direction, white and gold. And in the ground, under the light, a weight rising, nothing like the pushes of the one on the ridge: one long rise out of the water, slow, with the sweetness pouring into it and out of it at once, as if it were the sweetness, standing up.

The eldest knelt.

Front legs first. Down onto the brick, slowly, with her chain clinking, her head toward the light. Nobody had said the falling sound. No boy was down on the brick to say it. She knelt.

The cow beside her knelt.

Then the next. Down the whole yard, one after another, the herd folding its forelegs and going down toward the west wall, toward the white sky, with nobody telling it to.

She felt her own legs go.

She didn't decide it. It wasn't hers to decide. Her front legs folded under her and her chest came down onto the warm brick and she was kneeling, with her calf beside her, kneeling too, his small head down, not knowing what he was doing any more than she did.

Next door the bull knelt. She heard his chain.

They stayed down a long time. The light didn't go out. In the lane outside the gate, somebody was crying, and somebody else was saying the same few sounds over and over, high, and she heard the boys come down out of the loft and stand in the gate and look at the kneeling yard, and none of them made a sound at all.

---

The unicorns came that afternoon.

She felt them first. The ground had been so full of the one great weight that she didn't notice the small ones until they were close. Feet. Hundreds of feet. Light and quick and many, on the terraces above the yard, coming down. Like rain on the yard roof. Like the first big drops, and then all of it.

They came down past the back wall.

She saw them over it. Lean grey beasts, deer-boned, long in the leg, with a single horn each, coming down through the olive terraces out of the hills in a stream that didn't end. Grey and silver and darker grey, some nearly black. Leaping the terrace walls. Not stopping. The olive trees shaking as they went through. And at the bottom of the terraces, the city's wall, and they went up it and over it, the first and the next and the next, into the white city, toward the light.

She had seen one before. The one on the feast days, chalked white, at the head of the bright people's procession with the crowned man on its back, its feet ringing on the stone. The yard walked at the very back of that procession, painted, and in all the years she had walked behind it, it had never smelled of anything. These smelled of everything. Of the hills, of cold leaf-mould and resin and stone, and of fear, and of something else, a hard bright smell like the air before a storm.

None of them looked at the yard. They went past as if it wasn't there.

One of them stopped on the last terrace above the back wall. Old. Nearly black, gone grey at the muzzle. The tip of its horn was gone. It stood a breath, looking down at the city, at the light, with its sides going in and out. Then it went over the wall after the others.

The rain of feet went on into the dusk. Then it stopped. Down in the city, toward the water, she could feel them, hundreds of them, very still now, standing in something that moved.

---

Her boy came in the night.

The lane was full of feet. Everyone in the low city walking at once, down, toward the shingle, and back up, and down again. Carrying things. She could feel in how they walked that they were carrying things too heavy for them. Voices in the lane, all night, high and low. A word over and over that she didn't know.

Her boy slept against her side for the last of the dark. He didn't sleep. She felt him not sleeping. His breath went fast and slow and fast, and he had his hand flat on her side, and every time the bay heaved he pressed it harder.

Before dawn he got up.

He went up the ladder into the loft and came down with everything that was up there. The last of the carob. The bean straw. The olive branches the boys cut on the terraces for the calves. Armfuls of it, down the ladder, across the yard, in heaps in front of every cow, more than they ever had in a day, more than they could eat. The other boys were doing it too. She smelled their fear and the dust off the straw.

He came to her last.

He put the biggest heap in front of her. Then he stood in front of her with his hands on her face, one each side, under her eyes, and put his forehead against her trunk where it began, and talked.

She didn't know any of it. She never had. It went on and on, low, and it shook, and there was salt in it, on his face, wet, against her skin. She smelled the salt.

"I'll come back," he said. "Bruna. I'll come back. They're saying the island. Everyone. My mother says. I'll come back when it stops."

She knew her sound, at the beginning. Not the rest.

He let go of her face. He knelt down by her near forefoot, where the chain was, and took hold of the iron pin that fixed the chain to the stake in the brick, and pulled at it. It didn't move. It had never moved. It was rusted in. He pulled until his breath went ragged, and hit it with the flat of his hand, and pulled again.

A woman's voice in the lane. High. His sound, his own, the one the women called him by. Again. Closer.

He looked up at her. He got up. He went to the gate and pushed it open, all the way, back against the wall, and wedged it there with a brick.

Then he went.

She smelled him go down the lane. Fish-oil and onion and the black root and salt. Down toward the water. She held her trunk up after it until it was gone into all the other smells of the low city going down to the boats.

---

At sunrise her mother came down the harbour road.

She knew it at once. She would have known that walk in a herd of a thousand. The long stride, the near hind set down a little heavier than the off, as it had been since her mother slipped on the quay stones years ago. Coming down. From the white city's sea-gate, along the harbour road under the walls, toward the low city's end. Toward the shingle.

And the other two with her. Their walks too. And on each of the three, a man.

And in front of them, many feet. People, running. Beasts, frightened, hooves and paws, too many, too fast, all coming down the harbour road toward the shingle in a crowd that filled the ground with its noise.

She put her feet down hard. *Here,* she said. Down the hill. *Here.*

Her mother didn't say *here*.

She came to the place where the harbour road came onto the shingle through the gap in the low cliff, where the yard always turned and went back. She stopped there. The other two stopped beside her. They turned. All three of them. Across the road. She felt them do it, feet moving, side to side, shoulder to shoulder, until they were three planted things in the ground where the road was narrowest.

And her mother said the other word.

*Stand.*

Low. Held. Held. Coming up out of the shingle and the harbour road and the rock of the low city and into the yard, into every cow's feet at once, so that the whole yard went still as stone.

The stand. The real one. Not a drill. There were no men with drums. There was the crowd in the ground, coming down the road at a run, all its feet, and it struck the three of them and stopped.

---

The yard stood up.

It didn't decide to. The eldest moved first, and then all of them: up off the brick, to the length of their chains, side to side, shoulder to shoulder, facing the open gate. The whole herd, in a yard built for standing in rows. Trunks up. Ears out. Calves underneath, squealing, pushed into the middle by their mothers' legs.

They made the stand. In the yard. With nothing in front of them but a gate wedged open on a brick and an empty lane going down.

She stood in it with her calf between her forelegs and her ears out so wide they ached.

She didn't know what for. It didn't matter. Her mother had said it.

---

They held it all morning.

Through the ground she felt what was happening down there, in pieces, as you feel a cart going down the lane without seeing it. The crowd, pressed up against the three. One of the three stepping back, a step, and feet going through, a few, and the step forward again. Then again. People going down onto the shingle, a few at a time. Boats. She could feel the boats, faintly: a scraping, many scrapings, on the stones at the water's edge, and then nothing as each one floated.

Her mother didn't move.

Mid-morning the bay heaved again, bigger than all the others. A long lift of the whole rock, so long the yard's brick tilted under her and the water slopped out of the trough, and down by the water the low cliff over the fishing-stair cracked. She felt it go. A grinding. A great piece of the hill letting go and beginning to slide, with houses on it, brick and beams and people's pots, down toward the shingle.

And then, in the slide, a weight she knew.

The smell at the end of the street. That weight. She had felt it lying there for days, at the edge of the low quarter, heavy and still as a rock in the ground. Now it went onto the fishing-stair and stopped and braced against the slide, and she felt the slide lean on it. And then, beside it, out of the cliff itself, another weight came up out of the rock that was the same weight. And another.

Three of one thing. Holding a hill.

The eldest said *What* into the brick, very low.

Nobody had a word for it.

The slide stopped. It leaned on the three and stayed. Down on the shingle, the boats went on scraping and floating, scraping and floating, all morning.

---

At noon a few feet came down the harbour road, carrying something. They went through the three, through a step back and a step forward, and onto the shingle, and into a boat. She felt it float.

Her mother said *here*, once, then.

Not to the yard. Down. To the shingle, to the boat. As you'd say it to a calf going away from you. *Here.* As if to tell it where she was.

---

In the afternoon the sweetness went out of the ground.

She felt it start. At the far end of the bay. The thing that had stood up out of the sea was moving, she had felt it moving all day, turning in the water, its weight shifting in the rock. Now it walked. East, along the bay, and up. Out of the sea into the river at the bay's end, and up the river's valley, north, into the hills. Slowly. The ground under its feet bearing it as the cliff had borne the other one.

And the sweetness went with it.

As it went, the ground emptied. It went out of the brick under her feet like water out of the trough on the day the boys tip it to scrub it: all of it moving one way at once, toward one end, and then the end of it running out over the lip and away, and the trough left wet and bare. Up the valley. North. After the thing in the river. Following it.

She stood in the yard, in the stand, and felt it go out of her feet.

Then there was nothing.

Ground. Only that. Brick, and packed earth, and rock. She had never felt it before. She had been born on the sweetness, and her mother, and her mothers' mothers back to the small ones who came down, and she had never once stood on ground that was only ground.

It was cold.

That was the first thing. A cold in her feet that had never been there, not in the coldest night of the coldest winter, coming up through the pads and into the bones. She lifted one foot and put it down. Lifted another. The cold was everywhere.

The calves stopped squealing. Her own stood between her forelegs and didn't move. Next door the bull, who had been throwing himself at the wall since the light came, went quiet as if he'd been struck.

The eldest put her trunk down and touched the brick with the tip of it, carefully, as you'd touch a calf that was lying too still.

---

Down at the water, her mother said something.

This time it was the other word, the one no yard cow had. *The one who sits.* And after it, a thing that wasn't a word at all. A sort of stopping. A puzzlement in her feet, as if she had reached for something in the ground that was always there and found it gone.

*The one who sits,* her mother said again. *Is small.*

She didn't understand it.

Then her mother said *Stand.*

And stood.

---

The beasts came up past the yard after that.

These were the bright people's beasts. She smelled them coming up from the harbour road's end, through the vines on the slope, past the yard's side wall, going up into the hills. A cat, big, tawny, with silk on it that dragged, and the smell of fear coming off it so strong the calves cried. Two hounds, panting, with something trailing behind them that rang on the stones. One of the tall white bright-people's horses, the kind with too-long legs, screaming, going up the terraces in great uneven leaps, and a man's voice shouting after it, far below, and then not shouting.

They went up into the hills and didn't come back.

She stood in the stand, in the cold.

Down at the water the three stood too. All afternoon. A step back and a step forward, and feet going through, fewer, and fewer. Then none.

They stood anyway.

---

At dusk the boys didn't come and nobody hung a lantern on the gate.

There was no lantern. It had cracked in the white days and nobody had mended it. The yard went dark as the sky went dark, and the sky over the west wall, where the white had been, was only sky. Over the white city the smoke stood up red underneath from the fires.

Nobody said the lines.

The eldest stood with her feet out on the cold brick and didn't begin. The yard waited for her. She didn't begin.

And then, from the hills, close, something said *I am here.*

---

Close. Nothing like the faint thing in the bones of the feet on still nights, from far behind the city, that you had to hold your breath to feel. Right above the yard. On the terraces. Coming up through the rock so strong that every cow in the yard turned her head at once toward the back wall.

*I am here,* the hills said. *Of my mother. Of hers.*

A line. A long one. Longer than any line the yard had, back and back through mothers, so many that she lost count of them, so many that the eldest shifted on her feet and then went still again and listened. On and on. Each mother a long note and a breath.

It came down the last terrace as it spoke. Feet. One set. Heavy, but not like the yard's feet. Careful, and sure, and very slow, setting each foot down as if it knew exactly what was under it. As if it had been down this way before.

Something stood at the back wall.

---

She was old. Older than the eldest. Lean, with her skin hanging in folds off her hips and her ribs showing under it, and her ears torn to fringes along the edges, so that the dusk came through them. One tusk broken off at half its length long ago, the end gone smooth. The other long and yellow and uneven, never sawn, never capped, curving a little to the left.

She smelled of the hills. Thyme, and the sticky rock-rose that grew on the terraces above the vines, and goat dung, and cold water. And olifant. An olifant smell, but other. Not straw, not the yard, not brick dust and the boys' hands. Wind. She smelled of having been in the wind all her life.

She put her trunk over the back wall and held it there and went on with her line.

Down, back, mother after mother, until the yard was so still that the dust didn't move on the brick. And then, very far back, among the oldest of them, she said one mother differently. Slower. Lower. Heavier on the feet.

*Of the one whose small one was taken down.*

Then she went on. On, back, past it, to mothers before.

The eldest made a sound.

A sound in her chest, not a word, that came up through her feet into the brick and into all of them, a sound she had never made, and the cow by the gate made it too, and then the cow beside her, and then the yard.

*The small one who came down.*

The end of every line in the yard. The place where it stopped. And here it was, in the middle of somebody else's line, not at the end at all. Going on past it. Mothers before.

---

The eldest walked across the yard.

She went to the length of her chain. The chain came taut. She stopped.

Then she leaned.

She had never leaned on it. Nobody in the yard had. You went to the end of the chain and the chain stopped you, and that was where you stopped. She leaned on it now, all her weight, slowly, forward, toward the back wall, with her head down and her trunk out toward the wild cow's trunk over the wall.

The stake came out of the brick.

It came out as a root comes out of wet ground after rain, all at once and easily, with a lump of brick and lead on the end of it, and the eldest stumbled one step forward with the chain dragging and the stake clanking behind her on the floor. And stood. And looked down at it.

Then she went on to the back wall and put her trunk up and touched it to the wild cow's.

---

The wild cow said a place.

Into the ground, slowly, a part at a time. A slope, going up and leaning to the left. A stone under the feet, broken into steps. A cold. Water coming out of rock into a hollow.

The word went into her feet and had nothing to stand on.

The wild cow said one other thing after it. A short one. She knew that word. Every beast in the yard knew it, because the calves said it to their mothers in the night.

*Come.*

---

The cow by the gate pulled her stake.

Then the one beside her. Then the whole yard, all at once, leaning, as the eldest had leaned, and the stakes coming up out of the brick one after another with their lumps of lead on them, and the chains dragging, a great clanking and grinding in the dark, and the bull next door roaring.

She leaned on hers.

It came out so easily that she nearly fell. All her life she'd stood at the end of that chain. She'd thought the chain was the yard. She'd thought the stake was in the hill. A lump of old brick and some lead.

Her calf was against her leg. She put her trunk over his back.

The yard went out of the gate. The eldest first, round from the back wall, out of the gate where the brick still wedged it open, and the wild cow came along the outside of the wall to meet her at the corner, and they went side by side up the beaten ground below the wall toward the terraces. The others after. One by one, then in a crowd, their chains dragging behind them through the gate and ringing on the drain-cover in the lane.

She went to the gate with her calf and stopped in it.

---

She put her feet down hard.

*Here,* she said. Down the hill. Toward the water. *Here. Come.*

It went down through the empty low city, through the cold ground that was only ground. Down the lanes, past the dark houses. To the harbour road. To the gap in the low cliff where the road came onto the shingle.

Her mother answered.

*Here.*

*Come,* she said. *The hill one. The line. The small one who came down. Come.*

A long time.

*The one who sits,* her mother said.

Nothing after it. Only that. The word no yard cow had. And then, held, low, so low it was barely in the ground at all:

*Stand.*

Beside her mother the other two said it too. *Stand.*

They didn't move.

She stood in the gate. The herd was going away up the terraces behind her, she could feel every one of them, their feet getting further, the clank of the chains. Her calf leaned on her leg and said *here*, too high, a squeak.

She said *Come* once more, down the hill.

*Stand,* said her mother.

She went.

---

The terraces were steep and the olives on them were grey in the dark and smelled of nothing. The wild cow didn't go up by the boys' paths, the ones the olifant-boys climbed in the mornings to cut branches. She went up across the terraces on a line of her own, slantwise, through the trees, stepping up over the terrace walls where they were low and along them where they weren't, and the yard went after her in a long string with the calves in the middle and the chains dragging.

And under the turf, under the olive roots, there was stone.

She felt it on the third terrace. Her foot came down and there was something under the soil that wasn't soil. Flat. Hard. Laid. Stones fitted to stones, like the brick of the yard but bigger, and older, and sunk under the earth so long that the olive roots had grown over them and through the cracks between them.

A road.

Nothing she had ever walked. A road going up, under the terraces, buried, slantwise across the hill, and the wild cow was walking on it.

Up. Leaning to the left.

She knew that.

---

She knew it.

The word. The place-word the wild cow had said into the ground at the back wall. The one the hills had said on still nights, all her life, that had come up into her feet and had nothing to stand on and gone out of her again. A slope going up and leaning to the left.

This was it. She was standing on it. The shape had ground in it now. It was under her feet.

She went on up it with her calf, and the word stayed.

Then the stones under the turf broke into steps. Wide ones, worn hollow in the middle, so old and so worn that the hollows had filled with earth and grown grass. Steps. She felt each one. The second part of the word.

She stopped on them.

Behind her the cow with the torn ear stopped too, and pushed her, gently, with her head, and she went on.

---

The road turned at the top of the steps.

She turned with it before the wild cow did.

She didn't know how. She was behind the eldest, who was behind the wild cow, three cows back, in the dark, and the road turned to the right along the hill, and her feet turned to the right. Before she'd felt the wild cow's feet do it. Before there was anything in the ground to tell her.

Her feet knew.

She stood on the turn with her calf against her leg and the yard pressing up behind her and felt it. Not in her head. Lower. In the bones of her feet, the ones that had been cold since the afternoon. There was something in them. A turn, here, to the right, along the hill. It had been in them all along. It had been in the feet of her mother and her mother's mother, back and back, standing on brick in a yard, and never once set down on the ground it fitted.

The small one who came down had come down this road.

She stood on the turn and the eldest, ahead, stopped and turned her head back. *Here,* the eldest said, telling her, not asking.

She said *here*. She went on.

---

The water was where the word said it would be.

Under a split rock at the top of the terraces, where the olives gave out and the hill went on bare. A rock as big as the yard's gatehouse, split down the middle by something a long time ago, and out of the split, cold, a spring. And under the spring a trough.

Stone. Cut. Square-edged, with a lip, long enough for four olifants to drink side by side. Hands had made it. Long ago. Its edges were worn round by trunks.

The wild cow drank.

Then the eldest. Then the rest of them, four at a time, the calves pushed in between their mothers' legs, and the water so cold it hurt her teeth. Colder than the yard's trough had ever been, colder than the sea. A particular cold.

She stood with her trunk in it and felt the word close round her. All of it. The slope leaning left. The steps. The cold. Water coming out of rock into a hollow. She had every part. The shape was a place now, and she was standing in it, and it was where the word had always been.

She put her feet down on the stone by the trough and said it.

The place-word. Into the ground. Her first. Slowly, getting the parts in the wrong order and starting again, as the calves said *here*.

The wild cow lifted her head from the water and listened. Then she said it back. Right. The parts in order.

She said it again, right.

---

They went on up after the water. Onto the ridge.

The road went along the top. Wide. She could feel it plainly here, the stones under a thin skin of earth and grass, going along the ridge to the north, toward the cold side of the hills. On one side the hills going away inland in the dark. On the other, far below, the bay.

And then the road was broken.

She felt it ahead before she reached it. The wild cow slowed. The stones under the turf stopped being fitted and started being thrown about, tilted, cracked, pushed up on edge. And then they went down. The whole ridge went down. A long hollow pressed into the top of the hill, as long as the harbour road from the sea-gate to the shingle, and wide, and deep, the turf crushed into it and the rock under the turf cracked, and the old road gone into it in pieces.

It stank. Old cold iron, and stone that had been pressed until it broke, and something else, very faint, that made every beast in the herd stop and stand and none of them say anything.

This was where the weight had lain.

All those days. Lying on the road, on the ridge, with its whole weight on the old stones, and the hill draining toward it.

The wild cow went round.

Slowly, off the road, on the bare hill, along the edge of the hollow, setting each foot down carefully on the cracked ground. The yard went after her. Nobody went into it. At the far end the road came out of the hollow, in pieces at first and then fitted again, and the wild cow stepped back onto it, and so did the eldest, and so did she.

She felt the stones come right under her feet. Fitted. Laid.

The herd closed up behind her on the road.

---

At the top of the ridge, past the broken place, she stopped and turned round.

The wild cow went on a little and then stopped too and waited, as a lead cow waits. The others went past her, one by one, the eldest and the torn-ear cow and the young ones and the calves, dragging their chains along the old road into the dark. Her calf stood against her foreleg.

Below, the city.

She could see it, a little, with her small eyes. The white city on its terraces had gone black in streaks, and fire stood up out of it red in a dozen places, and smoke over all of it, lit from underneath. The bright people's house on the cliff was broken, and the cliff under it gone, and where the cliff had been there was raw rock going down into the bay. The low city was dark. The bay was dark. Out on the water, on a rock, a very small yellow light.

And far away up the river's valley to the north-east, past the end of the hills, a glow. Going away. Getting smaller.

She put her feet down on the old road.

*Here,* she said. Down. All the way down the hill, through the ridge and the terraces and the empty yard and the dark lanes, to the harbour road, to the gap in the low cliff where the road came onto the shingle by the water.

It was a long way. The ground was only ground now, and cold, and carried less than it had. She waited.

It came back. Faint. Up through all of it. Three of them.

Planted. Side to side. Shoulder to shoulder. In the road by the water, in the gap, with the shingle behind them and the boats gone from it, and the crowd gone, and nothing in front of them but the dark harbour road and the burning city. Still standing.

*Stand,* her mother said, very low. To her, or to the road, or to the one who sat on her neck.

She stood on the ridge a long time with her feet on the old stones and felt them standing.

Then she turned and went on along the road after the herd, with her calf at her foreleg, north, in the dark, and the wild cow went ahead of her, and every few steps her feet knew the next stone before she reached it.

Far down, under all of it, by the water, three of them were standing in the road.
