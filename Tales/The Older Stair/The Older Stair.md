---

At Cotter's Cross they said afterwards that he'd come up out of the ground, and that much was true. They said he came up alone, under the Ruin, the morning after the great one woke, and walked south with the river's mud still on him. They said a good deal more than that. Most of it was wrong. He didn't put it right. A story about you is a thing other people are building, and if you take a stone out of it they only ask why that one.

What happened was smaller, and had more steps in it.

---

He'd paced it at dusk.

Eleven of his own strides from the iron boss to the back of the chamber, across the carved rings, to the place where the letters on the wall ran round a cut in the rock no wider than a man's shoulders. He'd walked it once with the lantern while Delling's men watched the passage, and once again in the dark, with the lantern behind his back, to be sure. Eleven. He'd stood in the cut and put his hand out into the cold beyond it and felt the ledge with his foot and come back.

Nobody had asked him what he was doing. Delling's men didn't ask things. That was what they were for.

Now he walked it with the floor lit white under his boots.

The line ran from the rim to the boss, every ring of letters stopped in it, burning, so bright he could see the bones of his own feet through the leather if he'd looked down. He didn't look down. One. Two. The farmer was behind him on his knees on the stone, held there by the grinding, and the red dragon was in the broken dome with rock coming down round it, and none of that was on the count. Three. The Fellow lay on the edge of the disc where he'd fallen, on his side, with his white hair across his face. Four. Corvin stepped over his hand.

His wrist hurt.

He noticed it at seven. The farmer's grip. It had been on him while he said *two* and *one* and while the key turned, a barley farmer's whole weight hanging off his arm, and he hadn't felt it then, because the candle had been the only thing in the room. He felt it now. Five places, round the bone, hot.

Ten. Eleven.

The cut.

He went into it sideways, as he'd practised, with the lantern in front of him, and his foot found the ledge, and behind him the floor went.

---

He heard it go. He didn't turn round.

A sound he had no other sound to put beside. Not a crash. A great letting-go, the whole disc dropping away out of its seat with a noise like breath going out of something enormous, and then the ring of rock round it cracking off after it, and then the passage, and the ledge beyond the passage, and over his head, through all that stone, the tower and the fort and the arch with the verse cut over it, going down together into the dark. The cut he stood in shook and held. Grit came down on the lantern. The flame bent and stood up again.

Behind him somebody shouted.

Then a man came into the cut at a run and nearly went past him off the ledge, and Corvin put out his arm, the hurt one, and the man hit it and stopped.

Tull. One of Delling's. Grey stubble, a pike-man's shoulders, no pike. He stood on the ledge with his back flat to the rock and his mouth open.

Another one behind him. Young. He came through the cut on his hands and knees, because the ground at the lip of the chamber had gone out from under him as he jumped, and he'd caught the edge of the cut with both arms and dragged himself in. He lay on the ledge and made a sound in his chest.

"Up," said Corvin.

The young one looked at him. His face was grey with dust. Corvin knew it. He'd been one of the four on the farmer's arms.

"Up," said Corvin again, and held the lantern out over the steps so they could see them, and went.

---

The cavern under the river had been dark for three hundred years.

It was lit that night, for the first time since anybody's grandfather's grandfather, and it was lit four ways at once. There was the white of the writing inside the chains, six lines of it going down into nothing, each link burning on its inner curve where no one was ever meant to see. There was the orange of the fire still caught along the rim of the hole far above, where the island had been, with the night sky over it and stones still dropping through. There was the red dragon's fire, below, coming and going among the falling rock. And there was one small yellow lantern going up the far wall, back and forth, carried by a man who didn't look at any of the others.

The stair had been cut into the face of the cavern wall itself. No rail. No wall on the open side. Steps a little wider than a man, worn hollow, climbing across the rock in long slants, turning back on themselves, climbing again. Corvin went up them with his left shoulder to the rock and his eyes on the next step and nowhere else.

Behind him the two men came on hands and feet.

"Don't look at it," Tull said to the young one. "Gil. Look at the step."

Gil looked at it.

Corvin heard him look. Heard the breath go into him and stop. Below and behind them, a long way down, the rock had stopped falling, and in the quiet after it there was the sound of a dragon roaring, close to the ground, and then another sound, lower, under it: the six chains, all at once, going taut.

"He's alive," said Gil. "The farmer. He's on the stones down there, by the old one. The red's come down to them. He's alive, he's moving, he's…"

"Walk," said Corvin.

"He killed Danny."

"I know."

"He put Danny's own…"

"Gil," said Tull. "Walk."

Corvin went on. Three steps. Four. The slant ended and turned back on itself and he turned with it.

He didn't need to look. He'd seen the farmer's face a hand's breadth from his own, and the farmer's hand on his wrist, and the farmer's eyes when the key went round anyway. He knew what was on the stones down there. A man with no halter on him, and three dragons, and a door he'd meant to keep shut standing open under his feet.

*A halter,* he'd said. *That's what you are.*

Somewhere under the far bank, at the same moment as his own, the other floor had turned. He'd felt it through the boss. Through his knees. The same grinding, the same breath, as if the whole rock between the two locks had been one stone and somebody had turned it with both hands. He'd lifted his head to listen to it and it had been there, exactly where it should be, exactly when.

*My brother has the same candle.*

He'd smiled. He knew he had. He'd felt his own face do it.

He climbed.

---

They'd gone round a shoulder of the rock a while before, where the stair bent away out of sight of the floor, and the dragon-light below had gone with it.

The first chain went when they were most of the way up the open wall.

He felt it before he heard it: a jolt through the steps, sharp, as a cart stops dead when its wheel drops in a hole. Then the sound came across the cavern, a crack like a tree going in frost but bigger than any tree, and after it a long grinding scream of iron through stone.

He stopped and looked.

He hadn't looked at anything else. He looked at this.

Across the dark, near the top of the cavern, by the ragged hole, one of the six lines of white had come loose. It came away from the wall at its upper end and fell. Not fast. It was too long to fall fast. It went down through the dark in a great slow swinging curve, every link of it lit inside, white after white after white, and as each link went past the ones below it the whole lit line folded and dropped and kept dropping, a long way, longer than he'd have thought the cavern went, and the sound of it hitting the bottom came up to them a long time after it had gone out of sight.

"One," said Corvin.

Tull was pressed against the rock with his eyes shut. "One what?"

Corvin didn't answer. He turned and went on up.

He knew what it was. He'd known it before the stone stopped shaking. There'd been a reckoning, and it had been his, done over years at a table with a candle, and in it the chains came off one at a time and as each one came off a hand went onto the beast in its place. That was the whole of it. Two locks, two hands, and then the halters: the lion at its door, the farmer with his three, and binders enough on the hill to do the rest. You didn't let a thing go out of a chain into nothing. You let it go out of one grip into another.

The halters were on the cavern floor, or they were somewhere under the school, or they were nowhere. Nobody's hand was on it.

And the chains were coming off by themselves.

The stair turned off the open wall into the rock. A doorway, square, with the hooked letters round it in rings, and beyond it the dark of a passage going up. He went in. The wall came round his right side as well as his left. The two men came in after him, and Tull sat down on the first step inside with his back to the rock and his hands between his knees and said, "Gods. Gods," and Gil stood in the doorway looking back.

"Gil," said Corvin.

Gil turned round.

"Inside," said Corvin. "Or out. Not in the door."

Gil came inside.

---

After the open wall the stair went straight.

Long flights, cut true, as if whoever had made them had laid a line on the rock and followed it. Then a landing, square, with carving on its walls. Then a turn, and another flight. The steps were deep and wide, wider than they needed to be for a man, and worn in the middle into shallow troughs, and the lantern lit five or six of them ahead and none behind.

The walls were cut all over in rings. Letters inside rings inside rings, the same hooked hand as the verse on the arch, row on row, flight after flight. In places there were shapes among the letters, beasts or men, he couldn't tell which in the lantern-light and didn't hold it up to see. He'd have liked to read them. There wasn't the oil.

He counted the landings. He did it under his breath, without moving his lips, as he'd counted the notches down the side of the candle. Four. At five the flight was steeper. At seven there was a crack across the treads where water had come in once and gone. Each of those was where it should be.

Behind him Gil said, "How far?"

Nobody answered him.

"How far is it? How do you know where it goes? There's no light. There's nobody been down here. How do you…"

"Save your breath for the steps," said Tull.

"I want to know how he knows."

Corvin went on climbing. After a while Gil stopped asking.

---

The second chain went on the ninth flight.

This time they didn't see it. They felt it come through the rock, a blow from below and a long way off, and then the sound came after it, up the stair behind them, and it was a different sound in here, shut in, without the open dark to spread into. Thunder in a cellar. It went on and on, rolling up the flights, and then it stopped.

"Two," said Corvin.

The third came while they were resting. He'd stopped on a landing because Tull's knee had gone and Tull wouldn't say so, and he'd turned the wick down until the flame was a bead, to save the oil, and they sat in the near-dark with their backs to the carved wall. The third chain came up through the stone under them and through their backs, and the dust came down off the carving in a fine fall that ticked on the lantern glass.

"Three."

"Stop that," said Gil. "Stop counting."

"Leave him," said Tull.

"Why's he counting them? What does it matter? It's all coming down. The whole river's coming down on us, we're under the *river*…"

"We're under rock," said Corvin.

Gil was quiet.

"We've been under rock for four flights," said Corvin. "The river's behind us." He turned the wick up a little, and stood. "Come on."

---

The fourth and fifth went close together, on the long flights after the hall.

The hall came first. The stair brought them up into it through a doorway, into a square room with a high roof, and the lantern couldn't find the far wall. Seven doorways round it, theirs among them, every one with the rings of letters cut round the lintel, every one with steps beyond it going down. Corvin walked across the hall without stopping, through the dust, to the place on the far side where an eighth way went up out of a corner and turned. He didn't need the lantern for it. He knew where it was.

Tull stood in the middle of the hall with his bad knee and looked round at the seven doorways.

"You've been here," he said.

Corvin started up the stair in the corner.

"You've never been here," said Tull. "Nobody's been here. Look at the dust." He came after him slowly, on the bad leg. "Who told you?"

Corvin went on up. Tull didn't ask again. He was a man who'd been sixteen years in the companies, and he'd learned what you got for asking a question a second time.

Then the fourth chain, and very soon after it the fifth, and the stair shook under them, and they put their hands flat on the walls until it stopped.

"Four," said Corvin. "Five."

The sixth was a long time coming.

They were on the seventeenth flight when it came. It was smaller than the others, from where they were, a deep far shudder and a sound like a door shut at the other end of a long house. And then nothing. No more. The quiet came up the stair after it.

Corvin stood on the step with his hand on the wall.

There'd always been a hum in this rock. He'd felt it from the first step off the ledge without thinking about it, as you don't think of the sound of your own blood. A low steady note in the stone, coming up through his boots, going through him, so constant he'd taken it for silence. Now it was going. He felt it go. Not all at once. It ran down out of the stone under his hand, slowly, a cask emptying, until there was nothing under his palm but cold rock and his own pulse.

"Six," he said.

He stood there a moment longer.

"What does it mean," said Gil. Not a question. He was too tired to make it one.

Corvin took his hand off the wall.

"It means it's loose," he said.

---

The river came in a little after.

They heard it first. A long way behind and below, so far it was only weather: a roar that didn't stop, that grew. Then the air began to move. All night the stair had been dead air, cold and still, his breath going up straight in front of the lantern. Now a draught came up it from below. Cold. Wet. Smelling of the river, of mud and weed and snowmelt, as if the Wend itself had found the bottom of the stair and was blowing up it.

The draught grew to a wind. It pulled at their coats from behind. It went past them up the stair, moaning in the carved rings.

It put the lantern out.

The dark was total.

He heard Gil make a sound, and Tull's hand going about on the stone for his tinder-box, and finding it, and the click and click of the steel, and nothing catching in the wind.

"Leave it," said Corvin.

"We can't go up in this."

"I can." He put the dead lantern down on the step, and his left hand on the wall, and found the edge of the next step with his foot. "Hold my coat. Gil, hold his."

He felt Tull take hold of the back of his coat, and after a moment, a pull lower down the line: the boy, holding Tull's.

"Twenty-two steps," said Corvin. "Then a landing. Then thirty."

He started up.

It wasn't hard. He'd thought it would be. There was nothing to see, so there was nothing to look at. There was only the wall under his palm and the edge of each step coming up under his boot, where it should be, and the count. Twenty-one, twenty-two. The landing, flat, four paces, the turn. Thirty. He said each one under his breath. The wind pushed at their backs. Behind him Tull breathed through his teeth, and behind Tull the boy was crying, without any sound, only the breath of it.

At the forty-first step of the long flight the ground heaved.

It came up under them like a floor in a ship. The whole stair lifted, and dropped, and lifted again, and somewhere overhead, very far off and very deep, stone split with a long tearing sound. Tull's fist dragged at his coat. Corvin stood with his hand flat on the wall and his feet on the step and waited.

He knew what it was.

The heave went on. It went on longer than anything he'd felt. Then it went away, north and east, through the rock, as thunder goes off over hills: further, and further, and less.

He waited until it was nothing.

"Forty-two," he said, and went on.

---

The steps stopped at a stone.

He put his hand up in the dark and there it was: the roof come down to meet the stair, flat and rough and cold, and wet. Water was coming round its edges. He could feel it running down his wrist into his sleeve.

"Here," he said. "Your backs."

The three of them stood bent on the top steps with their shoulders under the stone and pushed.

It didn't move. Then it did. A hand's breadth, with a sucking noise, and mud came down through the gap onto them, mud and water and something like turf, and light. Grey light. Corvin turned his face away from it and pushed. Tull swore steadily in a Wendmouth voice. The slab tilted, and slid, and they got it up on its edge and over, and it went down on the ground above with a thud he felt through his feet.

He climbed out into the morning.

---

Mist.

It lay on the ground knee-deep, white, so thick he couldn't see his own boots in it. He came up out of the hole into it on his hands and knees in mud and running water and stood up, and the mist was round his legs and over it, very high, very pale, was the sky.

The sun was coming up.

In the north-east, over the hills, a long way off, something was flying.

He stood in the mud with the water running out of his sleeves and looked at it.

It was too big to be far away and it was far away. Grey. Moving against the dawn with a slow, enormous beat of wings, and under the wings a shape that was a lion's and was a hill's. Going away. Every beat took it further over the hills. As he watched it was a mountain in the sky, and then it was a hill, and then it was the size of a hawk, going away north-east into the light.

Behind him Tull came up out of the hole on his belly and lay in the mud.

Gil came up after him and stood and saw it and was sick.

Corvin watched it until it was a bird. Then a speck. Then nothing. Then the place where it had been, which was sky.

He'd counted it down to *one* with his hand on the key. Ten marks. He had stood in the dark and knelt on the boss and watched a flame come down the wax, and at every notch the world had been exactly what he had said it would be, and now it was empty sky over a line of hills, and the sun coming up into it as on any morning.

He stood a while longer.

Then he turned and looked at the hole. Steps going down into the dark, with the drain-water pouring into them.

"The stone," he said.

Tull lifted his head out of the mud and stared at him.

"Put it back," said Corvin.

They put it back. It took the three of them, and it went down over the steps with a sound like a lid. Corvin knelt in the mud and pulled the turf back over its edges with his hands and pushed the mud in after it, and the drain-water ran over all of it and smoothed it, and in a little while it was mud and water and turf like the rest.

He stood up.

He didn't look round. He knew where he was. The mist was on everything, and there was a wood somewhere to the west, he could smell it, oak and bracken and wet leaves, and nobody was looking this way. Everybody on this bank, everybody on both banks, had just watched the sky.

"Walk," he said.

---

They lay up all day in the wood.

Bracken, old oak, the ground soft with leaf. He found a hollow where a tree had come down years ago and its root-plate stood up like a wall, and they lay in it under the bracken, wet through and filthy, and nobody came. Deer, once, at the edge of the hollow, a hind and her yearling, who looked at them and went away. Far off through the trees, at the end of the morning, there were bells. They rang a long while and stopped.

Gil slept. He went down as if somebody had hit him and lay curled with his face in his arm. Tull didn't sleep. He sat with his back to the root-plate, rubbing his knee, and looked at Corvin.

Corvin sat with his right hand in his lap and looked at the wrist.

Five marks. The farmer's four fingers and the root of his thumb, round the bone, black now under the dirt. When he closed his hand the marks went pale and came back.

"Your brother," said Tull.

Corvin looked up.

Tull had been in the camp on the island. He'd been at the fire under the arch, three nights back, when the boat came over from the Eelweir with the lanterns and the crowd cheering behind it, and Cassian standing up in the bow. He'd seen them stand under the arch together. All of Delling's men on the island had seen it. Nobody had said anything. Delling's men didn't.

"Under the school," said Corvin.

"Did he…" Tull stopped. "It turned. You said. Down there. Both of them."

"It turned."

"Then he…"

"It turned," said Corvin.

Tull looked at him a moment longer. Then he went back to rubbing his knee.

Corvin looked at the wrist.

He'd felt it turn. That much he had. The same breath, through the rock, exactly at the mark. Whatever else had been under the school, whatever had come down the stair at Cassian or not come, his brother's hand had been on the key at *one*.

There'd been a place agreed. If it went wrong. There'd been two, in fact, one on each bank, and a night for each. He'd made the list himself, at a table, a long time ago, and Cassian had laughed at it and learned it.

He took his eyes off the wrist and looked up through the bracken at the light coming down through the oaks.

The first of the places was on this bank, and its night was tonight, and every knight left in Hearthmere had just watched what they'd been sitting on for three hundred years get up and fly away, and would be looking, now, for somebody to hang for it.

The second was across the river.

He'd go to the second.

---

At dusk they went down to the water.

The wood ran out into reeds and the reeds into the river, and the river was high and brown and very fast, faster than he'd ever seen it, carrying things. Whole branches. A great limb of oak with the leaves still on it, turning over and over. Planks. A barrel. A dead dog. The island had come down the Wend all day.

There were boats in it too.

They'd come off the reach above, where the Restoration had been crossing and fighting when the thing came up out of the river, and the men in them had sat with their oars shipped and watched it go, and then got out wherever they could and left the boats to the water. One had drifted into the reeds a little below the wood. A broad river-boat with the six painted small on its bow, half full of water, with one oar still in it.

They baled it with their hands and with Tull's cap. Corvin took the oar.

He'd rowed on this river as a boy. Not much, and not well, and not where anyone could see. You didn't fight the Wend. You let it take you down and you worked across it as it took you, a little at a time, and it put you on the other bank a long way below where you'd meant to land, and you walked back.

Halfway over, Tull, baling, lifted his head and looked back at the bank they'd left. It was going dark from end to end.

"Where were we?" said Tull.

Corvin pulled on the oar.

"Hearthmere," he said.

"I know that."

"Then you know," said Corvin.

---

The first night above ground they spent on the Saltreach bank, in the lee of a fish-shed somebody had left in a hurry, a long way below where they'd meant to come ashore. No fire. They had nothing to light one with that would light, and nothing to cook on it, and men on both banks to see it.

Corvin sat with his back to the boards of the shed and looked across the river.

All his life there had been lamps over there.

He'd grown up on the far side of that water. He'd stood at the back of the line in Greyhold's yard with his brother and his father and said the noon words at the wall. And at night, every night, from the yard, from anywhere, you could see them: the lamps on Greyhold's walls, white and steady, that nobody filled with oil. The lamps at Redwatch's tower. The cold white of the licensed lamps in the villages, Millbrook and the rest, a line of them down the river like the beads on a tally-frame. He'd counted them as a boy. He knew how many there should be.

There were none.

The far bank was dark from end to end. Here and there a small red spark: a fire, a candle in a window, somebody's lantern on a path, the kind of light a man makes for himself with oil and a wick. The rest was black. The walls on the hill were black. The tower was black. There was nothing over there tonight that a man hadn't lit with his own hands.

He sat and looked at it.

*No ward,* he thought. *No word.*

He thought about that a long time.

---

Tull was digging.

He was doing it a little way off along the bank, on his knees in the soft ground above the reeds, with a knife and his hands. Corvin heard him at it before he looked round. The knife going in and the earth coming up in handfuls, and Tull breathing.

Gil was watching him. He didn't help.

When the hole was as deep as Tull's forearm, Tull sat back on his heels and wiped his hands on his coat.

He didn't put anything in it.

He knelt there over the hole in the dark, with the river going past behind him, and said, "Danny."

Gil made a sound.

"Ferrand," said Tull. "Hobday. Mott. Sallis." His voice was quite flat. "Jem Leckie. Rab Leckie. Carrow." He stopped. "That's eight."

Corvin had known them by their coats. He hadn't known their names. Ten men of Delling's in the chamber, and two had come up the stair, and the farmer had killed one before the floor went, and the floor had taken seven, and the man the farmer had put the brand into, screaming. That was eight.

He watched Tull push the earth back into the hole with the flat of his hands, and pat it down, and kneel on it a moment.

"What's that for?" Corvin said.

Tull looked up.

"The ones you can't get home," he said. "You dig a hole and you say them into it. So the ground knows." He got up, slowly, on the bad knee. "Old company thing. Wendmouth. My first captain did it. You don't put anything in." He looked at the patted earth. "There's never anything to put in."

Gil said, "Danny's mam'll want…"

"There's nothing," said Tull. "She'll want it anyway. You'll tell her you said it. That's all you've got." He looked at Corvin. "Was there anybody of yours?"

Corvin thought.

"The Fellow," he said.

"What was his name?"

Corvin didn't answer.

"His name," said Tull. "For the hole. I'll open it again."

"I never asked it," said Corvin.

Tull looked at him for a while in the dark. Then he went and knelt by the patted earth again, and dug a little way into it with two fingers, and said, "The Fellow," into the hole, and closed it.

Corvin turned back to the river.

He looked at the dark bank. After a while Tull came and sat down by him against the shed, and the boy lay down on the boards, and the river went on carrying the island down to the sea.

He didn't dig anything.

There was nobody to dig for. There was a list, and a second place, across the water from the first, and a night agreed for it. It was this bank. It was tomorrow night.

He'd be at it.

---

He wasn't.

He thought about it at first light, sitting against the shed, and decided, and it took no more time than a man takes to move a stone on a board. The second place was a day's walk up the bank, and there were companies on the Way already, going past it, and his face was the face every one of them had cheered from the hill at Harrow's End, and by tomorrow night the crown would be on this bank too, in boats, looking. And there was a third line on the list, under the two places. He'd written it himself. *If nobody comes, go where the companies are paid.*

Cassian had laughed at that line most of all. He'd learned it anyway.

If his brother came to the second place and found nobody, he'd know what it meant. He'd know where to go.

Corvin couldn't sit in a burned shed and wait for him. Not with the companies walking south with nobody at their head.

He stood up.

"South," he said.

---

The Drover's Way was full of men going home.

They came onto it in the afternoon, a long way below Harrow's End, and it was like coming onto a road the evening after a fair. Strings of men on it, walking. Twos and threes. Whole companies, some of them, with their banners furled or gone, with a cart or two, with the wounded on the carts. Nobody leading them. Nobody marching. They walked as men walk who've been paid nothing and told nothing, and are going wherever the road goes because it goes somewhere.

The three of them fell in among them.

Corvin had his collar up and the river's mud on him to the hair, and a long dirty face like any other long dirty face on that road, and he walked with his hands behind his back, and nobody looked at him twice. He'd had a great deal of practice at that.

He listened.

He heard where they were going. Cotter's Cross, most of them, because it was where they'd been hired, and the hiring yard was there, and if anybody was going to pay them for the winter it would be there. Some of them were going further, to Wendmouth, home. Some were going nowhere, only away.

He heard what they were saying. About the thing coming up out of the river. About the shadow going over the hill, and every man on it going down on his face in the mud. About the ladders on the Hearthmere bank. About the island, gone. About the lamps on the far bank going out all at once in the morning, every one, as if a hand had been closed over them.

He heard them say *Vale*.

They said it the way they said the river and the sky. As if it were weather. *Vale's dead,* one said. *Vale's taken,* said another. *Vale went over on the island and the island went down.* *Vale's got away to the coast.* A man by the side of the road with his boots off, binding his foot, said Vale had been the one on the hill all along, he'd seen him, standing on a cart with his arms up, before the boats went over. Somebody told him he was a fool, Vale went over in the first boat. They argued about it for a while and then they stopped, because their feet hurt, and it didn't matter.

Corvin walked past them.

Nobody asked who was paying.

They would.

---

They slept the second night in a barn with forty others, and in the morning Tull took his belt off.

He did it outside, behind the barn, where nobody was. He sat on a stone and took out his knife and slit the stitching along the inside of the belt, carefully, and from the slit, one at a time, he took coins and laid them in his palm. Corvin stood over him and watched.

Silver. A good deal of it, for a pike-man. Old coin, mostly, worn smooth, Saltreach and Wendmouth and some he didn't know. And among the old, a run of new: bright, hardly worn, with the burr still on some of them.

"Two years," said Tull. "More. Everything I've not drunk." He looked up at Corvin. "You'll need a room. At the Cross. If you're going to the Cross." He looked at the coins in his hand. "You'll need to sit somewhere they can come and see you, and you'll need to be the man that's got the room. I've been sixteen years in the companies. I know what a captain goes up the stairs for."

Corvin looked at the silver.

"You'll have it back," he said.

"Everybody says that." Tull tipped the coins into a scrap of cloth and knotted it and held it up to him. "Every captain I ever had said it."

"Did they pay?"

"Two did."

Corvin took the cloth. He undid it and looked in, and took out one of the bright coins, and turned it over in his fingers.

A ship on one side. A wave on the other. And on the rim, struck small, where you wouldn't see it unless you knew to look, a ring of six dots.

He knew that coin. He'd counted it into bags in a back room at the Ram's Head, and Rook had carried the bags out to a table in the yard and paid the captains across it, one after another, and written every bag down. Ten a man. This was some of Tull's ten.

He put it back in the cloth with the others and knotted it.

"Two did," he said. "Then I'll be the third."

---

Cotter's Cross on the second day after the flight was already full and still filling.

It had always been a rough town. A market where the Drover's Way met the Saltreach Track: inns, a beast pen, tithe barns, a hiring yard where the sellswords sat along the wall in the autumn and waited to be bought. Nobody kept the peace in it but whoever had paid most recently. He'd hired companies here himself in the autumn, sitting in a back room with Delling, and he'd known then what the town was for.

Now there was nobody paying, and the men kept coming.

They were in the streets. On the steps of houses. In the hiring yard and the beast pen and the barns, lying in the straw with their boots off. Company coats on all of them: the red hand, the black ship, the wolf's head, Delling's yellow tower on blue, a green boar, a blue fish. Pikes stacked in corners, crossbows against walls. They'd come in off the Way all day and all the day before, and they were waiting, without anybody having told them to wait, because there was nowhere else to go and nobody else to be owed by.

At the bottom of the main street, the Fleece.

Three storeys, the biggest house in the Cross, with a gallery round its yard and a sign over the door: a fat white ram on a green ground. He'd sat under it in the autumn. He went in under it now with the mud dried grey on his coat and two of Delling's men behind him, and the taproom was so full of men there was no floor, and nobody looked round.

The landlord was behind the board. Dunmore. A big soft man with a big soft voice and eyes like a pair of pegs. He looked at Corvin. Then he looked again, longer.

"You were here," Dunmore said. "In the autumn. With Delling. At the hiring."

"Yes."

Dunmore looked at the mud. At the coat. At the two men.

"I heard you were under an island," he said.

"I was."

Dunmore waited for more. There wasn't any.

"Your top floor," said Corvin. "The big room. Shutters. A lock on the door." He put Tull's knotted cloth on the board. "For a week. And a cask for the yard tonight, on me, and the men in it told who it's from."

Dunmore undid the cloth. He looked at the silver in it, and he picked out a bright coin and turned it to the window, and looked at the rim, and his face did nothing at all.

"Company money," he said.

"Yes."

"Coming back round."

"It does," said Corvin.

Dunmore looked at him a while longer with his peg eyes. Then he folded the cloth over the coins and put them under the board, and reached up behind him and took down a key.

"Top of the house," he said. "And who shall I say the cask's from?"

Corvin took the key.

"They'll know," he said.

---

They knew.

The cask went out into the yard at dusk and the men round it drank it in an hour, and by the end of the hour the whole yard knew whose it was, and by the end of the night the whole Cross did. He didn't go down. He sat at the window of the big room on the top floor with the shutters a hand's breadth open and watched it go through them. You could see it from above. A man at the cask would turn and say something to a man at a fire, and the man at the fire would look up at the house, and then turn and say it to the next fire. It went across the yard and out through the arch into the square and on into the streets, from fire to fire. A thing passed hand to hand along a line.

*He's at the Fleece.*

Behind him Tull sat on a stool by the door with his leg out straight and a jug, and watched him watch.

"They'll come up," Tull said. "The captains. Tomorrow. Tonight, some of them."

"Some."

"What'll you tell them?"

Corvin shut the shutter.

"That they'll be paid," he said.

---

Harker came first. She was the wolf's head's captain, a small square woman with a cropped grey head and a face like a fist, and she came up the stairs alone in the middle of the night without knocking, and Tull got up off his stool, and she looked at Tull until he sat down again.

She stood in the middle of the big room and looked at Corvin, who was sitting at the table under the lamp.

"Well," she said. "It's you."

"Yes."

"We went flat on our faces on that hill," said Harker. "All of us. In the mud. When it went over." She didn't sit down. "I've not done that for a man or a god in my life. I did it for that." She looked at him. "Where's our pay?"

"Coming."

"From where?"

"South."

"When?"

"When it comes."

Harker laughed, a bark, without anything in it.

"That's what the last one said," she said. "Your little man. Your Rook. Week on week on the island. When it comes. And it came, give him that, every week, in bags, till the night it didn't. And now he's dead on a spit, the boys say, and the chest's gone south with Delling, and Delling's sat in the Plough with it on his knees and his spectacles on, telling his own men it's for safe keeping." She stopped. "So you'll forgive me, Vale."

He didn't move.

She'd said it as people said it, the name for the man with this face, and she'd said it to the face. He let it go by, as you let a word go by that a horse doesn't need to hear twice.

"You'll be paid," he said. "Back pay and the winter's. In new silver. Up the Track."

"And what do we do for it?"

"Wait."

"We're good at that," said Harker. "We've been doing it since the morning." She looked round the room. At the lamp. At the shutters. At Tull on his stool. "Then what?"

"Then I'll tell you."

She looked at him.

"You were the one in the back room," she said. "At the Ram's Head. With the little man. Not the one on the steps." She tilted her head. "I always thought you'd have been better at it if you'd ever smiled."

He said nothing.

She went down. On the stairs he heard her stop and say something to someone coming up, and the someone laugh, and then two more pairs of boots coming up past her.

Ivers of the black ship. The green boar's man, whose name he never learned that night. They asked the same things and he gave them the same answers, and they went down and told their companies, and the next night there were more.

---

He wrote south on the third day.

A short letter. He burned the first and wrote it again. He sealed it with nothing, because he had nothing to seal it with, and Tull took it down to a carter on the Saltreach Track who was going to the coast with an empty wagon and asked no questions for a silver piece. It was Tull's silver piece. It came out of the cloth under Dunmore's board.

He didn't say what was in the letter. Tull didn't ask.

---

Gil went north on the second day, and came back on the sixth.

It was Corvin who'd sent him. He'd called him up to the room the first evening, while the cask was going round the yard, and the boy had stood in front of the table with his cap in his hands and his eyes red.

"Do you know the far end of the ferry?" Corvin had said. "Below the school. The Saltreach side."

"The Staithe. I've been through it."

"Go and stand on it. Look across. Look at the gate on the hill." He'd looked at the boy. "Then come and tell me what's on it."

"What'll be on it?"

"Something," Corvin had said. "Or nothing."

Now Gil stood in front of the table again, four days later, with mud to his knees and his cap in his hands.

"There's a man on it," he said.

Corvin waited.

"On the gate. On the wall over it. There's writing cut there, big, a whole line of it, and he's under the writing. In chains. By the arms." Gil's hands were turning the cap round and round. "They put him up the day I got there. The afternoon. A ladder. Ordinary chain, like for a dray. There's a board round his neck."

"What does it say?"

"I can't read."

"No," said Corvin.

"I asked a man. A fisherman. On the Staithe. He'd had it off the ferry." Gil swallowed. "It says *The Restoration's leader*."

The lamp on the table was burning steadily. It was an oil lamp, an ordinary one, Dunmore's, with a smoked chimney. Corvin looked at the flame.

"His face," he said.

"It's too far. Across the water. I couldn't see. I'm sorry." The boy's voice cracked. "But they all say. On the Staithe. Everyone. They say it's the one from the Tidesteps." He stopped. "And there's three dragons lying on the shingle on the far side, below the ferry. Like cattle on a riverbank. And the lion on the jetty. I saw the lion. And next morning a man from the crown come down to the landing with a horn, and shouted a paper off the steps, and the horn was cracked, you could hear it across the water. The fisherman said it's Saltreach folk to go back over. And the crossings'll be shut." He stopped again. He had run out of things. "That's all."

Corvin looked at the lamp a while longer.

"Thank you," he said.

Gil stood there turning his cap. He was waiting for something. Corvin saw it, and saw him not know what it was himself.

"You can go," said Corvin.

The boy looked at the door. Then back.

"I want to go to the tower," he said. "Delling's. In the yard. Delling owes me four months." He said it fast, all together, as if he'd carried it four days to say. "My mam's in Wendmouth and Danny's mam and there's nothing, there's four months, and you don't walk off a debt like that. Not in the companies. You stand under the flag till it's paid or you're nobody's." He looked at the floor. "Tull'll stay. Tull's said."

"Go," said Corvin. "Say nothing in the yard."

"I never say nothing," said Gil, and heard it, and went red.

"He'll say nothing," said Tull, from the door. "Delling's men don't."

Gil went.

Tull, on his stool by the door, didn't look at Corvin. He looked at the boy's back going down the stairs.

---

He sat up that night with the lamp.

He didn't do anything. That was the thing he'd remember of it, after, if he remembered it. He didn't stand at the window. He didn't drink Dunmore's wine, which was bad, or eat the bread, which wasn't. He sat at the table under the lamp in the big room on the top floor of the Fleece with his hands on the wood and the shutters closed and the noise of three companies coming up through the floor, and did nothing at all.

There was nothing to put in a hole.

There was a man in chains under the charter at Greyhold, under the line he'd learned to read before he could read anything else, *the bonded and the unbonded serve as equals,* with a board round his neck. There was the crown, which wanted every company on the Way to look across the river and see it. There was a gate-watch, and a river being shut, and a fort full of knights who had watched the sky. There was nothing he could bring home. There'd been nothing for Tull's eight, and Tull had dug for them anyway, and knelt, and said them into the ground.

He didn't dig.

At some point in the night Tull said, from the door, "You want to say anything."

It wasn't quite a question. Corvin looked at him.

"Over it," said Tull. "There's no hole. You don't need a hole. You can say it anywhere. The ground's the ground."

Corvin looked at him.

"No," he said.

Tull nodded, as if that were an answer he'd heard before, and went back to looking at the floor.

The lamp burned down. Corvin watched it. When the flame got low he didn't turn the wick up, and when it went out he sat on in the dark until the shutters went grey at the edges and the yard below began to wake.

---

Reede came on the eighth day, up the Track road from the river in a cart with a canvas over the back, sitting beside a boy who drove.

Corvin saw him from the window. The cart came in through the arch into the Fleece's yard and stopped, and the little round man in the grey coat climbed down off it carefully, as an old man does, and stood in the yard among the fires and looked round him at the companies. He had his spectacles pushed up on his forehead. He looked like what he was: a travelling bookbinder who mended inn ledgers and talked about history, come to the wrong inn.

Then he took the canvas off the back of the cart.

The press. Iron screw, oak frame, the bar. A crate of type beside it. Paper in oilcloth.

He'd got it over the river.

Corvin went down himself.

---

"Through Millbrook," said Reede, upstairs, with a cup of Dunmore's bad wine in both hands. "There's a boat on the back channel that doesn't ask. It took four of us to get the press in it." His soft voice was hoarse. "Millbrook's half burned. Fennick's barn, the tithe barn, the jetty road. The well's gone sour. The sweet well, under the stone hood. Since the morning it flew. Brown, and stinks. They're going to the Widow Rudd with cups of it and paying her a copper to say words over it and the words don't take." He drank. "Nothing takes over there now. Nothing."

"I know."

"Of course you know." Reede looked at him over the cup. His eyes were wet. They often were; it was how he looked at people. "You were always the one who knew."

Corvin waited.

"I saw him," said Reede.

Corvin said nothing.

"From the landing. On the gate. Before we went for the boat." Reede put the cup down on the table, carefully, as if it might go over. "I didn't go close. I couldn't. But I saw." He took his spectacles off his forehead and held them, and didn't put them on. "He spoke so well. Corvin. He spoke so well. Three thousand on the Tidesteps, and not one of them breathing."

"Yes."

"I wrote some of it. Some of what he said. I've got it in the crate. I thought you'd want…"

"Not now."

Reede looked at him. He saw something, or didn't. He nodded and put his spectacles on.

"What do you want printed?" he said.

---

Reede wanted to say a great deal.

He sat at the table in the big room with a pen and a sheet of Dunmore's paper and wrote, and talked as he wrote, gently, as he talked in the Kettle in the evenings to whoever would sit with him. About the six crowns and the one realm. About the first knights, and what they'd found, and what they'd done with it, and the lamps, three hundred years of lamps, every one of them burning stolen light. About the farmer with three dragons, and the girl with the lion, and the old powers coming back. About the one who'd been hanged. He wrote it all down in his small round hand and turned the sheet round and pushed it across.

Corvin read it.

Then he took the pen and began to cross things out.

Reede watched him do it. He didn't protest. He watched the lines go one after another, the one realm, the first knights, the old powers, the farmer and the girl, the one who'd been hanged, and he took his spectacles off and cleaned them on his coat and put them on again.

At the end there were seven short lines left, and a space at the top.

"That's all?" said Reede.

"That's what they'll read."

"Most of them can't read."

"Then that's what they'll have read to them." Corvin turned the sheet round. "Put the ring at the top. Big."

Reede looked at it.

"And at the bottom?" he said. "A name. They'll want to know who's calling them."

"No name."

"Corvin. They called him Vale. Everyone did. You can't…"

"No name," said Corvin.

Reede looked at him a moment longer, and folded the paper, and went down to the yard to his press.

The next morning the bills were on the doors.

THE LAMPS WERE A CRIME. THE CROWN DRANK YOUR WELLS FOR THREE HUNDRED YEARS AND CALLED IT LIGHT. ASK WHO SOURED THE WATER AT MILLBROOK. ASK WHO DIMMED YOUR CHARMS. ASK WHY THE MARCHES BURN WHILE HALLOWMERE SITS IN THE DARK. THE ONE REALM IS COMING. COME TO THE CROSS.

The ring of six at the top. The back blank.

---

On the tenth day Dunmore painted the sign.

Corvin hadn't asked him to. He watched it from the window: Dunmore up a ladder over his own front door in the morning, with a pot of white and a brush, and a crowd of sellswords below him calling up advice. He painted six white dots round the fat ram's head, in a ring, one at a time, and came down the ladder and stood back in the street to look at it, and some of the men cheered.

Dunmore looked up at the top-floor shutters.

Corvin didn't open them.

That night Dunmore sent up a jug of the good wine without being asked, and the reckoning he'd been keeping under the board, which had gone past Tull's silver on the fourth day and gone on, stopped coming up with the bread.

---

The days went.

The companies kept coming in, off the Way and up the Track, and the Cross kept taking them, as a pen takes beasts until the rails creak. He sat in the big room. The captains came up at night. Harker and Ivers and the rest, and men from the smaller companies, and men who said they were captains of companies he'd never heard of and might have been anybody. They sat at his table and drank Dunmore's good wine and asked when, and he told them, and they went down and told their men. *Wait.*

Delling didn't come. Delling sat in the back room of the Plough across the hiring yard with the paymaster's chest and his account book and his spectacles on a cord, and once a week sent a boy up the Fleece's stairs with a note that said, in a neat clerk's hand, *My regards. D.* Corvin read each note and burned it. He'd known Delling two years. Delling would come up the stairs when there was something on this table worth more than what was on his own knees.

Tull played stones with him in the afternoons, on a board Dunmore found, with the shutters half open for the light. Tull played badly and with great care, and lost, and set the stones up again without being asked, and lost again.

"You never say it," Tull said once, setting up.

"Say what?"

"*You've lost.* Every captain I ever played says it. Every time. You never say it."

"You know you've lost."

"That's not why they say it," said Tull, and put the first stone down.

The marks on Corvin's wrist went from black to green to yellow. He looked at them sometimes, at the table, with his sleeve pushed back. Four fingers and a thumb. By the fifteenth day he had to turn the arm to the light to find them.

He didn't shave. There wasn't any need. He didn't go down.

---

On the fourteenth day, at the window, he watched two wool-carters from somewhere in the north sell their spring fleeces in the square for half what they were worth, and argue with each other about it the whole time, and a sellsword from the blue fish buy one fleece off them to sleep on, and walk away with it over his shoulder.

He watched the shorter of the two carters point after the sellsword and say something to the other. The other said something back. They went on like that all the way to the end of the square.

He shut the shutter.

---

The word came into the yard on the sixteenth day.

He heard it before Tull brought it up. You could hear things from the top floor of the Fleece if you sat still enough: the yard came up through the floor, every fire, every argument, all of it mixed together, and in the mix, if you'd learned to, you could hear when one voice was saying something the others had stopped to hear.

A man had come back over the river. One of the green boar's. He'd gone over, the boys said, the second day after the flight, before the crossings were shut, to see if his sister's family in Penfold were alive, and found her man dead and stayed to put him in the ground and the barley in after him, and come back the long way, round by the fords, and walked into the Cross that morning with a story. He'd been to Greyhold. He'd stood on the landing under the hill and looked up at the gate. And there was a man hanging on it. In chains. With a board round his neck that said *the Restoration's leader*. And the man on the gate had a long pale face and short dark hair. He'd seen it. He'd been close enough. It was Vale's face.

Vale was dead. Hanged at Greyhold.

That was the story. It went across the yard from fire to fire, from hand to hand, and every man who heard it turned his head and looked up at the shutters of the top floor of the Fleece.

---

They argued about it for three days.

He listened to them do it. He could hear every word. In the yard, in the taproom under him, in the square. Vale was hanging at Greyhold. Vale was upstairs. The green boar's man was a liar, or drunk, or he'd seen somebody else. He'd seen the face. He was drunk when he saw it. He'd been three days sober in a ditch with no money for drink, he'd seen it right enough.

Then somebody said it.

Corvin heard who. A voice from the black ship, Ivers's own sergeant, a big man who'd been at the Ram's Head and on the hill and on the Tidesteps before that, at Wendmouth, in the crowd. He said it on the third night, at a fire under the gallery, loud enough for the whole yard.

"There was two."

The yard went quiet.

"Gods," said Ivers's sergeant. "Think. Use your heads. The Tidesteps and Ferriby, the same day. A hundred leagues. And everybody said magic. What magic? There was two of them. One on the steps and one on the roof." He said it with a kind of anger, as if somebody had cheated him at cards and he'd only just seen how. "One's on the gate. And one's upstairs."

Nobody answered him.

Then somebody did, from across the yard, and then everybody did, and the yard was loud again, louder than before.

But it was a different noise.

---

Men began to walk the next day. The captains came up that night all together.

Harker. Ivers. The green boar's captain, the blue fish's, two from the smaller companies. They came up the stairs in a body, which they'd never done, and stood round the table, which they'd never done, and nobody sat.

"They want to know which one," said Ivers.

He was a thin, careful man with a sailor's tar-stained hands, and he looked as if he'd rather have been anywhere else.

"Which one what," said Corvin.

"Which one they followed." Ivers looked at the table. "Which one hired them. Which one they stood on the hill for. On the Tidesteps. At the Ram's Head. They want to know if the man upstairs is the man they went flat in the mud for, or his…" He stopped.

"His brother," said Corvin.

The room was very quiet. Under it, the yard.

"They knew him," said Harker. She didn't look away from Corvin. She never did. "The other one. On the steps. They'd heard him. Half the men in that yard went to the Tidesteps to hear him talk, and came away weeping into their beer, and signed up the next week. Nobody signed up for you. Nobody's ever heard you talk. You've sat up here seventeen days with the shutters shut and sent down *wait*." She folded her arms. "They'll wait for a man. I don't know if they'll wait for a room."

"Some of them are walking," said the blue fish's captain. "Since this morning. Down the Wendmouth road."

"Then hang a few," said the green boar's man.

"You'll hang my men when you're paying them."

"Go down," said Harker.

Corvin looked at her.

"Go down," she said again. "Tomorrow. Stand in the yard. Let them see you. Talk to them." She paused. "Do what he did."

Nobody said anything.

"I can't do what he did," said Corvin.

Harker looked at him for a long moment, and something went over her fist of a face that might in another woman have been pity.

"No," she said. "I know. But they've only got you."

---

He didn't sleep.

At first light he sent Tull down to Dunmore for hot water and a razor.

Tull brought them up himself. A basin, a jug steaming, a razor in a leather fold, a scrap of cloth. Dunmore's glass, a small square of it in a wooden frame, from his own room, which he'd never lent anybody. Tull set them on the table and stood back.

Corvin pushed his sleeves up.

The wrist was clear. He turned it to the window. Nothing. Not a mark. The skin was the colour of the rest of him.

He lathered his face from the jug and opened the razor.

He shaved as he'd always shaved, from the same side, in the same order, without hurrying. Twenty days of beard came off in long strokes. He didn't look in the glass any longer than the razor needed. But the razor needed it, and the face came up out of the lather into the square of glass a stroke at a time: long, pale, the dark hair cut short, the grey eyes. Neither young nor old. It looked back at him with no expression at all.

It was the face on the gate at Greyhold.

He wiped the razor and folded it and put it down, and washed his face in the basin, and dried it.

"Noon," he said.

---

At noon the Fleece's yard was full.

Not only the yard. The arch to the square was jammed with men, and the square behind them, and the street. The fires had been trodden out. Men stood on the rims of the water-troughs and on the roofs of the sheds and on Reede's cart, with Reede among them on the tail of it in his grey coat and his spectacles, ink to his elbows. Men stood on each other's shoulders. Every company in the Cross, red hand and black ship and wolf's head and green boar and blue fish, and Delling's yellow tower on blue against the far wall with Gil under it, and the captains at the front, and behind them more men than he'd counted, and he had counted. It was the most people he'd ever seen in one place who were waiting for him.

They went quiet when the door onto the gallery opened.

He came out alone. Tull stayed in the doorway behind him.

The gallery ran round three sides of the yard at the height of the first floor, with a wooden rail. He walked along it to the middle of the long side, over the arch, where everyone could see, and stopped. He put his hands on the rail.

Below him three thousand men looked up at the face on the gate.

Nobody said anything.

He'd stood at the back of a line all his life. He'd stood at the back of Greyhold's yard with his face to the wall and said the noon words in a voice nobody heard. He'd stood in back rooms and on smithy roofs and under arches in the dark, and moved men as you move stones on a board, and never once in his life had he stood at the front of anything and been looked at. He'd had a brother for that. He'd had a brother who could stand on the Tidesteps and make three thousand people breathe out at once, and he had stood at the back and watched him do it, and been glad, and never once wanted it.

They were waiting for that.

He felt them wait. He could feel the shape of what they wanted, as clearly as he'd ever felt anything: a voice, and arms going up, and the one realm, and tears. The whole yard was leaning toward him for it.

He didn't have it.

Somebody shouted.

A big voice, from the back, from the black ship. Ivers's sergeant. Not angry now. Only asking, loud, for everyone.

"Which one are you?"

The yard waited.

Corvin looked down at them. At the captains at the front. At Harker with her arms folded. At Reede on the cart with his spectacles in his hand. At the boy under the yellow tower against the far wall, with his cap in his hands. At three thousand men who had been told it was one, and had just found out it was two, and wanted him to tell them which.

"Vale," he said.
