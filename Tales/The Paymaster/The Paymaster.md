On the quays at Wendmouth they say that only two men ever know what a voyage cost. One is the master who sailed it. The other is the clerk who never left the room. The master knows what it cost him, in sleep and weather and men over the side. The clerk knows what it cost. The houses on Gallows Quay pay the clerk better, because a master can be drowned and replaced by the next tide, and a clerk who never blots is hard to find.

Rook had never blotted.

He kept the book inside his coat, on the left, in a pocket he had had sewn for it. It was black calf gone soft at the corners, the size of a hymnal, with a brass strap that buckled with one hand. He had bought it new from a stationer behind the fish market the autumn before last, and he had written in it every night since, in a small slanting hand, in the short forms the counting-houses use. A line for each thing that came to him and each thing that went out. A place, a thing, a number, and where there was one, a name. *Received. Paid.* Beside the bags that came up from the coast he drew the ring of six in the margin, small, so he could find them again.

What nobody paid for, he didn't write. A thing that nobody paid for had happened only as weather happens. It went in no one's account.

---

The steward paid him on the third night after the fair, in the tally room off the hall at Varrhold, by rushlight.

He was a sour old man with ink on his fingers who had come up through the Clan's dues-school and never forgiven it. He counted the silver out of a leather bag onto the table, four pieces, and counted them again, and pushed them across with one finger as if they were damp.

"Four silver and keep," he said. "For the winter. Which you've not done."

"No," said Rook.

"Ulric says pay the hired men what they're hired for, so I pay. Twenty-two of you. Twenty-two." The steward wrote in his own book, which was a dues-roll, long, on a stick. "Eating like wolves. And now the coast drover's off to the coast."

"My mother," said Rook.

"Your mother." The steward looked up at him. He had the look of a man who has seen a great many mothers fall sick on the night before a hard week. "And you'll want a horse."

"If there's one going spare."

"There's not. Not in the uncle's lines, not with the meeting tomorrow." The steward put his pen down. "You'll walk, drover. Your mother's had you this long."

Rook smiled. "She has," he said. "She'd tell you I was never worth the wait."

The steward grunted and put the bag away. Outside, across the yard, the Roosts were making their evening noise, a long grinding chorus off the cliffs like ships' timbers in a swell, that the hold had stopped hearing years ago. The two fires burned in the yard, east and west, with the wide dark between them.

"Tomorrow," said the steward, to nobody, rolling up the dues-roll. "Tomorrow the chief's boy shakes hands in a beck and we're all to be friends. He'll be down there at first light, you watch. Standing in the water before anybody's out of bed. He always is." He tied the roll. "He wants to be the first thing they see."

"I'd heard that," said Rook.

He took his four silver and went out.

---

He went down to the lower stable, where it was warm and dark and smelled of horse, and sat on the upturned bucket by the door, and wrote.

There was a lantern on a nail. He took the book out and undid the strap and opened it on his knee to the page, and the ink-horn out of his other pocket, and the pen. He was a neat writer even on a bucket. It was the one skill he had.

*The bucket in Ulric's yard: myself, a week, for the listening. Unpaid. (My own time.)*

He looked at it. Then he wrote under it:

*The drover, Varrhold, paid, for the winter: the uncle's steward, four silver and keep.*

And under that, after a moment, with the pen held off the paper while he chose the words:

*A horse, from the uncle's lines; to the coast for my mother.*

He left the end of the line open. He blew on it, and buckled the strap, and put the book back in its pocket.

The halter was on its nail beside the door, where he had hung it every evening of the week. He took it down and looked at it in the lantern light. A good Clan halter of plaited hide, with the cheekpiece parted where some horse had pulled back on a post. He had sat on the bucket by the foot of the cliff path for seven days with that halter across his knees, and every day he had worked at the splice, and every day it had come apart in his hands, quietly, so that nobody would see. He was a truly hopeless mender of halters. An old groom in the end stall had watched him at it the first afternoon and spat, and had watched him every afternoon after, and spat every time.

He hung it back on its nail, still broken.

---

The farmer had looked at his hands.

He thought of that, sitting on the bucket, with the yard dark outside and the fires low. Not the boy. The boy never looked at anything for longer than it took to smile at it. The boy had come down the cliff path on the last afternoon talking over his shoulder and had turned on the last step, three paces from the bucket, lit up like a lamp in a window, and said it out loud, the day and the hour and the place, as a man calls a price across a quay. He hadn't looked at the man on the bucket. Nobody looked at the man on the bucket. That was the use of the bucket.

The farmer had.

A big slow man from the Saltreach bank with barley dust in the seams of his coat and a sand-coloured dray-mare, who stood at the back of things. Rook had watched him rub the mare down and talk to her the whole while, low, about nothing, and had watched the man watch him back without seeming to. A farmer who looked at hands. Rook had held out his own, in a manner of speaking, and said the thing about sheep, and the farmer had said *Would I*, in a voice with nothing in it at all.

For a moment then Rook had let the face slip. He knew he had. It was a thing he didn't do.

Then the farmer had gone up the cliff with the boy, and come down again behind him, and walked past the bucket looking at the ground, and the knife had been on Rook's belt with the light from the stable door on the pommel. And the farmer had gone home to his barley with his face doing nothing.

A man who looks too long at the hired hands, and says nothing, and goes home. There was one in every house. In Rook's experience they were the safest men in the world. They saw everything and they told no one, because telling would mean they'd been looking.

He hadn't written the farmer down. Nobody had paid for him.

---

Above the stable, on the ledge over the cliff path, something moved on the rock and the stone answered.

Rook went to the door and looked up. The ledge was a black line against the sky. The brazier was out. In the mouth of the cave behind it, low down, two eyes the colour of coals in a damped grate had opened and were looking at the yard.

The rust-red one. The axe-man's.

It had watched him all week. Whenever he crossed the yard he had felt it, the long hot regard coming down off the cliff onto the top of his head. The green one, the girl's, never came to the front of the ledge at all while he was in the yard. Beasts did that with him. Dogs got up and went out of rooms he came into. He had never minded. He liked beasts. They had the sense to dislike what they couldn't account for, which was more than most men had.

"Good beast," he said, softly, to the eyes on the cliff.

The eyes didn't close.

---

He took the horse at midnight. A tall-legged chestnut from the end of Ulric's lines that he had marked on the second day for its feet. The old groom in the end stall was asleep in the straw with his mouth open. Rook saddled the chestnut in the dark without a light, which he could do, though he couldn't mend a halter, and walked it out past the east fire, where the last of Ulric's men lay rolled in their cloaks, and took an axe off the rack there as he went, the long-hafted kind every man at Ulric's table carried. Nobody stirred. At the gate the man on watch was one of the twenty-two, and he looked at Rook and opened it and said nothing at all, which was what he was paid for.

It was the same man who had stood by the gatepost the week before and told three Saltreach boatmen that somebody was going to do a lot of things, soon. Rook had found him at the trough that evening and told him, pleasantly, that a man who talks to boatmen gets talked about by boatmen, from one end of the Wend to the other, and the man had gone white and not opened his mouth since.

There was a moon a few nights past the full, coming up late over the moor, and the Drover's Way went down the glen under it like a strip of linen laid on a dark table. Rook rode it hard.

---

He was not a horseman. He had learned late, at the coast, for the work, and he rode as he did most things, correctly and without pleasure. The chestnut was better at it than he was. It went down the Way at a long steady trot, past the falls on Hollow Beck with their noise coming up white in the moonlight, past the sheepfolds, down off the edge of the moor where the cold came up out of the hollows to meet them. There was frost on the heather. The chestnut's breath went out in front of it in two grey plumes.

Six leagues. He did the sum as he rode, from habit. The boy would leave the hold before light, alone, and ride down at a walk, because he was a boy who rode at a walk when he was happy. He would come over the last rise above the ford at first light. If Rook was there an hour before, that was an hour lying in the gorse in the frost, and he would be cold, and his hands would be slow.

He put his hands inside his coat as he rode, one at a time, against his shirt.

---

The ford on Hollow Beck lay at the bottom of two long slants of road, one coming down from the north and one going up to the south, with the beck between them running wide and shallow over a bed of smooth brown stones. Alders leaned over the water on both banks. On the south slope a shepherd's shieling of turf and stone sat dark in the gorse, with nobody in it.

He rode through the water, which came up cold round the chestnut's knees and made it blow, and up the south slope to the back of the shieling, and tied the horse in the fold of ground behind it where it couldn't be seen from either road. He took the axe. He went down through the gorse to a place above the near bank where he could see the whole of the north road coming down off the moor, and the water, and the alders, and lay down on his belly in the frost with the axe beside him.

The gorse was dry and brown and smelled of nothing much. The cold came up through his coat. He lay there and did small sums in his head to keep his hands from mattering. The steward's four silver. The price of the axe if he'd had to buy it, which at Cotter's Cross would be eleven coppers, or nine to a man who argued. The horse, returned, nothing. A week on a bucket, his own time.

The moon went thin and pale over the west. The sky over the moor went from black to the colour of slate, and then to the colour of a pewter plate, and geese came over in a long wavering line very high up, going south, talking to each other.

Then the boy.

---

He came over the last rise above the far bank on a tall bay, alone, as the light came grey into the glen. He sat easy in the saddle with the reins loose. He had his cloak thrown back, though it was cold, and the grey armband on his arm with its thin line of red, and he was looking at the ford below him as a man looks at a field he has just bought.

He rode down the long slant at a walk. At the water he didn't stop. He put the bay into the beck and came through it, splashing, with the water going up white on both sides, toward the near bank, toward the south road, where his uncle would come down.

Rook stood up out of the gorse.

He stood up slowly, as a man does who has been lying on cold ground, and lifted a hand.

The boy saw him at once. And his face did what Rook had known it would do, because it was the only thing the boy's face ever did. It lit.

"Hoy!" called Teo Varr. He lifted his own hand, high, and waved it. "You're early! Are they behind you? Is Uncle coming?"

Rook came down through the gorse toward the water. He didn't hurry. He had left the axe in the gorse, for later.

"Behind me," he said.

"I knew he'd come." The boy brought the bay up out of the beck onto the stones of the near bank, under the alders. "I knew it. Everyone said he wouldn't, and I knew." He was smiling so hard it must have hurt. He looked at Rook and Rook could see him trying to think of the name and not finding it, and not minding. He had never had to find a hired man's name in his life. "You've come down to hold the horses. That's good of you. That's good of Uncle. Here."

He swung down off the bay at the edge of the water. He was tall and thin and too young for his own face, and his boots went into the shallows, and he didn't notice. He held the reins out.

"Hold him for me? I want to be standing in it when they come."

Rook took the reins in his left hand.

---

It was quick. He had thought about it, on the bucket, and on the road, and it was as he'd thought. Low first, under the ribs, close in, and then under the collarbone, because the first is never enough when a man is young, and the boy's hand went to Rook's sleeve, not to his own knife. That was all. The hand went to the sleeve and held it, as you'd catch at a friend's arm on an icy step.

Then the boy went down at the edge of the water, on his face, with his arms out in front of him, half in the beck and half out of it, as if he had been about to drink and had lain down instead.

He never drew. His knife stayed in its sheath on his belt, the good one with the hound on the pommel. He went into the water like a stone.

The bay threw its head up against the reins and Rook let them go. It backed away from the smell, snorting, and turned, and went off through the ford in a clatter of stones to the far bank, and stopped there under the alders, trembling. Then, being a horse, it put its head down and began to graze.

"Good beast," said Rook.

He stood on the stones with the knife in his hand and his breath going out grey in front of him. The water went over the boy's back and round his shoulders and out past his hands, and it went red, and the beck took the red away downstream over the brown stones as it took everything, and came on clean behind it.

---

He did the rest without hurrying.

He went back up into the gorse for the axe and came down and threw it into the shallows a little way out, where the beck ran fast over the stones and would half cover it. He took the armband off his own arm. Plain grey wool, no thread, the mourning band every man in Ulric's grey had worn since the old chief's pyre, and he had worn it with the rest of them for a month. He reached up and caught it on the low branch of the alder that hung over the bank, at the height of a man's shoulder, as if it had been torn there going past.

He washed the knife in the beck below the ford. Then his hands. The water was so cold that it hurt in the bones, and he held them in it anyway until they were clean, and when he took them out the scar across his right knuckles stood out white as a thread of salt.

He had had the scar since he was fourteen. A strongbox lid in his first counting-house, a heavy one with iron corners, that he had been told to hold open while the master counted, and hadn't, quite. The master had let it come down. Then he had made the boy hold it open again, with the crushed hand, until the count was done. *A box is shut or it is open,* the master had said. *There is no such thing as nearly.*

Rook dried his hands on the inside of his coat and looked at the boy one more time. The long thin face turned sideways on the stones. The eyes open. The mouth a little open, as if he had been about to say something cheerful. Water in his hair.

He had been pleased to see him.

That was the part Rook turned over, standing there. Not the knife. The boy had come down a road in the first light and seen a man with a face nobody kept, and had smiled at him without needing to know who he was. Rook couldn't remember the last man who had done that. It was a thing he could have written down, if anybody had paid for it.

---

Far down the south road, on the moor, something moved.

He saw it from the top of the slope, getting his horse from behind the shieling. Very far off. Two leagues, three, where the Way came up out of the folds of the lower moor in the grey light. A cart. Small as a beetle. With a lantern still burning on it, though there was no need now, a yellow point that went out of sight in a dip and came up again.

Coming up.

The boy had asked for witnesses. Ordinary people from the villages, people the Clan protected, who'd stand on the bank and see. *Bring the miller. Bring the old man with the knees.* Rook had heard every word of it from the bucket. The farmer from Harrow's End had said he'd come.

He looked at the yellow point on the road a while longer. Then he led the chestnut down the back of the slope and away from the Way altogether, across the beck below the ford and down its far side, by the sheep-trods along the hillside where no cart could go. By noon he was three leagues off and below the folds of the hill, and he heard none of it. Not the horns. Not the riders. He saw the smoke, later, a thin white line going up from the gorse above the ford into the clear cold sky. He didn't know who had lit it. It was nothing he had paid for.

He rode down the beck all that day with the smoke behind him. In the dusk, on the hill above Harrow's End, where the beck came out of the trees and the barley stubble began, he got down and took the chestnut's bridle off and turned its head uphill and slapped its rump. It went off up the hillside at a trot, and then a walk, with its saddle on, toward home, as a good horse will. A horse knows its road.

That night, in a reed-cutters' hut on the bank below the Eelweir, by the light of the Captain's fire, he took the book out and found the open line and finished it.

*Returned.*

He wrote nothing else for that day. There was nothing else to write. Nobody had been paid.

---

He spent two days with the Captain's people in the reeds, and on the third he went up into Harrow's End.

It was a village of thirty-odd houses of river stone and tarred timber, with turf on the roofs and a mill on the beck and an alehouse beside the mill, and barley and flax running down to the water. The Clan's riders had been on the green all afternoon, Arn's down the Way from the moor and Ulric's up it from Cotter's Cross, each asking for grain and men and a name. By the time Rook came up from the river they were gone, and the village had gone indoors to argue about it, and most of the arguing was in the Wheel.

He sat in the corner of the Wheel with a cup of small beer and let the room go by him.

The miller was standing at the fire. A loud wide man with flour still in the hair of his forearms, who said every price as if it were the first word of a song. Forty sacks of flour to Ulric's people, he was telling the room. Ulric's silver, and good silver too. To be fetched by the new moon, at night, so Arn's lot wouldn't see it go. "At night," the miller said, and laughed, and tapped his nose, and told the whole room which night.

Rook drank his beer.

Someone at the far table said the farmer at the top of the hill was a fool to sell to both sides and fight for neither. Someone else said the farmer was the only man in the Marches with any sense. A woman said the farmer had been up at the ford and found the boy, and hadn't said a word about it since, not to anyone. They were quiet a moment at that.

"He was dead when I got here," said the miller, in a voice not his own. "That's all he'll say. Like a dues-roll."

Rook finished his beer and went out the back.

---

The man was waiting by the woodstack behind the Wheel, as he had been told to.

He was a Wendmouth man, past forty, lean and quiet, with a long bundle in sacking at his feet and a face that had been out in a great deal of rain. He had come up the Wend on a wool barge a fortnight before, ahead of his company, and had sat in the Wheel every night since, drinking small beer slowly, as men do who are paid to wait. He belonged to Delling. Delling's company would be hired later, all of it, when the people Rook worked for needed a company. This one man had been hired first.

"You heard the miller," said Rook.

"Everybody heard the miller."

"The new moon's a week off. They'll come for it sooner than that, at night, and the old woman on the bronze one won't be far from them. Arn will hear. Arn hears everything. He only listens to some of it." Rook looked at the long bundle. "When the fighting comes up out of the lanes, it will come up onto the fields above. There's a farm on the hill. Fenn's, the barley man's. There's a hedge at the bottom of Fenn's field, and the hedge is thick."

"And?"

"And if two of the three come down, you put the woman on the green one out of the fight."

The man looked at him. "Only if two come down."

"One rider dead is a feud," said Rook. "Two is a bad night. All three, and every dragon they had goes loose at once." He took the bag out of his coat. "Forty."

"And if the two don't come down?"

"Then you've had forty for sitting in a hedge."

The man weighed the bag in his palm without opening it. He was a professional. He knew what forty weighed.

"I've sat in worse hedges for less," he said, and put it inside his shirt.

Rook knew his name, and his captain's name, and the street in Wendmouth he was out of. He wrote it that night, in its place. It was the kind of line that needed its place.

---

He was on the island when it happened.

The Captain had wanted him there, to see the money's worth, and he had gone, and stood at the edge of her hooded lanterns under the oaks with his hands in his pockets while her men laid chain over something he had first taken for a hill. A flank of grey-gold hair, going up into the dark higher than a man. One foot, laid out on the leaves toward the path, as broad as a round shield. The sweet heavy smoke from the pots drifting over all of it. He had stood and watched as he watched everything, and kept his hands where they were, because his hands were no use to anyone here.

On the third stake the foot moved.

He kept little of what came after. A sound like a sack dropped from a loft. A man screaming in his own jaw-trap on the slope. The Captain shouting *back, back, leave it*. He went down to the raft at a walk, which was harder than running and kept him out of the traps, and got onto it, and they poled out. On the way someone found a little skiff in the reeds of the west channel, tied to a stake, and cut its rope, and they watched it turn away into the current.

"Leave it. Whoever it is, they're not going anywhere now," the Captain said, across the water. "We'll come back for them."

"No," said Rook. "We won't need to."

Then, as they came out from under the island's shadow, poling for the far bank, the sky over Harrow's End went orange.

---

He stood on the raft and watched it.

The mill first. Nothing else on that bank stood high enough to burn so. A glow on the underside of the sky, growing, and small sounds coming over the water a league and a half off: shouting, a horn, iron. Then the dragons. Three shapes against the glow, high, enormous, turning. A long jet of fire poured out across the dark, so bright he saw the faces of the Captain's men lit yellow on the raft beside him. Another shape came up to meet it.

He counted them. It was all he could do from a raft. Three, and then the fire, and then three again, and then one was going down, a long falling streak against the orange. Then another.

Then, for a long moment, nothing he could see.

Then the voices. Three of them, coming across the river all together, high and terrible, the cry of every dragon that has ever lost its rider, going up and up into the night. Rook had heard the Roosts every evening for a month. He had never heard anything like this. The men on the raft stopped poling and stood with the poles in their hands.

The three voices went up and up. And then they stopped, all at once, together, as if a lid had come down on them.

*There,* he thought. *They broke.*

He didn't feel anything about it. He seldom did, at the moment of a thing. He felt the sum come right, the small cold click in the back of the head that he had felt all his life when a column added and the total was the total.

Then, behind them, the island roared.

It came up out of the dark under the oaks they had just left. Up out of the ground, through the water, through the logs of the raft and the soles of his good boots and his shins and his teeth, so deep that he didn't hear the beginning of it, only felt it, and then heard it, a sound with no top to it, going out over both banks of the river at once and on into the hills. The raft shook. The water round it went into little standing points, like the skin of a drum. One of the Captain's men sat down hard on the logs and put his arms over his head.

It went on a long while. Then it stopped.

Nobody on the raft said anything. The Captain looked back at the island, at the black mass of the oaks against the stars, with her heavy jaw set, and then she said, "Pole," and they poled.

Rook stood with his hands in his pockets and looked at the orange sky over the far bank, where the voices had been. He had felt the sum come right. Now he stood very still and waited to see whether it had.

---

In the morning they put in at the Eelweir for water, and a boy on the jetty told them.

He was a skinny Clan boy of twelve or so, a herd's boy off the Way who had come down in the night with the news, and he had the important look of a boy who has seen something before anyone else. He told it to the Captain's men while they filled the casks. He told it to the Captain. He'd have told it to the eels.

The three riders were dead. That was true. All three, in the fight at the mill, the boy said. The bronze one's rider in the top of the barley field and the axe-man on the roofs below the mill and the girl at the bottom of the field by the hedge, with a bolt in her neck, and nobody knew whose bolt, because the Clan didn't carry them. And the dragons should have gone after them. Everybody knew they did. They should be dead too by now, or dying, or flown off mad into the hills.

They weren't.

"They're lying in Fenn's field," said the boy. "All three of them. Round him. Round the farmer. He's sitting up in the stubble with his ribs tied, and they're lying round him like dogs round a fire, and they let him put his hands on them." The boy's eyes were enormous. "They say he's bonded. To all three. Overnight. They say there's marks on him."

The Captain laughed, short.

"Which farmer?" said Rook.

The boy looked at him for the first time, and then away, as people did. "Fenn," he said. "Calder Fenn. Up the hill. The barley man."

---

Rook stood on the jetty with the river going past under it and did the sum again.

Then he turned to the Captain, and told her quickly and quietly that the cat was hers to finish. She was to take it as it lay, if it could be taken, and the money for it was at Wendmouth when she brought it in, as agreed. He was done with the cat. The people he worked for, he told her, would pay twenty times what they were paying for the cat, for the three. Twenty times.

The Captain looked at him a long moment with her short hair and her heavy jaw.

"For three war dragons," she said, "bonded to a man you'd have to get past them to get at."

"Yes."

"You want that job?"

"I have it," said Rook, and went up the hill.

---

He was off the jetty inside the hour and up the river lane through the village. There were people in the lanes, a lot of them, more than he'd have expected for a place this size, all going the same way, up the hill, quietly, not talking. He went with them. Nobody looked at one more man in a drover's coat.

The lane came up past the burned mill, still smoking, a black heap where the wheel had been, and on up between hedges toward the farm at the top. The people stopped at the hedge where the lane looked over the farm's lower field, and stood along it, a long line of them, with their arms on the top of the hedge or their hands at their mouths, looking. Rook stood behind a broad woman in a shawl and looked over her shoulder.

The field was black. A great furrow ran down it from top to bottom, as wide as a lane, where something enormous had slid, ploughing the stubble and the stooks up in front of it, and at the bottom the end of a stone byre was down. There was a smell of burnt barley over everything, and of something else, hot iron, and a forge with no fire in it.

And in the middle of the field, they lay.

The bronze one, the old one, with her back laid open in long raw stripes and one eye gone to a dark wet hole. The rust-red one, the axe-man's, with a wing dragged out across the stubble, torn at the root. The green one, the girl's, the small one, lying close, with her narrow head flat on the ground. They lay in a rough ring with their tails round, the three of them, as dogs lie round a brazier on a quay in winter.

In the middle of the ring a man sat in the stubble with his shirt off and his chest bound up in linen. A small girl sat beside him with her knees drawn up.

Rook looked at the man.

A big slow man with barley dust in the seams of his coat, which lay beside him on the ground. He had his hand on the old one's jaw and he was talking to her. Rook couldn't hear what. Low. About nothing, by the look of it. As he had talked to a sand-coloured mare in the lower stable at Varrhold, the whole time he rubbed her down, while he watched the man on the bucket without seeming to.

*Would I,* the farmer had said.

Rook stood behind the broad woman and looked at him across the black field for longer than he had ever looked at anyone. He forgot, for a while, to keep his face. It didn't matter. Nobody at that hedge was looking at anything but the dragons.

The green one lifted her head.

She lifted it off the ground and turned it, slowly, on her long neck, away from the farmer, toward the hedge. Toward the line of people. Her pale eyes went along them. They came to the broad woman in the shawl and stopped.

Rook stepped back behind the woman's shoulder and down the lane.

He walked down past the smoking mill and through the village with his hands in his pockets, and he felt it come right in the back of his head, coldly, and click. It wasn't the click he wanted.

The war-pairs had broken, all three, in one night. The dragons had gone loose, every one, at once. That was what the forty in the hedge had been for. And they had gone, all three, to the one man in the Marches who had looked at Rook's hands.

Nobody had paid for that.

---

That night he wrote by a lantern in the loft of a carrier's barn on the bank road south of the village, with mice going about in the thatch over his head.

He wrote the Captain's water at the Eelweir, which he had paid for, two coppers. He held the pen over the page a long time after that, with the ink drying on the nib, and wrote nothing about the field.

The next morning he sent for the coin man.

---

The autumn went by in roads and bags.

He was good at it. He had always been good at it. It was work for a man nobody kept: to be in a yard at noon and gone by dusk, to sit at the end of a table while other men talked, to put a bag into a hand and take nothing out of it but a word. He went where the bags came up from the coast, the ring on every bag, and he went where they had to go, and between the two he wrote it down.

He did not go to Harrow's End again. Not once. The farmer knew his face. There were not many men in the Marches who could say that, and Rook didn't mean to stand in front of the one who could. So it was the coin man who went up the hill in his good dark coat at dusk on the new-moon night and stood in the farmer's yard while the farmer sat on his mounting block, and offered him new silver with the ring struck small on the rim of every piece, and the farmer looked at the coins and didn't take them. It was the coin man who paid the miller by the black heap of the mill the next day, for the farmer's road and his hour, and came away with them, and handed them on.

*The miller at Harrow's End, through the coin man: the farmer's road and hour, two silver. Paid.*

The men on the road waited where the miller said. The farmer didn't come by it. He went the other way, east, into the Fellwood, on the old one's neck, with the red one walking and the green one over the trees, and the men on the road sat in the bracken till morning and came back cold.

*Two silver, wasted,* Rook thought, and didn't write it. He didn't blame the miller. A road sold is a road sold. It was the farmer who hadn't kept to it.

---

Others wanted the farmer. That was the trouble with him. A man with three war dragons is a price, and everyone in the Six Kingdoms with a purse came up the Wend to name it. A Saltreach captain with spectacles and grey gloves came up with a hundred and twenty men on the Tide Council's commission and sat round the farmer's crag for three days with green-wood fires and bolt-throwers, and the farmer went out through the back of the rock on a wet night and was gone. Rook heard it in a yard at Cotter's Cross and smiled, privately, for the first time in a month. The Tide Council had paid for three days and got smoke. He thought better of the farmer for it.

The price on him went round the companies: two hundred silver for the farmer, alive. The people he worked for were very clear about *alive*.

Then the camps, through the autumn and into the winter. He paid for those as well, for the soup at the front and the blankets, and the rings on their strings that the young men hung round their necks, and the fields at the back with their fences, which he never went into. He bought what went into the fields. Some of it had been bought before, in the summer, at the beast yard in Wendmouth through the men in grey coats, beasts out of the Marches with papers. It was three lines in the summer pages. He seldom looked back at the summer pages. A book is for going forward in.

He was in a camp on the drove road below Cotter's Cross when the report came from the Ewe Beck.

---

It came in the handler's own words, written down by the camp's clerk, who spelled badly. A man out of Upper Rush had come into the beast lines in the night with a halter and a knife, for his grey. The big grey dray-beast with the split ear that pulled the water-sled. The man had put his hand on the grey's face and said its name. The handler had woken. The grey had taken fright.

The clerk had written *took fright* and underlined it, as if underlining would hold it in place.

Rook read it through in the cold tent with the camp's soup smell coming through the canvas. Upper Rush. He knew the name from the summer pages. *Upper Rush, the barns: the red hand, twenty, for the burning. Paid.* He had paid for the barns. He had paid, through the grey coats, for what came out of them.

He had not paid for the man.

*That wasn't meant,* he thought.

The camp had sent the body home. That was the policy, and it was a good policy, he thought; the man at the table who made it was seldom wrong. A cart, a quiet bay, two men in grey with their caps off and a woman with a kind face sitting on the tail. They had taken him up the river lane to his own gate under a camp blanket, with his knife and his good halter, and told his people the beast was bought fair at Wendmouth with papers, and that the one realm grieved with them, and that its door was open. The farmer had been at that gate. The report said so. The report said the farmer had looked at the two men and at the woman a good while, and then let them go down the lane.

Rook paid the camp for the cart.

*The cart from the Ewe Beck: the Upper Rush man, home, with his knife and halter; the camp's bay and two men and the woman. Two silver. Paid.*

He sat with the book open on his knee for a while after that, in the tent, with the soup smell. Then he buckled the strap.

---

Four nights later the farmer burned the Ewe Beck camp.

Not the people. The report was very particular about that, as if the clerk couldn't believe it. The green dragon came down first over the tents, low, screaming, and drove the refugees out onto the beck ice. Then the red one burned the fence and the stores and the binding tent and the handlers' tents. Not one refugee tent. The old bronze one came down at the beast lines with the farmer on her neck. Three handlers dead. The grey with the split ear was taken off on a halter, walking home up the river lane in the dark, empty as a sack.

The young man at the camp with the soft beard had stood on the ice and shouted after them. *Enemy. Enemy of the one realm.* The people on the ice had taken it up.

Rook read that part with some interest. Not the shouting. The tents. A man with three dragons and a reason had come down on a camp in the night and left every tent with a family in it standing.

He thought of a big slow man in a lower stable, talking to a mare about nothing.

He wrote down the price of the binding tent, which would have to be paid for again.

---

Two nights after, a hundred went up the river lane to Upper Rush for the farmer, at ten silver a man, and came back down it by morning with eleven dead on the hill. Rook paid the ten silver a man, because it had been agreed, though the farmer had not been brought. He paid the dead men's ten to their captains, who would keep it.

One of Delling's came back three days late, without his crossbow, in his own boots and nothing else, and went to Delling, and Delling sent him to Rook to say it himself.

Brask, his name was. A lean coast-man with a broken nose. He stood in front of Rook's table in the yard and looked at the ground.

"The dragon took me alive," he said. "In its claws. Off the hill. The farmer let me walk." He swallowed. "He said to tell Delling. What I saw on the hill." He swallowed again. "He said to tell him the price is too low."

Rook looked at him.

Then he laughed. It was so seldom that he did it, aloud, that two men at the next fire turned round.

"Tell Delling," said Rook, "I'll put it to the people I work for."

---

After midwinter the camps came together at the Ram's Head.

It had been a drovers' inn on the Drover's Way, halfway between Harrow's End and the hold, a long low building of stone in a hollow where the cart road from Cotter's Cross came in, with a ram's horns painted on the sign. By the end of the winter it was the middle of a town. Grey canvas, in rows, up the hill behind the inn and down to the beck and half a league along both sides of the Cotter's Cross road. Five hundred fires. A quarter-mile of beast lines along the beck, all standing. Companies with their own flags: a red hand, a black ship, a wolf's head, and Delling's yellow tower on blue. A charm-seller's board of warranted tin by the trough, doing a trade. A woman in a plain grey coat on a stool by Delling's fire, whom everybody went wide of without looking.

Rook paid all of it.

He paid it in the inn yard under lanterns on poles, from a trestle table with the chest on it, iron-bound, its lid up, and the book open in front of him. The captains came up one at a time, and he found them in the book, and wrote, and counted the silver out into their bags. Two of the man's own guard stood behind him, old men of the six who didn't drink, and when he was done they shut the chest and locked it and carried it in between them to the back room of the inn, where the landlord used to count his coppers.

It was the best work he had ever had. He thought so, sitting there in the lamplight with the yard full of men and the column of the day adding as it should. The quays had never given him a table like this.

On the hundred-and-twenty-fourth night the yellow tower's red-faced captain came up last, a big man with his company's badge stitched on his coat and a laugh he used for everything, and took his bag, and weighed it, and grinned.

"I'd laugh to see you in a charge, paymaster," he said, low, so it was only for the table. "At the front. With your little pen."

Rook looked up, and smiled.

"You would," he said. "Truly. I'm hopeless."

The red-faced man laughed again and went off with his bag.

---

Rook closed the book and stood up, and said a word to the two behind him, and they shut the chest and took it up between them. He went across the yard to the inn's back door with the book under his arm, and in.

The man was at the table, writing.

He was always writing. A tall man, very straight in his chair, with a long pale face and the lamp at his elbow. He didn't look up when the door opened. Rook had never seen him look up when a door opened. He went to the table and stood beside it and bent, and said it quietly: the yellow tower paid to the month's end, the red hand wanting its bags before it would move a pace, and the black ship grumbling, and the price on the farmer still standing where it stood.

The man finished the line he was writing.

He put down the pen. Then he turned his head and looked at Rook. It was a still, patient look, and Rook had never been able to tell what was behind it, which was a thing he had never been able to say of anyone else.

"Pay," said the man.

Rook nodded and went out.

---

He came out of the back door into the yard and crossed it toward the horse lines behind the stable block. He liked to walk the lines at night before he slept. He liked the beasts. The horses at least were still horses, not like the long silent rows down by the beck.

In the lane between the stable and the lines, an old carter was mending a trace by lantern light, beside a big brown carthorse. Another man stood at the mare's head. A carter too, by his coat: a long one with the cape on the shoulders, old and good, and a winter beard. He had his face turned into the mare's neck and his hand on her mane, and he was standing very still.

Rook came along the lane with his hands in his pockets.

There are only two reasons a carter stands like that at a horse's head, with his face in her mane. One is that he's crying. The other is that he doesn't want his face seen. On the Cotter's Cross road that winter there was a great deal of both, and Rook passed it every night, and it was nothing he had to pay for.

He stopped beside the mare anyway. A good beast. Broad, quiet, with a white blaze, and an eye on him that didn't like him.

"Good beast," he said to the old carter. "Look after her."

He went on down the lane toward the lines and didn't look back. Something stayed with him a few paces: the set of the shoulders under the cape, the size of the man, a hand on a mane. Then a horse at the end of the line threw its head up at him, as horses did, and he lost it.

---

He found it again three days later.

An old rider of the ledges had come down out of the Roosts that morning on an old black dragon and gone to the hold, and was going to Skarrow on the morrow. Under the old law. In the boy's name. A ford meeting under truce, at the ford on Hollow Beck, the first ford, where it had all begun. Twenty riders a side, each on his own bank. The eleven bonded pairs of the ledges to sit on the hill above as truce-keepers, as hadn't been done since the old chief's father's time. And in the water, between the two Varrs, the barley farmer, who had something to say to both their faces.

A carter up from Skarrow told it in the inn's taproom as news, as carters told everything.

Rook stood at the end of the bar with his cup and heard it. And he was in the lane between the carts, by the brown mare, with the old carter mending his trace. A big man, standing too still, with his face in a mane.

*There,* he thought.

He put his cup down on the bar very carefully.

The farmer had stood in the yard of the Ram's Head. Close enough to touch. In a carter's coat with a cape on it, and a whip under his arm, and a winter's beard. He had stood at the corner of the stable block and watched the paymaster at his table for as long as he liked. He had looked at the hands, no doubt. He was a man who looked at hands.

And then he had gone home and asked for the ford.

---

The man at the table heard it without putting his pen down.

"Then they'll both be there," he said. "In the water. Together. At last." He finished his line. "Delling has crossbows."

It was all he said about it. The rest was Rook's to arrange, and he arranged it, in the back room, over two evenings, with the yellow tower's red-faced captain across the table.

Sixty men in the gorse, thirty a bank. Those above the far bank in Clan grey with the red thread through it, Arn's colour, to shoot across the water into Ulric's riders; those above the near bank in plain grey, Ulric's, to shoot into Arn's. So that each Varr would see his own men fall, and look up, and see the other's colours on the hill.

"Thirty for me," said the red-faced captain. "Two a man for them."

"Agreed."

"And the bonded ones on the hill? The eleven?"

"They'll sit," said Rook. "No bonded rider fights kin. The men in the gorse are wearing kin." He wrote the line. "Long enough for you to be done and gone."

"And if one of them doesn't sit?"

Rook looked at him over the book. "Five over the count," he said, "for any rider who comes down off the hill."

The captain laughed, the laugh he used for everything. "They won't. Their law."

"Then it costs nothing," said Rook.

He sent a carter to Cotter's Cross with silver for sixty plain grey armbands, and a bolt of grey wool, and a skein of red thread, and a woman who could sew. He wrote all of it down. The thread came to more than he'd thought. Red always did.

---

He did not go to the ford.

He had thought he might. He had wanted to, a little, which surprised him. He thought he would have liked to see the farmer stand in the water where the boy had lain and open his mouth at last.

But on the morning of the hundred-and-thirtieth day, when he came out into the inn yard at first light, there was something in the sky over the road.

Small. High. Green. Going round in long slow circles over the Ram's Head road, where it climbed out of the hollow toward the ford, round and round, as a gull hangs over a fish-boat's wake.

Rook stood in the yard and looked up at it. It was too high to have a face. He knew it anyway. The green one. The girl's. Who would never come to the front of the ledge at Varrhold while he was in the yard.

The farmer had a rope out on the road. He had put the green one on the end of it, to watch for the paymaster.

Rook went back inside.

He spent the whole of that day in the back room of the inn, with the shutters half closed. He counted the black ship's bags again. He wrote up the week. He trimmed the lamp. Twice he went to the shutter and looked out through the gap, and both times the small green shape was there over the road, going round. Once it came lower, over the inn itself, and he stood back from the shutter in the dim room with the book in his hands and waited, and listened to it go over, a light quick sound of wings like a sail luffing. It didn't come lower than that. It went back to the road.

At the table in the corner the man went on writing, and didn't look up, and didn't ask why the paymaster was standing in the middle of the room.

---

They came back at dusk, by twos and threes, with their wounded on hurdles.

The red-faced captain came to the back room himself, with mud to his thighs and his badge torn half off his coat, and sat down without being asked. He didn't laugh.

"It went," he said. "Both lots. Each lot into the other's. You should have seen their faces, the two Varrs. Each one sure. The uncle's man with the red beard is dead in the water. The bonded sat, the way you said. Every one of them, up on the hill. Long enough." He drank from Rook's jug. "All but one."

"One," said Rook.

"An old man. On an old black. White beard, a stick. He came down the slope on her shouting *they're not kin, they're not Clan,* with his stick up, as if he could stop it with a stick." The captain wiped his mouth. "One of mine put a bolt in his chest. The black came down with him on the stones. And then the farmer's red one came down on the near-bank gorse like a house falling in and took one of mine out of it, and that was when we went." He looked at the jug. "He's dead, that one. Mine. The red one had him. They'll have his boots."

Rook said nothing. The boots. Delling's men bought their boots on the same street in Wendmouth that he did. Oiled black leather, brass eyelets. He had always known his own were wrong for a drover. He had kept them all the same. They were the only thing on him he had ever bought for himself and not for the work.

"There's another thing," said the captain.

"Go on."

"The farmer got into the water before we started. Like they said he would. Stood in it, where the boy was found. And he got as far as a name, before the first bolt." The captain looked at Rook, and for once there was nothing in his red face at all. "Yours. *Rook.* Clear as a bell, the boys on the near bank said. And the uncle knew it. You could see it on him from the gorse, like he'd been hit. The two of them were talking across the water about you, the uncle and the nephew, when we loosed."

Rook looked at the captain for a while.

"Did he," he said.

---

The captain came back in the morning for his pay. Thirty for himself, two each for the men, the dead man's two with the rest. And then he put his big hand flat on the table, on the corner of the book.

"And the five," he said. "For the old one. Over the count."

"Over the count," said Rook.

"You said. Any rider that came down off the hill."

"I did."

He counted the five out of the chest onto the table, and the captain swept them into his palm and went away, and Rook opened the book and wrote the line.

*The gorse at the ford, the second meeting: sixty, Delling's. Thirty silver the captain, two each the men. One bolt over the count, for the old one: five.*

He sat with the pen in his hand when it was done. The ink shone on the page and then went dull. He thought of an old man coming down a hillside on an old black dragon, with a stick, shouting that the men in the gorse weren't kin. Nobody had thought he would. Rook had put the five in because it cost nothing.

*Rook,* in a beck. Clear as a bell.

He had heard his own name said a great many times in his life. He had never once heard of it being said in the open, by a man standing in water, to two other men who would remember it. He found that he didn't know what to do with it. It was nothing he could pay out or receive. There was no column for it.

He shut the book.

---

On the hundred-and-thirty-third day the tents began to come down.

The man at the table gave the word, and it went out through the yard like a tide going out of a harbour, and by noon the first of the companies was on the Drover's Way going south, and then turning off it onto the new road over the hills behind Harrow's End, the black road the camps had cut all winter down through the forest to the water opposite the island. The road had never been for the Marches. Rook had known that since the autumn. He had paid for every yard of it.

They went down to the river company by company, over the next six days, with their carts and their cages and their ladders and their long silent beast lines, and the hollow round the Ram's Head emptied out behind them, fire-scar by fire-scar, until there was only the inn in the middle of a great trampled ring of black circles in the mud, like the rings a wet cup leaves on a counter.

On the hundred-and-thirty-seventh day a man came down from the north with the last news from the Clan, and Rook paid him for it in the empty taproom.

An old woman of the ledges had read a paper out in both yards, the hold and Skarrow. Under the law, standing in the gate, so nobody could say it was hidden. The farmer's paper. All of it. The bucket by the stable door and the knife with the six, and the hour of the first ford said out loud three paces off, and the paymaster at the Ram's Head with his chest and his ledger, and the second ford, and the boots.

"In his mother's hand," said the man from the north. "She wrote it out for him. He can't. He made his mark at the bottom in big crooked letters, like a child's." He took his coppers. "Arn threw her out of the hold. The uncle listened to the end of it, and didn't say a word, and went back inside."

"And?" said Rook.

The man shrugged. "And nothing. That's all. Nobody believed her."

Rook gave him another copper for that, and wrote down what he had paid.

---

He sat a long time in the empty taproom afterwards, with the book shut on the table in front of him.

*His mother's hand.* The farmer couldn't read. Rook turned that over and found that it pleased him, in a small dry way, as the Tide Council's three days of smoke had pleased him. A man who looked at everything, and could read none of it. Who had stood in the yard and watched the paymaster write in his book under the lanterns, night after night, and could not have read one line of it if it had been put into his hands.

*The paymaster at the Ram's Head with his chest and his ledger.*

His book had been read out in a gate. Not the book. The fact of it. That there was one.

---

Delling came on the evening of the hundred-and-thirty-ninth day, the last of the captains to go down to the river.

He came into the back room heavily, a heavy soft-spoken man of sixty with the yellow tower stitched on his coat and a pair of ledger-keeper's spectacles on a cord round his neck, which he put on to look at anything that mattered and took off to look at men. He kept his company's books himself. Rook had always liked him for that. There were not many captains on the Wend who could read their own pay-rolls, and none of the others would have admitted to it.

The man at the table was gone. He had gone down to the river at noon with the Fellow and his guard, and the table in the corner was bare, the lamp unlit. The chest stood on the floor by the cold grate with its lid up.

"Four months," said Delling. "Less what's been had."

"Less what's been had," said Rook.

They counted it together. Delling counted every bag himself, with his spectacles on, his small soft hands moving quickly, and Rook read the figure for each one out of the book, and they agreed on every bag, and Rook liked him better for that too. When they had done Delling took a stub of chalk out of his coat and wrote on the inside of the chest's lid, in a neat square hand, the tower and a sum, and stood back and looked at it.

"It stays here," said Delling. "Under the man's guard. I've his word on it. I'll not take my company's money onto an island with dragons about." He took the spectacles off. "Silver doesn't swim, paymaster."

"No," said Rook. "It doesn't."

"When it's over, whatever it is, I'll come up for it myself." Delling looked at him with his spectacles off, as he looked at men. "And your book?"

"Goes where I go."

Delling considered that. He looked at the book in Rook's hands, black, with its brass strap, and then up at Rook's face, as if he were reading a column he had read before and was checking the sum.

"Books drown too," he said.

He went out. Rook heard him in the yard telling his last men to go on ahead, and the jingle of harness, and the horses going away down the road toward the black road and the river, until there was nothing.

---

He was alone in the back room of the Ram's Head with the chest.

There were four of the man's own guard outside the door, old men who didn't drink. There was the sign creaking on its chains over the empty yard. There was the lamp on the trestle, and the cold grate, and the smell the room had always had, tallow and old beer and the landlord's long-gone coppers. In the dark outside, a long way off down the hills, the whole Saltreach bank opposite the island was campfires, and in the morning at first light the boats would go over.

He sat down at the trestle and opened the book, and wrote the last of the day.

*The yellow tower, four months less what's had: counted with Delling, and left in the chest at the Ram's Head under the man's guard, his tally on the lid.*

He blew on it.

Then, because he had a quarter of an hour before he need ride, and because he never did, he turned back through it.

The winter went past under his thumb. The Ram's Head. The muster. The ten a man for Upper Rush, and the dead men's ten. The cart from the Ewe Beck, two silver. The camps, the soup, the rings. The miller's two silver. The autumn. The Captain's water at the Eelweir, two coppers. The bucket in Ulric's yard, his own time.

*A horse, from the uncle's lines; to the coast for my mother. Returned.*

He looked at that a while.

There had been a boy who came down a road in the first light and smiled at him before he knew what he was for. There had been a hand on his sleeve. It was nowhere on the page. Nobody had paid for it. He could have written it. He had the pen in his hand. He could have written it plain, the place and the thing and the name, and paid it to himself as he had paid himself for the bucket.

He held the pen over the line until the ink thickened on the nib. Then he wiped the nib on the inside of his coat and put the pen away.

---

He sat with the book open on the trestle in front of him.

There was nothing in him worth carrying anywhere. He had known that a long time. A face nobody kept. Soft hands. Good boots, wrong for the work. He had been a great many things to a great many men for a winter, a drover and a buyer and a paymaster and a man to hold the horses, and none of it would stay in anyone's head past the next tide. The quays had taught him that. It was what had made him worth paying.

The book was different. The book was the only place it was kept.

*His mother's hand. He can't read.*

He thought of the farmer in the water at the ford, getting as far as a name before the bolts. Of the old woman in two gates, reading the farmer's paper to men who wouldn't hear it. Of a chest under guard in a back room, with Delling's tally chalked on the lid, and Delling, who could read, coming up for it himself when it was over, whatever it was. Delling, who kept his own books, and liked a thing counted, and had never in his life sold anything for less than it would fetch.

*Books drown too.*

The grate was cold. He could have lit it. There was kindling in the basket, and the lamp. It would have taken a quarter of an hour, a book that size, page by page, and he had a quarter of an hour.

He buckled the strap.

He got up and went over to the chest by the grate, where it stood with its lid up on the bags, and Delling's tower in chalk on the inside of the lid, and the lamplight in the iron corners. He knelt down beside it. He laid the book in on the silver, square to the sides of the chest, with the brass strap uppermost and the spine to the hinge, as he would have laid it on a counting-house shelf at the end of a day when the column had come out right.

He kept his hand on it a moment.

Then he took his hand away.
