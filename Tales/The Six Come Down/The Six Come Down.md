---

On the Plains they say a roc forgets nothing it has seen from above. Where the elk calved forty springs ago, and which way the wind came that year. Which stones lean, and which have fallen. Which riders it has carried, and where it put them down for the last time. They say it because the riders say it, and the riders don't say much, so when they say a thing it is believed.

The rocs nest on the eastern faces of the Worldsend, the far side, where the mountains look out over nothing at all, and they cross the white every morning to hunt the grass. A clan with a roc-rider knows what is happening a day's ride off before it happens. A clan without one finds out. That is the whole of it, the horse clans say. That is why a clan feeds a roc-rider all her life and asks her for nothing but the sky, and why, when she dies, they carry her up the mountain on the bird's back and lay her on its ledge under stones and go home without her.

Yeva had carried one of them up. She was fourteen. It was the first time she flew.

---

That winter the Plains were dead, and the clans had gone up to the foot of the mountains to live.

It had happened in the summer, after the stag stood up out of the Thornwood and walked, and the forest walked with it. Behind it the ground had gone. Not burned. Not flooded. The grass still came up, but it came up grey, and stayed grey, and the mares ate it and got thin on it. The Keepers' knots fell open in their hands. The standing stones stopped humming. The hollow owls sat on the Keepers' platforms and wouldn't fly, and the Keepers sat under them and didn't know what was happening a day's walk off, and came out of the forest to ask the horse clans, who didn't know either, except for the riders.

Yeva knew. She had flown over it every day since. A hundred leagues of grey, from the Thornwood to the western edge, where the scrub began. Ushra hunted it and found nothing. The elk had gone. The wild herds went west in the autumn, and the clans' own riding elk went after them through the winter, one or two a night, walking out of the lines in the dark with their heads up as if someone had called them, and nobody stopped them, because you don't hold kin. Yeva's own bull had gone in the first snow. She'd heard him go. She'd lain in the tent and listened to him walk away over the frozen grass, and in the morning she'd gone out and stood where he'd been tied and looked at the hole his feet had made.

The horse clans rode their mares now.

---

Only the high pastures were green.

They lay along the Worldsend's foot, a day's ride wide and longer than anyone had ridden, where the snowmelt came down off the white in a hundred small cold streams and spread out in the grass before it found the rivers. The ground there was alive. You could feel it in your feet if you were a Keeper. You could see it if you were anyone: green, a thin green band at the bottom of the white, with the grey running away west from it to the edge of the sky.

Tova had brought the clan up in the autumn. Other clans came after. By midwinter there were tents along the whole foot of the mountains, a day apart, each clan on its stretch of stream, and the stonehorn clans to the south with their herds on the lower slopes, and the Keepers in their green coming and going out of what was left of the forest with their wolves. The herds ate the green to the roots and moved and ate it again. It was enough. It was nearly enough.

Aun had died at the winter's dark.

The old blind woman had come up the slope in a cart with her girl beside her, and sat at the door of her tent every day with her white eyes on the mountains, and one morning she didn't come out. The horse clans cut their plaits for her. Every rider, every woman and man, and the children too, with the clan's shears passed hand to hand round a fire, so that for a month afterward everyone you met had a cold neck and kept putting a hand up to it.

Then the elders sat Tova in Aun's place, because somebody had to sit there and she was the one nobody argued with.

---

Ushra came down off the mountain at dusk, three days before the year turned, and that was the first wrong thing.

She never came down at dusk. Yeva knew her days as she knew her own. Out at first light over the white. The grass all morning, hunting, or sitting on a stone far out on the Plains with her wings half open to the sun. Back over the white in the afternoon to her ledge on the eastern face, where she slept with the cold coming up out of the nothing below her. In the morning Yeva would go out of the tent and whistle and wait, and sometime before the sun was a hand high there would be a shadow on the grass.

That evening there was a shadow on the grass with the sun going down.

Yeva was at the stream with a bucket. She looked up. The camp looked up with her. The children stopped running. Ushra came down out of the east, low, from the white, so low her shadow went over the tents and the mares put their heads up and screamed, and she landed on the slope above the camp with a sound like a heap of hides thrown down, and stood there with her wings open, not folding them.

Yeva put the bucket down and went up the slope.

---

Close to, a roc is a cliff with feathers on it.

Her back rust-brown, every feather as long as a man's arm, ragged at the ends now after the winter. Her breast white. Her head white too, and on it the beak, hooked and grey and as big round as a man's body, and under the beak the ruff where the rider sits. Her feet were yellow, scaled like a dry riverbed, and her talons black. Each one was as long as Yeva's leg from the knee down. She had held a yearling elk in them, alive, from the grass to the ledge, a day's flight, without marking it.

Her eyes were gold. They were on the west.

*Come,* said Ushra.

It came into Yeva's head the way it always had, with no sound, all at once, a stone dropped in still water. The bird didn't speak much. When she spoke it was short, and it was now. She didn't know yesterday as a word, though she remembered everything that had ever happened in it.

"Where?"

*Here. Under me. Come.*

Yeva went in under the half-open wing. The feathers came down round her. The smell of the bird: dust, old meat, cold rock, the inside of a tent in winter. She stood in the dark against the warm white breast with the heart going in it, slow, under her hands.

"What is it?"

*In the west. Coming.* The wing came down closer. *Big.*

"How big?"

*Nothing lifts it.*

---

Everything on the Plains went to ground that night.

Yeva saw it from under the wing, through the gap between the feathers. Not much. The camp. The tents with their smoke going up straight, because there was no wind. The mares on their pickets, all facing west, all with their heads up and still. Out on the grey a long way off, the dark shapes of a stonehorn herd lying down, which stonehorns never did all at once. A hare came past the foot of the slope at a run and went into the stream and lay in the water with only its nose out. Then the camp's dogs began, and then they stopped, all together, as if someone had shut a tent-flap on them.

There were no birds. She knew that without looking up. The sky was empty. The night birds that hunted the stream, the plovers that whistled over the grass at dark, the crows that sat on the tent-poles. Gone to the ground, all of them, with their heads down.

She heard Tova's voice somewhere in the camp, slow, saying something to the children. Then nothing.

---

They went over near midnight.

Out of the west. Very high. So high that at first she thought it was cloud coming, a long dark bank of it eating the stars from the west, and then she saw that there was no cloud anywhere else, and that the dark had edges.

Six.

She couldn't see them. She could see where the stars weren't. Six shapes of dark against the dark, going over together, east, toward the white, slowly, as geese go over when they've a long way still to fly. No sound. No wingbeat. Nothing. The grass on the slope lay down flat under them, all of it at once, from the camp to the edge of the world, though there was no wind, and the tent-skins drew in on their poles as if something were breathing in, and then let go.

One of them had light coming off it. A little. White, like the moon on snow.

Ushra was flat on the ground with her wings over Yeva and her head stretched out on the turf in front of her, as Yeva had never seen her lie. Her heart under Yeva's hands was going fast now. Fast and small, a hare's heart in a body the size of a hill.

*Over,* she said. *Over. Over.*

They went into the white. Yeva watched them go, where the stars came back. Over the high pastures, over the foothills, over the first of the mountains. Into the dark over the Worldsend, toward the peaks that nobody had ever crossed but the rocs.

Toward the ledges.

---

In the morning the clan sat round Tova's fire and Yeva told them.

There wasn't much to tell. Everyone had been out of the tents. Everyone had seen the stars go out and come back. But a rider says it after, in order, as she saw it, because that's what a rider is for, and so she said it: from the west, near midnight, six, very high, east into the white. One with a light on it.

Tova listened with her hands on her knees.

She was old. Older than Aun had been when Yeva was a girl. Her grey hair was cropped short as a man's, like everyone's, but on her it looked as if it had always been short, as if she'd been born with it so. Her face was all furrows, as the high pastures are in a dry spring after the herds have been over them. Her hands were brown, and knotted at every joint, and lay on her knees like things she'd taken off and put down.

"The stag," she said, when Yeva was done. "Was the stag with them?"

"I couldn't see."

"The bird?"

Yeva asked. *The one with the trees,* said Ushra, from the slope, where she was still lying with her head on the turf. *Yes. And the grey one. And four.*

"She says the stag, and the grey one from the Ring, and four more she doesn't know."

"The grey one," said Tova. "The one the lion-woman went after."

"Yes."

Tova looked at the fire. She looked at it so long that two of the children began to fidget and their mother put a hand on each.

"Where are they going?"

"Into the mountains," said Yeva. "That's all."

"Ask her why."

Yeva asked. Ushra didn't answer for a while.

*There is a great deal in the white,* she said at last. *Under it. Old. I have always smelled it.*

Yeva told them that. Tova nodded slowly, as if it were what she'd expected, though Yeva didn't think anyone could have expected it.

"Then they've gone to eat," said Tova.

---

Ushra wouldn't go back over the mountains for two days.

She hunted the grey instead, without hope, and came back with nothing, and sat on the slope above the camp in the afternoons with her head turned east. The children came up the slope to look at her, as children always did, and she let them. They came closer than they'd ever dared. One of them, Brana's youngest, a girl of six called Tikka, went right up to the foot and put her hand flat on one of the great yellow scales, and Ushra looked down at her and didn't move.

*Calf,* said Ushra.

"Hers," said Yeva. "My sister's."

*It is warm.*

On the third morning she said, *Come,* and Yeva took the felt off the tent-pole and went up the slope.

---

The pad was old felt, layer on layer, that Yeva's mother had made and Yeva had added to every winter since. It went behind the ruff, where the neck met the back, and the hide strap went round under the wings and across the white breast, and Yeva knotted it there herself with Ushra's head turned round to watch her do it. You sat with your knees down in the soft feathers under the wing roots. You held the ruff. You did not let go of the ruff.

Ushra got up. She ran three strides down the slope, four, each stride the length of a tent, and opened her wings, and the ground went away.

Yeva had flown nearly every day for twenty-six years, and it still did the same thing to her belly. It went down. It stayed on the ground. The rest of her went up with the bird in great slow heaves, *whump*, *whump*, with the wind roaring in her ears and the camp under her going small, and the green band, and the grey beyond it going away west for ever, flat, dead, with the standing stones on it like the pegs of a tent that's been taken down.

Then they turned east, and there was the white.

---

The Worldsend is not a mountain. It is all the mountains there are, stood up on their ends one behind the other, and above them there is only sky.

From the high pastures you see the first of them. From a roc's back, climbing, you see the next, and then the ones behind those, and then you stop seeing separate mountains at all and see a country made of white, ridge on ridge, with the snow on it so thick and old that it's blue in the shadows, and the sky over it so clear and dark you can see stars at noon if the bird climbs high enough. The cold up there gets into your teeth. Yeva had her felt hood down over her face to the eyes, and her hands in the ruff, and still the cold got in.

Ushra climbed. She went up the valleys where the wind came up warm off the pastures, round and round in the warm air, rising without beating her wings at all, as she always did, and then she went over the first ridge, and Yeva felt her go stiff.

The wind was wrong.

It was coming the wrong way. Not up out of the valleys. Down into them. From the east, from the high peaks, from somewhere ahead, a long steady draw of air going in toward the middle of the mountains, as if the whole Worldsend were breathing in and hadn't stopped.

And it was warm.

---

They didn't go far.

They went as far as the ledges. Ushra took them along the eastern faces, where the mountains fell away on the far side into nothing, into blue air that went down out of sight with no bottom, and the rocs had their ledges along it in a long scattered line. Yeva had seen them a thousand times. The wild rocs sat on them in the afternoons, rust and white against the black rock, heads turning as Ushra went by.

They were all there. All sitting tight. Not one in the air.

All with their heads turned north.

Toward the middle of the range, where the highest peaks stood together round something Yeva couldn't see. A valley, maybe. A bowl among the peaks. She couldn't see into it. There was a ridge in the way. Over the ridge the air was moving, shaking, as air shakes over a fire, and the snow on the ridge's near side was wet. Running. In the cold. She could see the water coming down off it in a hundred threads, bright, onto the rock below, onto the next snow, and that was running too.

*Mine,* said Ushra.

Her ledge. She came down onto it. A long shelf of black rock, wide as the floor of a tent, with the old nest on it, sticks as thick as a man's arm, and at the back of it a cairn of stones.

The snow on the ledge was gone. The rock under it was wet and black. And warm. Yeva felt it through her boots when she got down. Warm, as the ground is in front of a fire that has been banked all night.

---

Hesk was under the cairn.

He'd been Ushra's first. Old when Yeva was a girl, a little bent man with a face like a dried apple and very pale eyes from looking into the sky so long, who never said anything at all to anybody and was the most respected man in three clans. He'd died in his sleep in his tent one winter. Yeva was fourteen. Ushra had come down off the mountain the next morning and walked into the camp, which no roc ever did, between the tents, with the mares going mad on their pickets and the dogs fleeing and people pressed against the tent-skins, and stopped in front of Yeva at the stream with her water bucket, and put her head down.

All the way down. Until the grey hook of the beak was in front of Yeva's face, so close she could see the fine cracks in it, like the cracks in old ice.

Then the beak touched her mouth.

Just touched it. Just set itself there, the point of it, on her lips, as a mare touches her foal's nose when it's first on its feet. And Yeva had stood still and let it, though it cut her, both lips, top and bottom, and the blood ran down her chin and into the bucket. She'd thought she ought to stand still. She'd thought it was a question.

*Yes,* she'd said, through the blood, in her head, to nobody, never having said anything to anybody in her head before.

And something had answered.

The clan had carried Hesk up the slope that afternoon wrapped in his felt, and tied him on Ushra's back, and tied Yeva behind him, because somebody had to hold him and the bird wouldn't take anyone else. She'd flown for the first time with a dead man in her arms. She'd laid him on this ledge and built the cairn with her own hands, stone by stone, crying, while the bird watched her with its gold eyes and didn't help and didn't go.

Her mouth had healed with the scar. White, both lips, so that her mouth pulled a little to one side for the rest of her life. People thought it made her look as if she were about to smile. She mostly wasn't.

---

She put her hand on the cairn. The stones were warm.

"Can we stay?"

Ushra was standing on the edge of the ledge with her back to the cairn and her head north, toward the ridge with the air shaking over it.

*No.*

"Why?"

The bird didn't answer for a time. Then she said a thing Yeva hadn't heard her say before, in twenty-six years.

*It is going out.*

"What is?"

*The mountain. Out of the mountain.* Ushra's feathers lifted all down her back and settled. *Like the warm out of a carcass.*

---

Yeva went down to Tova that evening and said what she'd seen.

The wind going in. The snow running in the cold. The rocs on their ledges, every one, with their heads turned north. The rock warm on the ledge where Hesk was, and the bird's word for it.

Tova heard her out.

"And the six?"

"I didn't see them. She wouldn't go near. They're in the middle, past a ridge. Where the highest ones are."

"Eating," said Tova.

"Yes."

Tova put a stick on the fire.

"When they've eaten," she said, "they'll come out." She said it slowly, as she said everything, weighing each word before she put it down, so that you had time to see it coming and still didn't believe it until it was there. "Things come out when they've eaten. Then they want water. Then they want to lie down somewhere." She looked at the fire. "Tell me when the rocs move."

---

The lights came on the fourth night.

The whole camp saw them. Nobody had to be woken. A little before midnight the east began to come up white, behind the mountains, as if the sun had turned round in the night and decided to rise out of the Worldsend after all, and the light came over the peaks and down the slopes and onto the high pastures, and every tent-skin in the camp glowed with it, and people came out.

White. Gold at the edges.

It didn't rise. It lay there over the mountains, behind the first peaks, and grew a little, and faded a little, and grew. Slowly. Like the glow in a felt-maker's tent when someone lifts the flap on the fire, and lets it fall, and lifts it.

You could have read by it, if anyone had been able to read.

Ushra was on the slope. Yeva went up to her. The bird was standing, wings half open, with the light on her white breast so that she shone.

*Drinking,* said Ushra.

---

At the foot of the mountains, where the light came down, the grass went black.

Yeva saw it begin from the slope. A line along the bottom of the white, a day's ride off, where the high pastures met the first rock, where the light lay thickest. Then the line was wider. By the middle of the night it was a band, all along the mountains' foot as far as she could see north and south, and the band was coming down the slope toward the camp, slowly, as a frost comes down a valley in the autumn, field by field.

Not burning. There was no fire in it. The green went dark and then black and then it was black, as if it had been burnt a year ago and not grown back.

The stones began after that.

There was a standing stone below the camp, a tall one, older than the clan, that the children weren't allowed to climb. In the middle of the night it cracked. Yeva heard it. Everyone heard it. A sound like stream ice going in a spring thaw, a long splitting crack that went on and on and ended in a bang, and in the white light she saw the stone stand there with a black line down it from top to foot.

Then another, far off, south. Then another, north. All night. You could hear them from a league away, the stones of the Plains, cracking one by one in the cold, like a river breaking up.

"The Grey Mother keeps the stones," said someone behind her. An old man. Brana's husband's father. It was what you said when the ground shook.

Nobody answered him.

The light went down at dawn. It didn't go out. The real dawn came up under it, grey, and drowned it, as a big fire drowns a candle. By full day there was only the sky, and the mountains, white, and along their foot a long black band where the high pastures had been green the night before.

---

The rocs came off the mountain an hour after sunrise.

All of them.

Yeva was at the stream. She heard the camp go quiet behind her and turned round, and there they were, coming over the first ridge of the white out of the east, low, in a long loose line from as far north as she could see to as far south. Rust-brown and white. Thirty. Forty. More than she'd known there were. She'd lived her whole life under the Worldsend and never seen more than three rocs in the sky at once. They came down over the black band and the green that was left and over the tents, so low the wind of them knocked the smoke flat, and the mares went down on their pickets and lay there with their eyes rolling, and the children screamed and then stopped screaming and stood with their mouths open, and the rocs went on over, west, out over the grey, not beating their wings, sliding down the cold morning air as if they were going down a hill.

They didn't turn.

They didn't hunt. They didn't circle. They went straight out over the Plains in their long ragged line until they were specks, and then they weren't anything.

Behind them the mountains stood in the morning with nothing in the air over them at all.

---

Ushra watched them go from the slope.

She didn't go with them. Yeva had thought, for a moment, standing in the stream with the water round her boots, that she would. That the line would go over and Ushra would get up off the slope and run her four strides and lift, and go west with her kind, and Yeva would stand in the stream and watch her be a speck.

She didn't. She sat on the slope with her head turned after them and her feathers lifting and settling.

*Gone,* she said.

"Where?"

*Down.* A long pause. *Everything comes down off the white when the white is gone. Goats. Snow. Water.* Another. *Us.*

Yeva stood in the stream.

"And them?"

*Them,* said Ushra. *When it is gone.*

---

Tova was at her fire when Yeva got there. She was already on her feet. She had her stick in one hand and she was looking west, after the rocs, and she didn't sit down.

"They've gone," said Yeva.

"I saw."

"She says everything comes down off the white when the white is gone. The rocs. Then them." Yeva had run up from the stream. She made herself say it as a rider says it, in order. "She says they'll come down when it's gone. She doesn't know when. A roc doesn't have when."

Tova was quiet.

All round them the camp was standing outside its tents, looking at her. Not at the sky, which was empty, or at the mountains, which had a black band at their foot, or at the cracked stone below the camp. At the old woman by the fire with her stick.

"How many in the camp," said Tova, "can walk a hundred leagues?"

---

She was a long time deciding, and then it was all at once.

She sat back down, slowly, on her log. She put her stick across her knees. She looked at the fire, and Yeva, who had known her all her life, stood and waited, and the camp waited. The fire burnt down. Somebody put a dung-cake on it.

Then Tova looked up.

"Riders west," she said. "Four. To the Hearthmere road, the one through the oaks. Ask the crown's men at the ford if they'll take us. Ask polite." She looked at the fire again. "They won't. Ask anyway."

Four of the young men got up and went to their mares.

"Dast."

Yeva's sister's son was standing at the back. He came forward. He was twenty, long in the leg, with the cold neck still and a hand that kept going up to it. He'd been the best horseman in the clan since he was nine.

"Go with them as far as the ford," said Tova. "Then go on. Down the river. Find the lion-woman."

Dast looked at her.

"The Keepers say she's at the river. Where the grey one slept. At the crown's school, or what's left of it." Tova's voice didn't change. "The Keepers remember her. From the Moot. Hesper's woman. Tell her what's on the mountain. Tell her we're coming west, all of us, because there's nowhere else. Tell her the crown's men are at the ford." She was quiet a moment. "Tell her I asked."

"What if she won't come?"

"Then she won't," said Tova. "Go and ask."

---

Yeva caught her afterward. By the mares, where Tova had gone to look at her own, the bay with the white face, which was standing very still with its ears back and its eyes on the east.

"I could go."

"No."

"She'd be at the river by tonight. Tomorrow. Dast will take four days. Five."

"They've never seen a roc in Hearthmere," said Tova. She ran her old root hand down the mare's neck, and the mare's ears came forward a little. "They've seen dragons. They don't like dragons. They put bolts in them. A thing that size coming down out of the sky at them, they'll put a bolt in her before you've got your mouth open, and then I've no rider and no bird." She didn't look round. "A boy on a mare, they'll talk to. Even crown men will talk to a boy on a mare."

"And if they don't?"

"Then he'll ride round them," said Tova. "He's a good boy on a mare."

She went on stroking the bay's neck.

"Besides," she said, "I want you here. I want you in the east, where I can see what's coming. You can't see east from the bottom of a river."

---

Dast went west at noon with the four riders.

Brana stood at the edge of the camp and watched him go, with Tikka on her hip. She didn't wave. Nobody waved. The clans don't. Yeva stood beside her and they watched five riders and five mares go down the slope off the black and the green onto the grey, and get smaller, and smaller, and become a little dust moving west across a country that had nothing else moving on it.

"Will she come?" said Brana.

"The lion-woman?"

"Anybody."

Yeva thought about the woman at the Speaking Stones, a year ago, a lowland woman with a soldier's shoulders sitting in the grass beside the great grey-maned beast that nobody would go near, and how she'd sat there for days and said hardly anything. She thought about the Moot afterward, and the lanes, and the lion standing across the near lane with the turf coming up in a bank round him, and another lion in the far lane at the same time, which nobody had explained to her since.

"She came last time," she said.

---

Then Tova had her take the word round.

It was what a rider was for. The hollow owls were sitting on their platforms in the dead forest with their great eyes open, going nowhere. The clans' riders on mares would take days. A roc could be at the next clan's fires by mid-morning and the next by noon, and the stonehorn clans in the south by dusk, and sleep there, and be back by the next noon, and do it again.

So she did it again.

Four days. She went north along the mountains' foot the first day, over the black band, which was wider every morning, over the tents of the clans strung out along the streams. She came down by each clan's fire and the children ran and the mares lay down on their pickets, and she got off the bird with her legs stiff and her face burnt with the cold and stood by the fire and said it to the elders. *Tova says: the rocs have left the mountain. Tova says they'll come down. Tova says get ready to go west, all of you, with everything. Tova says she's sent to the crown.*

Nobody argued. Not one. She'd thought they would. The horse clans argue about everything, and the elders argue longest. Nobody did. They looked at the empty sky over the mountains, and at the black at the mountains' foot, and at the cracked stones, and they nodded, and one old man at the second camp north said, "Tova said that?" and when Yeva said yes, he said, "Then we'll go," and got up to tell his people.

---

The Keepers were on the second day.

They'd come out of the dead forest in the autumn when the knots failed and the owls stopped, and made their camp at the forest's edge where the grey trees began, round a spring that still ran. Fifty or sixty of them, in their faded green, with the wolves lying round the tents in the grey grass and a few bears asleep against the dead trunks like heaps of old hides. They didn't run from the roc. Their wolves stood up, all together, and watched her land, and didn't make a sound.

A broad grey woman came out to meet her, with a wolf at each knee, grey as she was.

"You're Tova's," said the Keeper. "The bird-woman."

"Yes."

"We saw the rocs go." The Keeper looked at Ushra, who was standing behind Yeva with her head low and her gold eyes on the wolves. "Every one of them. Over the forest, west. Not one came back." She put her hand on the nearer wolf's head. "We had owls once that did that. Before the stag. They went off the stones in the night and didn't come back, and then the ground opened."

"Tova says get ready to go west."

"We're ready," said the Keeper. "We've been ready since the owls. There's nothing left to keep." She looked east, past the dead forest, to where the white stood over everything with the black at its foot. "Tell Tova we'll come to her when it starts. We'll run the flanks."

---

On the way back from the Keepers, high over the grey with the sun behind her, Ushra turned her head west and said, *There.*

Yeva looked. She felt the pull of where the bird was looking, as she always did, a tug behind her eyes, and her eyes followed it, and for a while saw nothing. The grey. The sky. Then, very high, higher than Ushra flew, higher than anything ought to fly, a speck going east.

Green. Catching the sun.

*The small one,* said Ushra. Her feathers lifted. *The green. That hisses.*

Yeva knew it then. The green dragon. The little one that had come down into the grass by the Speaking Stones a year ago, out of the west, in a long skid that threw up turf, with every elk-rider in the ring round the Stones going for a bow. Yeva had been one of them. She'd been on the ground that day, on her bull, and had an arrow on the string before she'd thought, and the old blind woman in the horseshoe had said one word, and the bows had come down. The dragon had stood there in the grass with its sides going in and out and looked round at four hundred people as if it had only just noticed them.

Ushra had looked at it from the sky all that afternoon. She hadn't liked it. She didn't like anything that flew and wasn't a roc, and she liked dragons least.

*It looks,* said Ushra. *It goes to look.*

The speck went on east, very high, toward the white, toward the middle where the highest peaks were, and was gone.

At dusk, back at the camp, sitting by the fire with her face burning from the wind, Yeva looked up and saw it again. Coming back. West. Lower. Slower. Going home across the grey in the last of the light like a bird that's flown too far.

---

She went south on the third day to the stonehorn clans.

They had the lower slopes, where the green ran down into the grey in long tongues along the streams. Their herds were on it. From the air the stonehorns were grey boulders scattered on the slopes in hundreds, and then you came down and they were each the size of a cart and smelled of dust and dung and old leather, and swung their great plated heads round at the roc with their little eyes trying to make her out, and didn't run. A stonehorn doesn't run from things. It can't see far enough to know it should.

The stonehorn elder was a fat man named Gorrach with a voice like a stonehorn bull's, who came out to her fire in his shirt.

"West," he said. "With the herds."

"With everything."

"Over the scrub? There's nothing on the scrub. It's dead as the Plains, deader." He looked at his herds on the slope, grey on the green, grazing it down. "Eighty leagues of nothing and a river at the end, and the crown on the far side of the river saying no. They'll be bone by the river."

"Tova says."

Gorrach looked at the mountains for a while. At the black band, which reached down here as it did everywhere, coming down the slopes toward his herds a field's width a night.

"Tova would," he said. "Tell her we'll be ready."

---

Every night the lights were brighter.

By the sixth night, at midnight, they were bright enough to tell a bay mare from a grey on the pickets. By the seventh, when the wind came from the east, it came warm, at night, in the cold of the year, and smelled of wet rock and something else, something like the smell of a mare's breath when she's been eating new grass, sweet and green and wrong. The black band came down to the stream above the camp and stopped there, as if the water held it.

On the seventh day Tova moved the camp off the slope, down onto the grey, half a day west. The herds wouldn't stay on the black. The mares stood on it and shook and wouldn't put their heads down.

The tents came down. The carts were loaded. The clan went down onto the dead grass with everything it had, and pitched again, and didn't unload.

Everyone slept in their clothes.

---

On the eighth night the lights didn't come.

Yeva waited for them. Everyone did. The whole clan sat outside the tents in the dark with their faces to the east, and the children asleep across their knees, and the dark stayed dark. Stars down to the line of the mountains. The mountains black against the stars. Nothing.

It was worse than the light.

"Is it over?" said Tikka, half asleep, against her mother.

"Hush."

Ushra was on the grey a little way out from the tents. Yeva couldn't see her. She could feel her. Awake. Her head up.

*Coming,* said Ushra.

---

Before the light, the bird said *Come.*

Yeva had the pad on her back and the strap knotted across the white breast in the dark without needing to see it, and was up, and they were running, four strides, and the cold came up round her as they lifted, and the camp's fires went small and red below.

They climbed toward the east. Toward the white, which was grey in the starlight. Toward the sun, which wasn't there yet.

Ushra didn't climb as she usually did. She went straight, beating, with her wings going *whump*, *whump* in the dark, hard, as Yeva had felt her beat only once before, years ago, carrying a yearling elk into a headwind. She wasn't going to the ledges. She wasn't going high. She was going to a place on the air a long way out from the mountains, over the black band, where she could see.

She stopped there. Hung there. Turning slowly on the cold air that was coming down off the mountains now, the right way at last, with the whole of the Worldsend in front of them going up into the dark.

*Watch,* said Ushra.

---

The sky came grey behind the mountains.

Then pink. Then the first of the sun along the topmost peaks, a line of red on the white, as on every morning of Yeva's life. She'd seen it from this height a thousand times. The light coming down the faces, peak by peak, onto the snow.

There wasn't any snow.

She saw it as the light came down. The mountains were black. The first peaks still had a little white on their tops, at the very highest, a cap, a patch. Below that they were rock. Bare, black, wet rock, shining, all the way down, as if the snow had been skinned off them in the night. Streams everywhere. Hundreds. Thousands. Running down the black in white threads, roaring, she could hear them from here, down every gully and every face into the foothills and out onto the black pastures in sheets.

A whole winter's snow. Twenty winters'. Going down off the Worldsend in one night.

*Gone,* said Ushra. *The white.*

---

Then the foothills moved.

In the middle, below the highest peaks, where a valley came down out of the mountains and opened out onto the high pastures in a wide black mouth. Something was coming out of it.

At first it was the light. White and gold, like the lights of the nights before, but low now, on the ground, coming out of the valley mouth slowly as dawn comes along a hill. The sun was up behind the mountains and this was brighter than the sun, and it came from something tall, something lean and tall and deer-shaped, the size of a hill, with one horn going up from its brow, long, twisted, shining. The light came off the horn in rays. Where it walked the dawn round it was full day.

Yeva put her arm over her eyes. She looked under it.

Behind the bright one, the stag.

She'd seen it before. Everyone at the Moot had seen it, standing up out of the Thornwood with the trees going off it like water off a beast coming out of a river. It came out of the valley mouth with its antlers going up like a dead wood in winter, black against the light, and round it the forest came too, walking. Trees. Black pines out of the mountain, and thorn, and things she had no names for, pulling their roots up out of the wet rock and swinging them forward and putting them down, a wood going along round the stag as a herd goes round its bull.

---

Then a grey one. A hill. She knew that one too. Not from the Moot. From the night it went over the Plains at midsummer, west from the Ring, with the sky empty under it for leagues. Grey, and rough, with a long ridge of plates down its back like a line of broken hills, and in the ridge six places that weren't like the rest, paler, as if the rock had grown over something.

Behind it something long and white, that came out of the valley in coils, slowly, the coils going over each other, and where it touched the black pastures they went white. Frost. Rime, on the wet ground, spreading out from it in rings, so that it went along in a white ring of its own making in the middle of the black.

Then something low. Wide. Flat as a heap of plates the size of a camp, with the ground under it shining, the grass where it went gone to something hard and bright. Things hanging off its sides. Long, black, broken. Chain. Some country's chain, still on it.

And last, slowest, dragging itself out of the valley mouth over the stones and down onto the pastures, a thing with a back like nothing she had ever seen. Grey and white and rough, with things growing on it that had died, dried, brown, hanging off it like old hides off a fence. And on its back, stones. Built stones. Pillars, broken off. A round roof fallen in.

And in the roof something swinging.

She heard it. Across all that distance, over the roar of the water off the mountains. A note. Low. Then again. Then again, as the thing dragged itself forward, one heave at a time, onto the Plains.

A bell.

---

Six.

They came down out of the valley mouth onto the high pastures together, slowly, all of them, one behind another and then side by side, spreading out a little, as a herd spreads when it comes out of a pass onto open grass. Not fast. A walk. Slower than a walk. Slower than stonehorns grazing.

And where they went, the grass went black ahead of them.

She watched it go. Out from them over the pastures that were already black, onto the grey beyond, in a line. The grey going dark in a long band, half a league ahead of them, a league, the dead grass going to black dust, and the dust going up. Black. Like smoke, in a long wall, all along the mountains' foot as far as she could see, going up into the morning, though there was no fire anywhere.

West. They were turning west.

*Nothing lifts those,* said Ushra.

---

Yeva was down by the camp before the sun was a hand high. She didn't remember the flight. She remembered the strap, after, her fingers on the knot, not working, and Ushra's head coming round to look at her hands.

Tova was on her mare.

The whole clan was up. The tents were down already. Yeva hadn't told anyone. Nobody had needed telling. They'd seen the black go up along the mountains' foot with the dawn, and the light under it, and they'd heard the bell, even here, half a day off, faint, once and then again, like the bell on a lead mare that has strayed far off on a hill.

Yeva walked across to the white-faced bay and stood at its shoulder and looked up.

"They've come down," she said.

Tova looked east, past her, at the black wall going up into the morning.

"All of them?"

"Six. Out of a valley in the middle. Onto the pastures." Yeva made herself say it in order. "The bright one first. Then the stag with the trees. Then the grey one from the Ring. A white one that makes frost. A flat one that makes glass, with chains on it. One with a fallen house on its back and a bell in it. The grass goes black in front of them. They're walking." She heard her own voice. "They're coming west."

"How fast?"

"Slower than us. Slower than the herds."

"Then we'll walk," said Tova.

She lifted her stick.

---

They went west that morning. All of them.

Yeva went up over it at noon, high, and saw the whole of it at once, as only a rider can.

The clans coming down off the mountains' foot onto the grey, from the north and from the south, in long lines like the strings of a net all drawing in to the same knot. The horse clans on their mares, and in front of the riders and behind them, children. Two to a saddle. Three, on the big mares. The carts with the old people in them, the oldest lying down, the next oldest sitting up on the loads with their hands in their laps, and the tents and the poles and the felts and the pots all tied on round them. The stonehorn clans out of the south with their herds, hundreds of the great grey beasts walking in a slow grinding mass with the herd-men going between them with their long poles, and the dust going up off them brown, a different dust from the black, an ordinary dust, so that from the sky you could tell the clans from the things behind them by the colour of the air they raised. The Keepers, last, out of the north-east, on foot, in their green, with the wolves running out wide along the flanks, grey and low and quick.

All of it drawing together on the grey, and turning west.

Ten thousand, Yeva thought. More. She'd never seen the clans all together in one place, not even at the Moot. Nobody had. Nobody had ever needed to.

Behind them, a day off and more, the black wall going up along the whole of the east. And under it, if she looked, which she did, every hour, the light.

---

The first night they made camp in the open on the grey, where there was nothing. No stream, no shelter, no stone that hadn't cracked. The herds stood in a great ring round the camp with their heads out and their horns lowered, and the herd-men walked the ring all night. The wolves lay outside it.

Tova came to Yeva's fire after dark. She didn't sit. She stood with her stick and looked at the bird, lying on the grey at the edge of the light with her head under her wing.

"She's not eaten."

"Not for six days."

"Will she?"

"There's nothing to eat."

Tova looked out at the dark, toward the herds' ring, where the stonehorns stood with their calves under their bellies.

"Not those," she said.

"She knows. She wouldn't." Yeva put a dung-cake on the fire. "They're careful with calves. She'll find something. Everything on the Plains is going to be moving, after this."

Tova was quiet for a while.

"That's what I'm afraid of," she said.

---

It was the beasts that moved first.

On the second day, flying back east to look, Yeva saw hares. Thousands of them, a grey-brown smoke running low over the grey grass toward the west, ahead of the black, going round the camp's dust as water goes round a stone and on. On the third day, wild horses, a herd of a hundred, running with their heads up and their tails out, mad, and Ushra stooped on them before Yeva could say anything and took a yearling off the back of the herd without slowing, as she'd take an elk, and carried it a league and came down with it on a cracked stone and ate it there, all of it, while Yeva sat on the grey and didn't watch.

*Better,* said Ushra afterward, wiping her beak on the stone.

On the fourth day, wolves that weren't Keepers' wolves, going by in a long silent line a league off, not looking at anything. On the fifth, a herd of the feral stonehorns that nobody had ever tamed, going west at their slow trot, with their little eyes not seeing the clans' herds until they were nearly on them, and the herd-men running out with their poles to turn them, shouting.

Everything on the Plains that could still go anywhere was going west. Ahead of the six. Ahead of the black. Round the clans and past them and on.

The six came on behind. A day off. Never more than a day and a half, never less than a day. Every morning Yeva went back to look, and every morning they were there, in the black, under the wall of dust. The bright one's light. The trees moving round the stag. The white rings of frost. The bell. Walking west at less than a walk, all together, as if they had all the time there was.

---

The riders came back on the fourth day of the walk.

Four of them, out of the west, on mares that were so thin and blown that the herd-men ran out and took their heads. Yeva saw them from the air and came down. She was off Ushra and across the grey to them before they'd got off.

Four.

"Where's Dast?"

"Gone on," said the eldest, a man called Orrik, sliding down. He could hardly stand. "Down the river. Like Tova said." He looked round for Tova. She was coming, on her bay, slowly. "They turned us. At the ford on the oak road. Knights. Twenty of them, and more coming, with hounds, and a barricade across the road made of whole trees. They said the council's order. No clans. No herds. No beast-folk." He spat on the grey grass. "Beast-folk."

Tova had reached them. She sat her mare and looked down at him.

"Did you ask polite?"

"I asked polite," said Orrik. "Dast asked politer. The captain was polite back. He said he was sorry. He said it was the council's order, and if we came to the ford with the herds his men would turn us, and he was sorry about that too." Orrik looked up at her. "Then Dast went south down the near bank of the river, alone, to find the lion-woman. He said tell Yeva he'd find her."

"Did they follow him?"

"They didn't care where he went," said Orrik. "As long as it wasn't over."

---

Tova sat her mare a long time after they'd gone to be fed.

Yeva stood beside her. The column went past them. Carts. Mares with children on them looking down at the old woman as they went by. A cart with two old men in it, asleep, leaning on each other. The herds. The dust.

"So," said Tova.

"The river," said Yeva. "The Ysse. I've seen it. It's high already, with all that water off the mountains coming down. By the time we get there it'll be higher. The fords will be under."

"I know the Ysse."

"The stonehorns could cross. The mares could. Not the children. Not the carts. Not the old." She heard her voice going and stopped it. "And if we cross it there's eighty leagues of scrub and nothing, and a river at the end of it, and knights on the river."

"I know."

"And them behind."

Tova looked at her. Down, from the mare, with her furrowed old face.

"You're telling me what I know," she said. "You're a rider. You're meant to tell me what I don't."

---

So Yeva went west to look.

She went the next morning, the day after the riders came in, and flew all day. West over the grey, with the column's dust going small behind her and the black wall behind that, to the western edge of the Plains, where the grey ran down in long slopes to the river. She came down on the near bank above the fords and sat on Ushra's back and looked at it.

The Ysse came out of the north, out of the lake under the dead forest, out of the mountains beyond that, and went south. Here it was wide and green and fast, and loud, with the white water off the Worldsend in it, and it was rising. She could see where it had been yesterday, a line of wet on the bank above the water. There were four fords, where the old drove roads came down off the Plains to the river and crossed it on gravel. They were under. She could see where they were only because the water humped up white over them.

Too deep for a child. Too fast for an old man.

Beyond the river, the scrub. Brown and grey. Going away west for ever under the sky, with nothing on it.

Nobody.

She sat on the bird and looked at the scrub until her eyes hurt. She didn't know what she'd hoped to see. A boy on a mare. A line of knights. A lion. Anything coming.

There was nothing coming. There was the river, and the scrub, and the sky.

---

*Here is low,* said Ushra.

They were on the bank. The bird had her head down, looking at the water, which she didn't like. No roc likes running water. You can't see what's under it.

"Yes."

*The white is gone.* Ushra turned her head and looked east, back the way they'd come, over the grey, toward the mountains that weren't white, a long way off, a line of black under the sky. *Where is high now?*

Yeva didn't know what to tell her.

She thought of the other rocs. Forty of them, more, going over the camp that morning in their long ragged line, west, down, out over the Plains, and never turning. Gone somewhere. Gone wherever rocs went when they had no mountain. Ushra could go after them. Any morning. Run her four strides and lift, and go, and Yeva would stand on the grey and watch her be a speck. Yeva had never heard of a roc leaving its rider. She had never heard of a roc without a mountain either.

"Do you want to go?" she said. "After them?"

Ushra looked at her. The gold eye, as big as a cart-wheel, with Yeva in it, small.

*Go where?* she said. *You are here.*

---

They flew back in the dark. Yeva gave it to Tova at her fire as it was: the river high and rising, the fords under, the scrub empty. Nothing coming.

Tova listened with her hands on her knees.

"Then we'll get there," she said, "and look at it."

"And then?"

"And then we'll look at it some more," said Tova. "I've crossed the Ysse in every month there is. It's been high before."

"Not like this."

"No," said Tova. "Not like this." She put a stick on the fire. "Go to sleep, girl. You're no use to me tired."

Yeva was forty. She went to sleep.

---

They walked.

Four leagues a day, at the herds' pace. No faster, because the herds couldn't. No slower, because of what was behind. The old people in the carts got quieter. The children stopped running between the carts and walked, holding the stirrups, or rode, two and three on the mares, asleep against each other. An old woman of the third horse clan died on the eighth day of the walk, in a cart, in the afternoon, and they didn't stop, because Tova said they couldn't stop; they laid her on the grey at dusk and put the cracked stones of a dead cairn over her and went on in the morning, and her people walked at the back of the column for a day with their hands at their cold necks.

The Keepers ran the flanks. The broad grey woman with her two wolves came to Tova's fire some nights and sat by it without being asked and didn't say much, and once, when Tova asked her what the forest would be when this was over, said, "Firewood," and nothing else.

Every morning Yeva went east to look.

Every morning they were there.

---

On the tenth morning of the walk Tova sent her west again.

Before light. Tova came to her fire in the dark on foot, leaning on her stick, and stood over her until she woke.

"The river," said Tova. "We're two days off it. Go and look at it. And look for the boy."

"He'll be at the crown's river. With the lion-woman. If he found her."

"Look anyway," said Tova. "I sent him. I want to know what I sent him into." She was quiet. "I didn't think she'd come, Yeva. When I sent him. I sent him anyway."

Yeva sat up.

"She came last time," she said. "At the Moot."

"Last time she was following the grey one," said Tova. "This time there's nothing to follow but us." She turned to go. "Look anyway."

---

Ushra took her up in the grey before dawn.

West. Away from the black and the light behind them, which Yeva could feel on the back of her neck like the heat of a fire you've turned away from. Over the camp, which was waking, small fires coming up one after another in the dark, ten thousand people and their beasts lying on the dead grass in a great sprawl a league across. Over the herds' ring. Over the Keepers' wolves, lying out on the flanks, lifting their heads as the shadow went over.

Then the empty grey.

The sun came up behind them. It threw Ushra's shadow out ahead of them on the grass, long, a league long, a great dark bird running west over the Plains in front of them. The light came along the grey and showed it to her, all of it, the cracked stones, the dead grass, the long swells of the Plains going down toward the river, and nothing on it. Nothing moving. Everything that could move had gone past already.

Ushra climbed. She climbed higher than Yeva had flown in years, round and round on the first warm air coming up off the grey, until the camp behind them was a stain and the river ahead was a thread, green, with the white on it where the fords were, and beyond it the scrub went away brown to the edge of the world.

The cold got into Yeva's teeth. She pulled the felt down over her face to the eyes, and held the ruff, and looked.

---

The bird's head turned.

Yeva felt it before she saw it. The pull. Behind her eyes. Ushra's whole body going still on the air, and her head turning, slowly, a little north of west, and staying there, and the pull of where she was looking coming into Yeva like a hand on her chin.

She looked.

The grey. The long slopes going down to the river. A ring of standing stones on a low swell, small as a child's knucklebones from here. The thread of the river.

Nothing.

*There,* said Ushra.

"I can't see it."

*There. On the grass. Walking.*

Yeva looked until her eyes watered in the cold, and wiped them on her sleeve, and looked again. The grey, and the grey, and the far edge of the grey where the Plains went down to the river and the sky came down to meet them.

And on the edge, something.

A grey thing, on the grey. So far off it was hardly there. A mark. A speck that wasn't a stone because a stone doesn't move, and this moved, slowly, steadily, a little at a time, and it was going the wrong way. Everything on the Plains for nine days had gone west. The hares, the horses, the wolves, the feral herds, the rocs, the clans. Everything.

This was coming east.

*I have seen that one,* said Ushra.

Her feathers lifted all down her back, under Yeva's knees, and settled.

*It walked under me at night. By a fire. When the grass was green. It looked up.* She was quiet. *It looked up at me.*

Yeva held the ruff.

She was a rider. She was meant to see first and say it after, in order, as she'd seen it. She sat on the bird in the cold over the empty Plains with the sun at her back and the black behind that, and she looked at the speck on the edge of the world until her eyes made it out, a little, for a breath, a shape against the light: four legs, long, going easily; a great head carried low; something dark on the ground beside it, small, keeping pace.

And the mane. Grey.

Far off, on the horizon, where the Plains went down to the river, a lion was walking east.
