---

The oldest story in Varrhold is about her.

It goes like this. When the first Varr came up Hollow Beck with his sheep, looking for grass the coast men couldn't reach, he climbed past the falls into the hollow under the black cliff and found a bronze dragon lying on a ledge above it, alone, with her wings folded and her chin on her forefeet, looking out over the moor. He stood in the beck with his ewes round his knees and asked her leave to stay. She didn't answer. He waited a day and a night and asked again, and she didn't answer that either, and on the third morning he took it for yes and started cutting turf for a wall.

The hold has been taking her silence for yes ever since. That is the joke, when the old men tell it at the long fire in winter, and they laugh, and look up at the cliff when they think nobody is watching them do it.

There are other stories. There is the winter the beck froze to the bottom and she broke it open with her tail so the sheep could drink. There is the summer the coast men came up the Drover's Way with ladders, and saw her on the ledge, and went home. There is the girl who climbed to her cave on a dare and came down with her hair white. None of them are true, probably. In all of them she is already old. Nobody's grandmother's grandmother could remember a time when she wasn't on the cliff, and nobody could say what she was doing there, until, in a summer nobody now living remembered well, she chose Ragnald Holm, and the hold had something to say about her at last. *She was waiting for him.*

And when the rock came down on Ragnald Holm on the south slope, and she lay down on the shore of Coldtarn and didn't die, the hold went back to saying what it had always said.

She's waiting.

For what, nobody agreed. To die, said most. For the pull to come down and finish her, slow, as it had to, as it always did. Some of the old women said no. They said she'd been waiting before the first Varr came up the beck and she'd be waiting when the last one went down it, and that Ragnald had only been a thing she did in the meantime.

Sigrun Holm, who was seventeen that spring, thought she was waiting for somebody to tell her to get up.

The ewe had been down since before light.

She lay at the bottom of the fold below the falls with her legs folded under her and her nose on the cold ground, and beside her, in the wet straw, lay the lamb she'd had in the night. It had come wrong, backwards, with one leg back, and by the time Sigrun's mother had got her arm in and turned it there was nothing left in it to turn. It was a big lamb. A tup lamb. That was the trouble, often, with the big ones.

The ewe hadn't looked at it since. She'd licked it, at first. Then stopped. Now she lay with her eyes half shut and her breath going slowly in and out through her nose, and when Sigrun put a bucket in front of her she didn't lift her head to it.

"She'll go," said Ase Holm. She was standing at the hurdle with her shawl round her and her arms bare to the elbow, still red to the elbow, and she looked at the ewe as she looked at the weather. "Give her a day. They do. They lie down and they go."

"She's a good ewe. She's four. She's got three more lambs in her at least."

"Then she'll get up. Or she won't." Her mother wiped her arms on a fistful of straw. "Sigrun. I'm cold, I've been up all night, and there's a gimmer at the top fold who's going to start any time and has hips like a hen. Leave it."

Sigrun didn't leave it.

There was an orphan in the byre at the top of the fold, a twin from the day before, whose mother had taken one lamb and butted the other off until it lay in a corner and bleated itself hoarse. She went and got it. It was small and yellow and all legs, and it yelled in her arms the whole way down. Then she knelt in the wet straw by the ewe, took out her knife, and skinned the dead lamb.

You did it quickly or you didn't do it. Round the neck, round the legs at the knee, and down. Then the skin off like a coat, warm still, and over the orphan's back, with its legs through the leg-holes and the tail flapping, so that it stood there in a dead lamb's jacket looking like something put together by a child. It went on yelling.

Sigrun pushed it under the ewe's nose.

The ewe opened her eyes.

She sniffed the jacket. She sniffed it a long while, as if she were reading it. Then she put her head back down on the ground and shut her eyes again.

"Sigrun," said her mother, from the hurdle.

"She can smell it's his."

"She can smell it's not."

"Then she can get up and tell me so." Sigrun took the ewe by the scruff of her neck in both hands, wool and all, and hauled. "Up. Up, you lazy, useless… *up.* He's hungry. He's yours. Get up and see to him."

The ewe got up.

Not because she'd been asked. Sigrun knew that, even then, even with her hands full of greasy wool and the cold coming up through the knees of her breeches. The ewe got up because a girl was pulling her neck off, and her legs did it, and once she was standing the orphan found the bag and went in under her, butting, and the ewe stood there with her head low and let him, because he was there, and she was too tired to stop it.

That would do for now. In a day or two she'd turn her head and sniff him and decide. They mostly did.

Her mother came and stood beside her in the straw and looked down at the ewe, and the orphan in its borrowed coat, sucking.

"You've hands for it," said Ase Holm, after a while. She didn't say it as if it pleased her.

They walked up to the house in the grey light with the falls loud below them and the whole of the hollow coming out of the dark as they climbed: the turf rampart, the ring of longhouses, the smoke going up straight from the roof-holes in the still air, and in the middle, the chief's hall, its roof just beginning to shine. Shed scale, green and bronze and black and rust, pinned in rows. Every child in the hold had been up on that roof on a dare once, to pull a scale loose and keep it. Sigrun had a bronze one under her mattress, as big as her palm. She'd been eight. Her mother had beaten her for it.

Above the hall, the cliff. Above the cliff, cloud.

The Roosts were waking. She could hear them before she could see them clearly, the grinding and the hissing and the long cries that the hold no longer heard unless a stranger asked about them. A pair of young blacks went out off the high ledges and dropped toward the moor. Others were out already, far off, low over the heather, hunting. On the lower ledges the bonded ones lay in the cave mouths with their riders, or without them; there was a fire lit on Hallam Brecc's ledge, and the black shape of Morrow along the lip of it like a cat on a wall.

And a third of the way up the cliff, under a cave mouth black with a hundred years of fire, nothing moved at all.

"You're looking again," said her mother.

"I'm walking."

"You're walking with your head on one side." Ase stopped at the door of the longhouse and turned round. "Sigrun. You're seventeen. As of this spring you're a woman in this hold, and you can wed, and you can carry an axe on the coast road if anyone's fool enough to ask you, and you can stand up in the hall and say what you like and be ignored like the rest of us. You've stood on that ledge two midsummers. It's enough. People are starting to talk."

"People always talk."

"They're starting to talk about you." Her mother held the door. "Leave the old one be. She's for the pyre. Everyone knows it. The priest's had the second half of it standing up there a year."

"She didn't go on it."

"Not yet," said Ase Holm, and went in.

He had smelled of smoke and honey.

That was most of what Sigrun had of him. A great slow man in a sheepskin coat who smelled of smoke and honey and sang, all the time, under his breath and over it, songs nobody else in the hold knew all the words to, because half of them he'd made up. He had come down the cliff path in the evenings with his saddle on his shoulder, singing, and the children had run after him, and he'd let them.

He'd let Sigrun sit on the old one's neck once. She'd been six. He'd lifted her up in both hands and set her down astride on the bronze, behind the head, where the neck was narrowest and there was a ridge between the plates, and told her to hold on to the ridge and on no account the horns. And the old one had lifted her head, with Sigrun on it, up and up, until the yard was far below and the longhouse roofs were like stones in a beck and the wind was in her ears. And Sigrun had been sick. All down the bronze. And Ragnald had laughed so much he'd had to lean on the dragon's leg, and on the way home he'd made up a song about it, a long one, with a verse for every part of the old one that had got some, and sung it in the hall that night, and for a year after, until she'd hidden in the byre whenever she saw him coming.

He'd been her grandfather's brother. Her grandfather had died before she could remember him and Ragnald had never wed, so it was Ragnald who came at midwinter with a pot of honey for every Holm house in the hold, and Ragnald her mother complained about, because he gave the children honey off the comb and let them chew the wax.

"He bought a charm," her mother used to say. "For those bees. Off some old woman in the hills, I'd swear it. Nobody sits with forty hives in shirtsleeves and comes away unstung."

"He talks to them," Sigrun had said.

"Everybody talks to bees. Bees don't listen."

He'd walked up to them on a spring morning, two years ago this month. The thaw had been on a week. You could hear the beck from the house, high and brown with snowmelt, and water running everywhere, in every crack of the cliff. He went over the shoulder of the hill by the sheep-trod to the south slope, where the hives stood in their rows in the heather, to take the winter covers off. He went on his own. He always did.

Sigrun had been in the yard with a bucket.

She'd heard it first as a sound in the cliff. Then she'd understood that it was coming out of a mouth. Out of the cave a third of the way up, under the black scorch, where the old one lay asleep in the mornings while Ragnald was off with his bees. It went out over the hollow, and down the beck, and on, a scream like nothing alive, so loud the dogs went flat and the bucket slopped over her feet. And the old one came out of the cave. She came out of it falling, as if she'd been thrown out, and opened her wings over the hollow so that the shadow of them went over the whole hold at once, and went up the cliff and over the shoulder of the hill, low, toward the south slope.

By the time the men got there with the shovels she'd already been digging. She'd moved half the hillside. They said you could see, where the rock had come down off the crag in a long grey tongue across the heather, that she'd been throwing boulders the size of byres into the gully with her forefeet, one after another, and that she'd stopped only when there was nothing left on top of him to throw.

The hives were under it too, most of them. The bees came up out of the rock for days after, in a cloud, and went round and round over the place, and wouldn't settle.

They waited for her to go after him. That was what you did. Everyone knew it. A rider dies and the beast goes after, or a beast dies and the rider goes after, and there's nothing to be done, and you don't ask why, any more than you ask why water goes downhill. One life between two. The priest had them build the pyre on Coldtarn's shore, the old way, big enough for two, and laid Ragnald on one half of it and left the other half empty, and the hold went up the mountain and stood in the cold and waited.

Seven days. She didn't go.

On the eighth they lit Ragnald's half, and burned him alone, and poured him into the black water, and the old one lay on the shore beside the cold half of the pyre with her head in the tarn and watched him go down.

And didn't go down after.

She lay there the whole year. Sigrun went up once, in the winter, with her mother and half the hold, because people went up to look, as they'd go to look at a calf born with two heads. The old one lay along the shore with the snow on her back like a second hill, and her head in the water to the eyes, and her breath coming out of the tarn in a slow grey smoke. She didn't move when they came. She didn't move when the priest said the old words over her. She didn't move when a boy threw a stone and hit her on the flank, and his father knocked him down for it.

The second half of the pyre stood beside her all winter with the snow on it.

In the spring she got up. Nobody saw her do it. One morning she was on the shore, and the next morning she was in her cave on the Roosts again, a third of the way up, under the black scorch, as if she'd never left. And she'd lain there since. A whole year. She didn't hunt that anyone saw. The hold sent a sheep up to her ledge every few days, because nobody knew what else to do, and most of them she didn't touch, and the crows had them.

The hold said: she's waiting.

And on two midsummer nights, while the old one lay above it in her cave with her eyes shut, Sigrun Holm had stood on the Choosing Ledge in a clean shirt with her plait pinned up, and not been chosen.

---

The Choosing Ledge runs the length of the cliff's foot, a long shelf of black rock below the lowest caves, as wide as a lane, and on midsummer night the whole hold stands in the hollow below it with torches, looking up.

You stand in a line. Everyone who's in their fifteenth summer or older and hasn't been chosen, from every family, in clean clothes, with your hands at your sides. You don't speak. You don't move. You don't look at the young ones, the old riders tell you, because a young dragon won't come to anyone who's staring at it, and because if you look at them you'll start to want one in particular, and they can smell wanting, and they don't like it.

Then the young ones come down. Yearlings and two-year-olds, off the lower ledges, in a rush, like lambs through a fold gate when it's opened, all at once and pleased with themselves. And they walk the line.

That's all it is. Sigrun had watched it every midsummer since she could stand. A young dragon walks down the line of you, sniffing, looking, in no hurry, and it stops in front of someone, or it doesn't. And if it stops, it stays. It puts its head down, and it stays, and the one it's stopped in front of puts out a hand, and that's the choosing. Sometimes three or four in a night. Sometimes one. Some years none, and the young ones go back up the cliff and the line goes home to bed and nobody says anything about it at breakfast.

She'd stood the first time at fifteen, the midsummer after Ragnald died, when the old one was still lying on the shore of Coldtarn with her head in the water. She'd stood with her hands at her sides and her eyes on the rock in front of her feet, as she'd been told, and her heart going so hard she thought the yearlings must hear it.

A young bronze had stopped in front of her.

She'd seen its feet. That was all she'd let herself see. Two forefeet on the rock in front of hers, bronze, new, the claws still pale at the tips. It had put its nose down to her and sniffed her, slowly, from her boots up, and she'd felt its breath on her neck, hot, and it had found her plait and sniffed that, and sneezed.

Then it went on. To Oda Leck, who was standing next to her. It didn't stop for Oda either. But further down the line a young blue stopped for Oda's cousin, and later in the night came back up the line and stopped for Oda, after all, as if it had forgotten something, and stayed. And Oda had put out her hand and burst into tears, and the hollow had cheered, and Sigrun had stood there beside her with her hands at her sides and her eyes on the rock and kept them there until the young ones had gone back up the cliff and it was over.

The next year, nothing had stopped for her at all.

She'd felt them come along the line. She'd felt the air move. One of them had trodden on her foot. That was the nearest any of them came.

Afterwards she'd gone up the cliff path in the dark, past the riders' ledges, to the place where it turned under the old one's cave, and stood there with her hand on the iron rail, looking up at the cave mouth, black on black. She hadn't known what she'd come for. The old one hadn't moved. You could hear her breathing in there, slow, like the sea. After a while Sigrun had gone down again.

Hallam Brecc had been sitting on his ledge at the turn of the path, with his feet over the drop and Morrow's chin on the rock beside him, and he'd looked at her as she went by and hadn't said anything. She'd liked him for that. She'd never liked him for anything before. He was four years older than her and had a nose like the prow of a boat, and he'd been chosen at sixteen and told everybody about it for a year.

In the evening of the day she'd fostered the orphan, she went up the cliff.

She'd decided it on the walk back down from the top fold at dusk, with the gimmer delivered of twins and both on their feet and her mother gone ahead to the house. She'd stopped on the sheep-trod above the hollow and looked at the cliff, and the cave, black on black, and she'd thought: *she's been lying there a year*. And she'd thought of the ewe in the straw with her nose on the ground, not lifting her head to the bucket. And her hands had closed at her sides, as they closed on a ewe's wool to haul.

She took a lantern. She didn't take anything else. The path went up from the yard behind the steward's barn, cut in the rock, steep, wet with the spray off the falls, with the iron rail on the outside worn bright by hands. It went past the lowest caves, close enough that the smell came out at her, hot stone and iron and burnt hair, and the dragons on the ledges turned their heads to watch her climb, and their eyes caught the lantern and shone.

Hallam was on his ledge. Of course he was. He had a fire going in a brazier and a leg of mutton on a stick over it, and the smoke was blowing flat across his face and he wasn't blinking, which all the riders did and which Sigrun had hated in every one of them since she was eleven.

"Holm," he said. "It's dark."

"I've a lantern."

"Going up?"

"I'm going where I'm going."

"Only there's nothing above me but her." He turned the mutton. Behind him Morrow lifted her head off the rock and looked at Sigrun, a long black head, young, smooth, with the light running down it like water. "And she doesn't like visitors. The priest went up at midwinter to say the words over her and she opened one eye at him and he came down that path faster than I've ever seen an old man move."

"I'm not the priest."

"No," said Hallam, "he's got more sense," and then he looked at her face in the lantern light and stopped grinning, and went back to his mutton, and didn't say anything else.

She went on up.

---

The cave was bigger than she remembered. Or she was smaller, standing in front of it in the dark. The mouth was as high as the hall's gable and black all round with old fire, and the ledge in front of it was scattered with bones and the remains of sheep the hold had sent up and the crows had found, and the smell of those was worse than anything that came out of the cave itself.

She held up the lantern.

The old one lay at the back. She filled it. She lay with her head on her forelegs and her wings folded close along her sides and her tail round her, the end of it lying almost at Sigrun's feet in the mouth of the cave, and her scales were bronze, dull, gone greenish in places with age like the old bell in the hall porch that nobody rang. Her eyes were shut. She was breathing slowly. Every breath came out of her and across the cave floor and out past Sigrun's ankles, warm, smelling of hot stone.

Sigrun had come up the whole cliff path saying it in her head. She'd had it ready on the sheep-trod and she'd had it ready going past Hallam and she had it ready now, all of it, in the right order. She opened her mouth.

Nothing came out.

The old one breathed.

Sigrun put the lantern down on the rock. She wiped her hands on her breeches. They still smelled of the dead lamb, and the ewe, and the fold. Then she stood up straight in the cave mouth, with her plait down her back to her waist and her elbows out because she didn't know what to do with them, and said it anyway, too loud, so that it went into the cave and came back off the walls at her.

"I'm sick of you."

The old one breathed.

"I'm sick of you lying there. A year. Two years. Feeling sorry for yourself, in the dark, with the crows eating your dinner. You're not dying. Everybody knows you're not dying. If you were going to die you'd have done it at Coldtarn with the whole hold watching, and you didn't, so you're not, so you can stop." Her voice cracked. She went on over the crack. "He's dead. My uncle. He's dead and you're not, and I'm sorry, I'm sorry for it, everybody is, but you're not, and you're lying up here like a ewe that won't get up, and the hold's flying its raids without you and doing it badly, and everybody knows that too. The coast men took sixty sheep off the low folds last summer and we got eleven back. Eleven. Because nobody up here can read a field. Because you did the thinking and he did the singing, they say, and now there's nobody doing either."

Her breath was short. She'd come up a long way.

"The hold needs a war-leader," said Sigrun Holm. "A real one. And I'm going to be one. And you're going to help me." She swallowed. "Whether you like it or not."

The cave was quiet.

On the ledge below, she heard Hallam Brecc's mutton spitting in the fire.

And at the back of the cave the old one opened one eye.

It was gold. It was the size of a cartwheel's hub. It caught the lantern and held it, a long way back in the dark, and it came round slowly in its socket until it was on her, and stayed there. It looked at her from her boots to her face, without any hurry at all, as Sigrun had once watched an old drover look at a ewe he'd been offered at Cotter's Cross, and been offered too much for.

Then it closed.

The breath went in, slowly, and came out again across the cave floor, warm, and went past her ankles and out over the ledge into the dark.

Sigrun stood there.

"And you stink," she said, at last, because she had to say something. "This whole ledge stinks. Of dead sheep. You should be ashamed."

She picked up the lantern and went down.

Hallam didn't look up as she passed his ledge. But Morrow did. The young black turned her head on the rock and watched Sigrun go by, all the way to the turn of the path, with an expression on her long smooth face that Sigrun would have called, on a person, amazement.

She went up the next evening, and the next.

She couldn't have said why. She'd been sure, coming down that first night, that she wouldn't. She'd lain awake on the mattress with the bronze scale under it, hot all over in the dark, saying the whole thing over again, every word of it, and hearing how it had sounded coming back off the cave walls, a girl shouting at a hill. She'd thought: *the whole hold will know by morning*. Hallam Brecc would tell it in the hall. Oda Leck would hear. Her mother.

He didn't tell it. Nobody said a word. In the morning the ewe in the bottom fold turned her head and sniffed the orphan in its borrowed coat, a long sniff, and licked it, and Sigrun stood at the hurdle and watched her do it and felt nothing at all but tired.

And at dusk she found she was climbing the cliff path again.

She didn't shout this time. She'd used it all. She stood in the cave mouth with the lantern and said, "It's me," and then felt stupid, and sat down on the rock just inside, with her back against the wall and her knees up, a long way from anything that might open an eye.

She sat there a while. Then, because the quiet was worse than talking, she talked.

She told the old one about the ewe. About the twins at the top fold, and the gimmer with hips like a hen. About her mother, who'd been up three nights and was in a temper that could have lit a brazier. She told it badly. She wasn't a talker; her mother said she had a tongue like a shepherd's crook, good for pulling and no good for anything else. But it was dark, and nobody answered, so it didn't matter.

The old one didn't move. Her eyes stayed shut.

The next night Sigrun took a bucket and a shovel up the cliff and cleared the ledge. All of it. The bones and the fleeces and the black stinking things the crows had left, and she tipped them over the edge into the dark and listened to them go down the cliff, bumping, a long way, and heard someone swear at the bottom. Then she sat down in the mouth of the cave, filthy to the elbow, and said, "There," and the old one breathed out across the cave floor, long and slow, and Sigrun could have sworn it was in a different way.

---

"She listens," said Hallam Brecc.

It was the sixth night, or the seventh. Sigrun had stopped on his ledge on the way down because her lantern had gone out and his fire was there, and because she was cold, and because, if she were honest, she'd started wanting to stop.

He passed her the end of the mutton. She took it.

"How do you know?"

"Morrow says." Hallam tipped his head at the young black, who lay along the lip of the ledge with her chin on her claws, watching the fire. "They can talk, you know. Dragons. To each other. If they can see each other. She can see the old one's cave mouth from here if she cranes." He grinned. "She cranes."

Sigrun looked at Morrow. Morrow looked at the fire.

"What does she say?"

"That she's awake. The old one. The whole time you're there. Every night. Wide awake, with her eyes shut." He turned the stick in his hands. "And asleep the rest. Morrow says she's never seen anything sleep like it. As if she's under water."

Sigrun ate the mutton. It was burnt on the outside and raw in the middle. She ate all of it.

"Does she talk to Morrow?"

"No." Hallam looked out over the hollow, at the hold's lights below, the long fire in the yard, the smoke. "Not since Ragnald, Morrow says. Not to any of them. They talk to her. She doesn't answer." He was quiet. "Morrow's a bit frightened of her. Don't tell her I said."

"I can't tell her anything. She's not mine."

"No," said Hallam. Then: "Holm. Can I ask you something?"

"You'll ask it anyway."

"Why her?" He wasn't grinning. "You could stand again at midsummer. There's a dozen yearlings this year. Good ones. There's a bronze off the high ledges that's nearly as big as Morrow already. You'd only have to stand there."

"I stood there."

"Twice. So stand a third time. People get chosen at eighteen. Twenty."

"And people don't." She threw the mutton bone into the brazier and watched it catch. "Plenty never are. Nobody's surprised any more when I'm not. That's worse. When I go up on that ledge now they're kind about it." She heard her voice and didn't like it. "I'm not standing in a line again for something to sniff my hair and walk past."

"So you'll stand in front of the one that won't even open its eyes?"

"She opened one."

"Once."

"Once is more than the yearlings did," said Sigrun Holm, and got up, and went down the cliff in the dark without a lantern, and nearly broke her neck at the bottom where the path turned under the falls.

---

Her mother found out on the tenth night. It was the spray. Sigrun had come in soaked down one side from the falls and with soot on her face from Hallam's brazier, and Ase Holm had looked at her once in the firelight, and known.

"No," she said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You don't need to. You've been up that cliff." Her mother put down the ladle. "To her."

"I sit there. That's all. I sit in the cave mouth and talk about sheep."

"Sigrun." Ase came round the hearth. She took her daughter's face in her two hard hands and held it, as she held a ewe's head for drenching, and looked into it. "Listen to me. Listen. I know what the hold says. I know you've stood twice and come down twice, and I know what that's like, I stood three times myself and nothing ever stopped for me either, and I married your father and kept his sheep and I've never once been sorry. Do you hear? Never once."

"I hear."

"You don't." Her mother's hands tightened. "That beast is two hundred years old. Three. More. Nobody knows. She's outlived two of them now. Two. She lay on that shore a year waiting to die, and she'll do it again, and one day she'll do it properly, and when she goes, whoever's on the other end of her goes too. One life between two. You know it. You've seen them burn the pairs together." She let go. "I've put your father in that water and I've put Ragnald in it. I'll not put you in it after some old beast who's halfway gone already."

Sigrun stood by the hearth with the smoke going up past her. It stung her eyes. She let it.

"She's not halfway gone," she said.

"How would you know?"

"Because she cleaned her teeth."

Her mother stared at her.

"Last night," said Sigrun. "I took a sheep up. A fresh one, from the bottom fold, the one that broke its leg in the hurdle. And this morning it was gone. All of it. Fleece and all. And when I went up tonight she'd been out on the ledge, there were scratches on the rock where she'd turned round, and she'd been to the spring at the back of the cave and drunk, and she'd…" She didn't know how to say it. "She smelled different. She smelled clean."

"A beast eats a sheep," said Ase Holm. "That's not a choosing."

"I know it's not a choosing."

"Then what is it?"

Sigrun didn't answer. She didn't have an answer. She went up the ladder to the loft and lay down on the mattress with her clothes on and the scale under it, and stared at the turf of the roof, and listened to her mother downstairs not going to bed.

---

She kept going.

The days got longer. The lambing finished. The ewes went up to the high folds with the lambs at foot, the orphan in its coat among them, and the moor went from brown to green in a week, and the larks came. Down in the hold they began to talk about midsummer: who'd stand, whose daughter, whose son, whether the big bronze yearling off the high ledges would choose a Varr or a Brecc or some shepherd's girl from the folds and spite them all. Oda Leck rode her young blue over the hollow every morning, low, showing off, and everyone pretended not to look.

Sigrun went up the cliff every evening and sat in the mouth of the cave.

She talked less. She'd run out of sheep. Some nights she sat there an hour and didn't say a word, and the old one lay at the back with her eyes shut and breathed, and the light went out of the sky over the moor, and the stars came, and the hold's fires lit one by one below, and it was not unpleasant. It was the only place in the whole of Varrhold where nobody was being kind to her.

Once she talked about Ragnald.

She hadn't meant to. It came out. She'd been telling the old one about the bees, how the Ewe Hollow man had come up for the hives that were left, after, with a cart, and how her mother had sold them to him for less than they were worth because she couldn't stand to look at them. How Sigrun had gone up over the shoulder to the south slope afterwards, on her own, and stood on the rock where the hives had been, and there'd been one bee left. One. Going round and round over the stones, in the heather, looking for something.

"He used to sing to them," she said. "Did you know that? You must have. All the time. He sang to everything. Mother said he'd bought a charm for them off some old woman. He hadn't. He just sang. I used to think…"

She stopped.

At the back of the cave, in the dark, the old one had lifted her head.

Only a little. A hand's breadth off her forelegs. But Sigrun heard the scales move against the rock, a long dry sound like a sack being dragged across a floor, and she saw the lantern-light go down the great bronze neck as it rose and stop, and stay there.

The eyes were shut.

Sigrun didn't move. She sat against the wall with her knees up and her heart going, and the old one held her head there, off the rock, in the dark, with her eyes shut, for as long as it would take to sing a verse.

Then she laid it down again.

"He sang badly," said Sigrun, very quietly. "Didn't he."

The breath came out of the cave, slow, and went past her into the night.

---

On the last night she went up to say she wasn't coming any more.

She'd decided it in the top fold that afternoon, with the sun on her back and a ewe's foot in her lap, paring the horn. It came to her as plain as weather. Twenty-six nights. She'd counted them on the doorpost of the byre with her knife, one notch a night, and that was a month near enough, and in a month the old one had lifted her head once. And midsummer was eleven days off.

Her mother was right. That was the worst of it. Her mother was right, and Hallam Brecc was right, and the hold was right. You stood in the line. You kept your hands at your sides. If something stopped, it stopped, and if it didn't, you married a shepherd and kept his sheep and were never once sorry.

She'd stand. A third time. With her plait pinned up and her eyes on the rock.

She climbed the cliff path in the long light, the sun still up behind the Scarfell and the hollow already in shadow, with the spray off the falls blowing across her and her hand sliding on the bright rail. Hallam was on his ledge, mending a strap. He looked up as she came past and opened his mouth, and looked at her face, and shut it again. Morrow watched her all the way to the turn.

The ledge in front of the old one's cave was clean. It had stayed clean. Sigrun saw to it.

She stood in the mouth of the cave. She didn't sit down.

"I'm not coming any more," she said.

The old one lay at the back with her eyes shut.

"I'm going to stand at midsummer. Mother wants me to. Everyone does. And I'm…" She stopped. She'd had this ready too, on the path, and it had gone, like the first night. She said what was left. "I'm tired. That's all. I'm tired of coming up here. I'm tired of being the girl who goes up the cliff to the old one every night, that's what they call me now in the hall, I've heard them. I'm tired of you not looking at me." She heard her voice go thin and high, a child's voice, and hated it. "The yearlings don't look at me either. Nothing does. I don't know what's wrong with me. Something is. Something must be. Everyone else, they stand there and something comes. And I stand there, and I stand here, and I keep my hands at my sides, I do everything right, and nothing…"

She stopped. There wasn't anything else.

"So I'm done," said Sigrun Holm. "That's all. I just thought I should say. Since you listen. Morrow says you listen."

The old one breathed.

Sigrun turned round. She went to the edge of the ledge, where the path came up, and put her hand on the cold rail, and stood there a moment looking out over the hollow, the hold, the rampart, the long fire in the yard, the moor going away green and gold into the evening. Oda Leck's young blue was coming in low over the heather, with Oda on its neck, laughing at something.

Behind her, stone moved.

It was a long sound. It started at the back of the cave and came forward, a grinding and a scraping and a rattle of small stones going down, as if the cliff itself had decided to come out for a look. Sigrun felt it through her boots. She felt it in the rail under her hand. On the ledge below, Hallam Brecc's strap fell off his knee and over the edge, and he didn't go after it.

She turned round.

The old one was standing up.

She was so big standing up. Sigrun had seen her lying down for two years and had forgotten. She stood in the cave with her head under the roof of it and her shoulders filling it from wall to wall, and the light from the sky came in at the mouth and found her chest, the long plates of it, and ran down them, bronze, and the dust of a year was coming off her in the light like smoke off a fire. Her wings shifted along her sides. Her tail came round across the cave floor with a sound like a cart in a lane.

She came forward.

One foot. Then another. Slowly, as if she'd forgotten how and was having to think about each one. Her claws went down on the rock at the cave mouth, one, two, as broad as a door, and the ledge took her weight and Sigrun felt it take it. Then she was in the mouth, filling it, with the evening on her, and her head came down.

Down and down. Past Sigrun's head. Past her shoulder. It came down out of the high dark of the cave roof on its long bronze neck until it was in front of her, level with her, so close that the breath of it went over her and lifted the loose hair off her face, hot, smelling of stone, and the great jaw was a hand's width from her chest.

And it stopped.

And it stayed.

Sigrun stood with her back to the drop and her hands at her sides.

She knew what it was. Every child in Varrhold knew what it was. She'd watched it every midsummer since she could stand: a young dragon on the Choosing Ledge, walking the line, and stopping in front of somebody, and putting its head down, and staying. She'd never seen an old one do it. Nobody had, except the oldest in the hold, the summer she chose Ragnald.

Both the eyes were open.

They were gold. They were as big as cartwheel hubs, one on either side of the long bronze face, and in the evening light she could see all the way into them, a long way, further than she wanted, and there was something in there looking back at her. There was no kindness in it and no unkindness. It was very old and very tired, and it was looking at her as if it had been looking at her for a month, every night, from behind its eyelids, and had come at last, reluctantly, to a decision it didn't like.

Sigrun put out her hand.

She didn't decide to. Afterwards she'd say she did, when people asked, because it sounded better. She didn't. Her hand went out as it went out to a ewe's neck, and she laid it flat on the great bronze jaw, below the eye, where the scales were small and smooth and warm as a hearthstone.

---

It went in through her hand.

That was the first thing. Something went in, through the palm, up the arm, into her chest, and on, down, into the middle of her, into a place she hadn't known she had. And it was cold. She hadn't expected that. She'd thought it would be warm, like the scales. It was cold and it was deep and it went down and down, and she went with it, as if she'd stepped off the rim of Coldtarn into the black water and it had closed over her head and she was falling, slowly, through a dark that had no bottom.

There was grief in it.

So much of it. It came up round her in the dark like the water, from every side. Not hers. She knew grief; she'd put her father in Coldtarn when she was four and remembered nothing of it but cold feet, and she'd put Ragnald in it, and cried for a week. This was older and bigger than either, and it had no edges. It had been lying here in the dark two years. It was a great slow man walking up a hill in the morning, singing. It was rock. It was the sound of the rock. It was digging, digging with both forefeet until the claws broke, and finding him, and knowing before she found him. It was a year with her head in the black water, waiting for the pull to come down and finish it, and wanting it to, wanting it more than she had ever wanted anything, and the pull not coming.

Sigrun was crying. She could feel it on her face, far off, up there in the air somewhere where her body was standing on the ledge with its hand on a dragon.

And then she was crying for something else. She felt it go out of her. Down the hand. Into the dark. Her own grief, small and hot, the thing she'd carried down off the Choosing Ledge two midsummers running with her eyes on the rock: *nothing comes, nothing ever comes, what's wrong with me*. It went down into the old one's dark like a stone into the tarn, and she felt the old one feel it. Feel it land. She felt the whole great cold depth of her turn, slowly, toward that small hot stone, as you'd turn toward a coal in the snow.

And there, a long way down, further than the grief, at the very bottom where she couldn't see, there was something hard.

She touched it. She didn't mean to. It was only there. A place where the dark was harder than the rest, like a break in a bone that's healed. A ridge. She'd felt them in sheep, running her hands down a leg in the fold, the old break gone hard and thick under the wool. She had no word for what this was. She knew only that the old one flinched from her touching it, all of her, the whole depth, and she took her hand away from it in the dark.

Then something closed in her. In the middle of her chest, where it had gone in. Like a hand closing. Like a root going down into the ground and taking hold.

She felt it take hold on both sides.

---

*You are very loud, child.*

It was in her head. Not in her ears. In her head, from inside, as if she'd thought it herself, except that she'd never thought anything so slowly in her life. Each word came up separate, like a stone lifted out of deep water and set down.

Sigrun was on her knees on the ledge. She didn't remember kneeling. Her hand was still on the jaw. The old one's head was still there, a hand's width from her, and the eyes were still open, gold, and the light had gone red over the moor.

"You," she said. Her voice came out a croak. "You could've said so. A month ago."

*I could.*

"Twenty-six nights. I counted them. On the byre door."

*I know. I watched you cut them.* A pause, long, the breath going in and out against her face. *I did not like it.*

Sigrun knelt there.

*I did not like any of it,* said the old one. *I did not like being shouted at, or being told I stank, though I did. I did not like you clearing my ledge with a shovel and throwing my dinners down the cliff onto the steward's barn. He has been to the hall about it. I did not like the sheep. It was a good sheep.* The great eyes looked at her. *And I did not like you telling me about the bee.*

"I'm sorry."

*Don't be.* The voice was very slow. *It is not your fault I did not like it. It is only that I had not wanted anything for two years, and it was easier. And then a girl came up the cliff with a lantern and made me want things. To eat. To be clean. To hear about the gimmer at the top fold.* Something went through the bond, then, that Sigrun had no name for at all, and it took her a while, kneeling there, to understand it was the old one being amused, very far down. *To find out whether the gimmer had twins.*

"She did."

*I know. You said. Twice.*

Sigrun laughed. It came out of her wet and cracked, and she put her other hand over her mouth, and then took it away, because she wanted both hands on the dragon. She put it beside the first, on the jaw.

"I'm your third," she said.

*You are.*

"Everyone said you were waiting to die."

*Everyone says a great many things about me.* The head lifted, a little, and the eyes went past her, out over the hollow, the hold, the fires lighting in the dusk. *I remember you, you know. Ragnald put you on my neck when you were six, and you were sick on me. All down my left side. He sang about it for a year.*

"I know. I hid in the byre."

*I know you did. I could see you from here.* The old one breathed out, long, warm, across her. *He was very pleased with that song.*

---

When she went down the cliff that night it was full dark, and she didn't need the lantern.

That was the strange thing. She didn't notice it until she was nearly at the bottom. She'd come down the whole path, past the ledges and the caves and the eyes catching no light, her hand on the rail, and her feet had found every step as if she'd been walking it fifty years, and when she got to the turn under the falls where she'd nearly broken her neck before, she just stepped round it. She stopped there in the spray and looked back up.

The old one was in the mouth of her cave. Sigrun couldn't see her. She could feel her. A great slow warmth up there in the dark, a third of the way up the cliff, like a banked fire in another room.

*Go home, child. Your mother has been standing in her door since the moon came up.*

"How do you know?"

*Because I can see her.*

Hallam Brecc had been on his ledge when she passed. He hadn't said anything. He'd stood up as she came by, all the way up, as the riders stood when the chief came into the hall. Behind him Morrow had lifted her long black head off the rock and held it up, and opened her mouth, and the sound that came out of her was low and long and went round the hollow and came back.

Then the whole cliff had answered.

All of them. Every dragon on the Roosts, bonded and unbonded, the young ones on the high ledges and the riders' beasts in the cave mouths and the ones out on the moor coming home late. They'd lifted their heads in the dark and opened their mouths and made the sound together, and it went into the rock and came up through Sigrun's boots, and the dogs in the hold had gone mad, and all over Varrhold she'd seen doors open in the dark and the firelight come out of them and people stand in the light looking up.

Her mother had been one of them.

She was standing in her door still when Sigrun came across the yard. She had a shawl round her, and the fire behind her, and her face in shadow. She didn't come out. She watched Sigrun come all the way across the yard and stop in front of her.

"So," said Ase Holm.

"Mother."

"I heard them." Her mother looked past her, up, at the cliff in the dark. "Half the Scarfell heard them. I thought it was the pull. I thought she'd gone at last, and they were crying her. I thought, there, it's over, she'll stop going up." Her voice didn't change at all. "And then I thought, no. They didn't cry like that for Ragnald."

"No."

Her mother looked at her. Then she put up one hand, and took hold of the end of Sigrun's plait, where it hung over her shoulder, and held it in her fist, as she'd held it when Sigrun was small and wouldn't keep still to have it done.

"You'll go when she goes," she said.

"I know."

"You don't. You can't. Nobody does at seventeen." She let go of the plait. "Come in. You're soaked."

---

In the morning the hearth smoked.

It always did, when the wind was in the north; it came down the roof-hole and lay along the room in a grey sheet at the height of your face, and everyone in the longhouse went about with their eyes streaming and swore at it. Sigrun came down the ladder into it and went to the hearth and knelt to blow up the fire, as she'd done every morning of her life, and the smoke came down round her head.

She looked into it.

She was halfway through blowing before she understood. Her eyes were open. Wide open, in the thick of it, with the smoke going across them, and they didn't sting. They didn't water. She could see the coals through the grey as clearly as if there were nothing there.

She knelt there on the hearthstones with her mouth open.

Behind her, her mother had stopped, halfway across the room with the milk pail. Sigrun turned round. Ase Holm stood in the smoke with the pail in her hand and looked at her daughter's face, at her open eyes, not blinking, and she put the pail down on the floor very carefully, as if it were full to the brim, which it wasn't. Then she turned round and went out to the byre.

Sigrun stayed kneeling.

She'd seen it all her life. The riders on their ledges with the brazier smoke blowing flat across them, not narrowing an eye. She'd hated them for it. It had seemed like showing off.

She put her hand inside her shirt, over her breastbone, where it had ached a little when she woke, like a bruise. There was a mark there. She'd felt it in the dark in the loft and thought it was a scab. In the grey light by the hearth she pulled the shirt open and looked down at it. Bronze. The size of the end of her thumb. Not raised. In the skin, as if it had always been there, like a freckle, and was the colour of an old bell.

*Good morning,* said the old one, from the cliff. Slow, from far up, as clear as if she were in the room. *You're awake very early.*

"There's lambs," said Sigrun, out loud, to the empty longhouse.

*There are always lambs.*

"Well, I can't help that, can I," said Sigrun. "Old girl."

There was a silence from the cliff. It went on for some time.

*I have been called a great many things in this hold,* said the old one at last, *and I do not think I have ever been called that.*

"You'd better get used to it."

*I am beginning to think,* said the old one, *that I had better get used to a number of things.*

---

They flew on the fourth day.

It took the four days to find the saddle. Ragnald's was in the steward's barn, on a peg at the back, where it had hung since the spring he died, because nobody in the hold had liked to be the one to decide what to do with it. It was huge. It had been made for a huge man. Sigrun sat in it on the barn floor and her feet hung a foot above the stirrups and the pommel came up to her chest, and the steward, who had never in his life been heard to laugh, stood in the barn door and looked at her and went away, and she heard him laughing all the way across the yard.

Her mother took the stirrup-leathers up. She did it in the evening by the fire with an awl and waxed thread, without a word, every stitch pulled tight, and at the end of it she bit the thread off and put the saddle down on the floor in front of Sigrun and went to bed.

Hallam helped her carry it up the cliff. It took both of them. He talked the whole way. He told her how to sit and how to hold and when to lean and what to do if she was sick, and Sigrun said she knew, and he said she didn't, and Morrow came up the cliff face beside them from ledge to ledge, watching, and once, at the turn under the falls, made a noise that Hallam said was a laugh.

"At what?"

"She won't say." Hallam shifted the weight of the saddle on his shoulder. "She says it's between dragons."

The old one was on her ledge. She'd been out every day since. She'd hunted; she'd drunk; she'd lain on the ledge in the morning sun with her wings half open and let the light into them, and the hold had come out into the yard to watch her do it, as they'd gone up to Coldtarn to watch her lie in the snow. She stood still while they put the saddle on her. Hallam showed Sigrun the girths. There were three, under the chest, and the buckles were as big as Sigrun's fist, and she pulled the last one tight with her boot against the old one's side.

*Not that tight.*

"Hallam says…"

*Hallam Brecc has never saddled me in his life. Ragnald had it a hole looser. He liked his dinner.*

She let it out a hole. Hallam watched her do it and didn't ask why, and then he looked at her, quick, and at the old one, and Sigrun saw him understand that she'd been told, and saw something go across his face, a long-nosed boy who'd been chosen at sixteen and told everyone for a year. He stepped back.

"Well," he said. "Go on, then."

She climbed up.

It was a long way. The saddle sat in the hollow at the root of the neck, before the wings, and from up there the ledge was a narrow thing far below and Hallam was a boy with his face turned up and the hold was a ring of stones in a green bowl. She put her feet in Ragnald's stirrups. They fitted. She took hold of the pommel, and then let go of it, and put her hand flat on the bronze in front of her instead.

*Hold the pommel,* said the old one. *Not my horns. I don't like it.*

Sigrun held the pommel.

And the old one went off the ledge.

---

There is a moment, the riders say, the first time, when you're sure you're going to die. The ledge goes out from under you and there's nothing at all but air, and the ground coming up, and the cliff going by like a wall falling the wrong way, and your stomach somewhere above your head. Every rider in the hold has had it. Every one of them will tell you about it in the hall if you give them the chance, and most of them will tell you they didn't scream.

Sigrun screamed.

She screamed the whole way down the cliff, with her face in the bronze and her fists locked on the pommel and Ragnald's saddle creaking under her, and then the wings opened.

She felt it. Under her, through the saddle, through the legs gripping, through the hand in the middle of her chest. The great muscles of the shoulders going hard. A jolt that went up her spine into her teeth. And the fall stopping. Not stopping: turning. Going over into something else, a long slow swing out over the hollow like a rope-swing over the beck in summer, and then up, up, and the cold air going past, and the roar of the falls going away below.

*Open your eyes.*

She opened them.

They were over the moor. The whole of it, spread out below in the sun, green and brown and gold, going away and away in long backs and hollows to the edge of the world. The Scarfell went up on the left into cloud, grey and white and black, with snow still in the gullies. Hollow Beck was a thread of silver running down out of the hills, past the hold, which was nothing, a ring of grey stones and a glint of scale-roof no bigger than a brooch, and on down, a thread among the folds, toward a long blue haze in the west that she knew, without being told, was the river. Sheep were white dots. The Drover's Way was a pale scratch in the heather. A hawk went under them, a long way under, and she looked down on the back of it.

The old one's wings went up and down on either side of her, slow, enormous. Every beat was like a sail filling.

Sigrun found she'd stopped screaming. She found she was laughing.

*Yes,* said the old one. *They all do that too.*

---

She took her up to Coldtarn.

Sigrun didn't ask where they were going. She knew by the second wingbeat, by how the old one turned in the air above the moor, north and up, into the hills, and by something in the bond that went quiet and set, like a jaw. She held the pommel and said nothing.

They came over the last rise and the tarn was below them.

From the air it was even blacker. A hole in the world, in its cup of black rock, so still that the cliffs round it stood upside down in it to the last stone and the sky lay in the middle of it, white, as if you could fall through the tarn into another sky underneath. The shore was a long slope of grey scree going down to the edge. On it, near the black flat stone that ran out into the water like a jetty, there was a heap of old timber, grey and split and half fallen in, with the snow-stain still on it.

The other half of the pyre. Nobody had taken it down.

The old one went round once, high, over the tarn. Then she came down on the rim above the shore, on the flat of the rock there, and folded her wings, and lay down.

Sigrun sat in the saddle a while.

Then she undid the strap and climbed down. Her legs shook when she stood on them. She walked a little way along the rim, to get the blood back, and found a flat rock just under the edge, out of the wind, with the shape of a seat in it, as if somebody had been sitting there a long time. She sat down. Her back was to the wind and her face was to the water.

The old one had put her head out over the rim and down. She held it over the tarn, low, her chin almost on the water, with her eyes on it. Not moving. Her breath went out over the black surface and didn't mark it.

Sigrun pulled her cloak round her. Up here it was still winter, in the shade of the rocks. There was old snow in the cracks.

"Here?" she said, after a while.

*Here.* The word came up very slowly. *A year. I could see him from here. Not him. Where he went in.* The great head didn't move. *I used to think, if I looked long enough, I would see where he'd got to. How far down. They say you can see them, if you look long enough. The old ones say it. A long line of them, going down.*

"Did you?"

*No.*

The wind came over the rim behind her and went out across the water and didn't leave a mark on it.

Sigrun reached back without looking, as she'd seen the riders do, and found the bronze of the foreleg behind her, and put her hand on it. It was warm. The bond in the middle of her chest was warm too, banked, and she could feel the old one there on the other end of it, all of her, the great slow cold depth with the grief still in it, less now, and the small hot coal Sigrun had dropped in, still glowing, and at the very bottom, where she didn't go, the hard place.

"Who was the first?" she said.

It came out before she'd thought about it. She'd wanted to ask since the cave mouth. Everyone in the hold wanted to know. Ragnald hadn't known, people said; he'd asked once and been answered with nothing, and he'd laughed and said he could live without, and sung about that too.

The old one didn't answer.

She didn't refuse. Sigrun felt that. Nothing pushed her off. Nothing shut. It was only that the bond went quiet, all the way down, and she leaned into the quiet, and it went on going down, further than she could follow, further than the grief, further than the hard place, into a dark that didn't end. Like dropping a stone in the middle of the tarn and listening and never hearing it land.

She waited. The old one lay with her head over the water.

Sigrun thought of the old women in the hold, and the first Varr standing in the beck with his ewes round his knees asking leave, and the hold taking a silence for yes for longer than anybody could remember. She thought of the old one lying here a year with her head in the water and the snow on her back, not dying. She thought of the cave mouth, and the great head coming down out of the dark, slowly, and stopping in front of a girl nothing had ever stopped for.

"They say you're waiting," she said. "In the hold. They've always said it. Before Ragnald. Before anyone. That you're waiting for something." She kept her hand on the foreleg. "What is it? What are you waiting for?"

The old one didn't move.

Her eyes stayed on the water. The breath went in and out of her, slow, warm, over the black surface of Coldtarn, and the cliffs stood upside down in it, and the white sky lay in the middle of it, and down the bond there was only the quiet, going down, and down, and Sigrun sat on the flat rock under the rim with her hand on the bronze and listened to it go.
