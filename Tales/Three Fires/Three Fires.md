---

The Clan says a scout's dragon is a question with wings on.

Imra said the Clan said it. Lisska had never heard anyone else say it, and she had listened, on the ledges and the long-house thatch and the stable roof, for years. So was it the Clan's saying, or was it Imra's? And if it was Imra's, why give it to the Clan? Imra gave things away like that. Apples, sometimes, if Lisska had been good. Her dinner, once, to a shepherd's dog. Sayings, to anyone who'd stand still.

There were a great many questions. That was the trouble with being a scout, or it was the trouble with being Lisska, and Imra said they were the same trouble. A scout went out ahead and looked at everything and came back and said what she'd seen. But looking at everything only made more of it. What was under that rock? Why did that sheep stand on its own? Why were there six crows on the dyke and not seven, when yesterday there'd been seven? Where did the seventh go?

Where do things go?

---

The long stable at Varrhold had a slate roof, and the slates held the sun.

That was the first thing about it. The second was the smell coming up between them, of horses and hay and warm dung, and under that the faint high fear horses always had when she lay on their roof, which she liked, though Imra said she shouldn't. The third thing was that the stable stood by the gate, and anyone coming into the yard came under it.

Teo came under it. Every day. He'd come out of the hall with his long face and his coat open, and look up, and see her, and stop.

He'd bend for an apple from the basket by the door. He'd throw it. Not to her; at her. It would bounce off her neck or her nose or the slates beside her and roll down into the gutter, and he'd shout up at her, and she'd put her head over the edge and look at him upside down until he laughed.

*Get off my stable, you green menace.*

That was what he shouted. She knew because Imra told her, every time, through the bond, a little behind. Imra told her everything people said. People's mouth-sounds were noise to Lisska unless they came through Imra first. What was a menace? Something that frightened horses. Did he want her off? No, Imra said. It meant he liked her. Then why did he throw things? Because he was a Varr, Imra said, and the Varr only knew how to like things by shouting at them.

She'd liked that. She'd kept it.

She kept things. Imra said she kept too many.

---

Teo didn't come under the roof any more.

She'd been there four days. She'd come down off the cliff the night he didn't come back from the ford, when Imra came up the cave ledge with her face gone strange and sat by the brazier and didn't speak and didn't speak, and then put her hands over her face and made a sound Lisska had never heard her make. Lisska had felt it come through the bond like the cold that comes off the scree at night. She'd asked what it was. She'd asked and asked.

*Teo,* Imra said at last. *Teo's dead.*

So Lisska went down to the stable roof, because that was where he came.

Deer come back to the same pool at dusk. You learn that hunting the woods behind the hold. You lie up in the bracken by the water and wait, and they come, because it's their water and that's where they come. If you lie where someone comes, don't they come?

He didn't come.

On the second evening they built a square of wood in the middle of the yard, taller than a man, and put something long and grey on top of it, and the whole of the west side stood round it in the rain. Arn put fire in it. And Lisska went up off the roof, because Aurveth called her and Kharrak called her, and the three of them went round over the fire, low, three times, round and round, with the smoke tearing sideways off their wings. Then they called, all three. She'd called as loud as she could. She'd looked down into the fire the whole time.

He wasn't in it. She looked. There was only the long dark shape going black in the middle of the gold, and that wasn't anybody.

When the others went up into the dark she'd gone up through the smoke, by herself, high, higher than she'd ever gone in rain, because Imra said that was where they went. Up. The long road up, the priest said; Imra told her. So she went up the long road, and looked. There was rain, and then cloud, and then more rain. There was nothing in it at all but wet.

Where was the road? Was it a road you had to walk? Why would anybody walk up, when she could have carried him?

She came back down to the roof. The slates were cold. She lay on them anyway.

---

Imra came up the ladder every evening.

She'd climb onto the stable roof at the gable end, where the ladder came up to the loft door, and walk along the ridge in the dusk with her arms out, quick and easy, and sit down by Lisska's neck with her back against the green scales and her knees pulled up. She didn't say much. She'd been different since the ford. Her hair was still black and loose and blowing, her face was still narrow and brown, but the laugh had gone out of it, and something hard and flat had gone into the bond where the laugh used to be, like a slate laid over a hole.

"You can come off now," Imra said, on the third evening. Lisska heard the sound of it and the sense of it, a little behind. *You can come off. He's not coming.*

*I know.*

*Then come off.*

*Why?*

Imra didn't answer that. She put her hand flat on Lisska's neck and kept it there.

*Where's he gone?* said Lisska.

*I told you.*

*Tell me again.*

*Up,* said Imra. *Into the smoke. The long road up, where they all go.*

*I went up. He wasn't there.*

*I know, love.*

*Then where is he?*

Imra's hand didn't move. Below them in the yard the black square where Teo's fire had been was going grey at the edges, and nobody walked across it. Beyond it there had been another fire, not long ago, the east one, with the men in plain grey round it. It was out. The space round it was only space now, with the rain on it.

How many fires does a hold need? Sigrun had said Varrhold had one fire for four hundred years. Then there'd been two. Now there was one again, and a black square, and nobody on the other side. Was that better?

*She'll stop,* Imra had said to the farmer, at the pyre, down in the yard. Lisska had heard it from the roof. *They always stop.*

Lisska didn't stop. She lay on the roof and asked.

---

On the fourth evening a boy came up the Way on a pony, at the gallop, in the last of the light.

Lisska saw him a long way off. She saw everything a long way off; that was what she was for. A small dark shape on the pale scar of the Way where it came over the rise, going too fast for the slope, and the pony's neck white with lather. She lifted her head off the slates and watched him come all the way down into the hollow and under the gate and under her, and the pony stopped so hard it nearly sat down, and the boy fell off it shouting.

Noise. A boy's sounds, high and cracking. Men came out of the long-houses. Arn came out of the hall, without his cloak, and the boy said it again, and Arn stood very still and then started shouting too.

Then everybody was running. Men for the horses. Under her the stable woke all at once, stamping and calling, and the grooms ran in and the horses screamed at being saddled in a hurry and at her on their roof both together.

*What is it?* she said. She couldn't find Imra. *What is it? What is he saying? Imra? What?*

Imra came up the ladder fast. She had an apple in her hand.

She held it out, flat on her palm. Lisska looked at it.

*Was I good?*

"No," said Imra, aloud, and through the bond, *no, you've been a lump on a roof for four days.* "Eat it anyway."

Lisska ate it. Her tongue came out and took it off Imra's palm and it was gone, sweet and cold, a late one from the hold's trees. Imra wiped her palm on her coat.

*Ulric's carts,* Imra said. *At Harrow's End. Tonight, in the dark, to load Hob the miller's flour, forty sacks of it, that Hob sold to Ulric on the green. Arn's going down. Now. Forty riders. Dag's going.* She looked at Lisska. *So are we.*

*Harrow's End,* said Lisska.

*Yes.*

*The ghost's? The barley one?* She felt herself go bright, all down her length, before she could help it. *The small one's? With the two cups? Will she be there? Will she see us? Will she come out?*

"Lisska."

*Will she?*

*She'll be asleep,* said Imra. *It's night. It's a war.*

*Do small ones sleep when there are carts?*

Imra almost smiled. Lisska felt it start, down in the bond, and stop.

*Listen to me,* Imra said. *Lisska. Listen.* She took hold of the horn over Lisska's left eye, which she only did when she meant it. *We look. We count. We come back and tell Arn. We keep low. And we stay away from Aurveth.*

*Will Aurveth be there?*

*If Sigrun hears of it.*

*Will Sigrun hear of it?*

*Sigrun hears everything.*

*Like me?*

*Nobody's like you.* Imra let go of the horn. *Whatever happens. Do you hear me? If Aurveth comes, we go wide. We don't go near her. She'd break you in half and she wouldn't even mean to.*

*She wouldn't. She's Aurveth.*

*That's why,* said Imra, which wasn't an answer, and Imra always said that wasn't an answer when Lisska gave one like it. Lisska opened her mouth to say so. Imra was already going back along the ridge to the loft door for the saddle.

---

The saddle was the light one. The scout's saddle: a pad, two short horns to hold, a girth strap and two leg-straps and nothing else, because the Clan's best scout didn't want to carry anything she didn't need, and Lisska didn't want to carry anything at all. Imra dropped it out of the loft door onto the slates and came after it and threw it over Lisska's withers in front of the wings, and bent under her to buckle the girth.

The girth strap was cracked. It had been cracked since the end of summer, where the buckle bit, and Imra had said she'd mend it tomorrow every day for a month. She said it again now, under her breath, pulling it tight.

*Tomorrow?* said Lisska.

"Shut up."

They went up to the cave first. Imra wanted her bow.

The cave was a third of the way up the cliff, on the broad ledge with the brazier, and it had been three dragons' cave since before Lisska could fly. Aurveth at the back, filling it, old and bronze and slow. Kharrak on the left. Lisska wherever she liked, which was usually on top of Kharrak, until he moved. Now the back of the cave was empty. It had been empty for two nights. The great smooth place in the floor where Aurveth had lain for longer than anybody knew was cold, and still had her shape worn into it, and smelled of her.

Was it cold, at Skarrow? Was there a cave? Did Aurveth have room?

Kharrak was on the ledge, standing, with Dag on him already, and his wings half open. The broken spine on his neck caught the brazier light.

*Small,* he said.

*Big,* said Lisska.

*Keep up.*

*Keep up? Me? Have you seen yourself fly? You fly like a cart going downhill.*

Kharrak made the millstone noise in his chest. Lisska had known him all her life and she still didn't know if it was a laugh. Did Kharrak know? Did Dag? Dag was squat and broad on his back with his shaved head shining and his axe across his knees, and he looked at Imra coming up onto the ledge with her bow, and looked at Lisska.

"She's been on that roof four days," he said. Imra gave it to her, a little behind. "She fit to fly?"

"She's fit."

"She going to sit on a roof at Harrow's End and ask questions?"

"She'll do what I tell her."

"First time for everything," said Dag Rorke.

Imra slung the bow and swung up into the scout's saddle and buckled the leg-straps, and put her hand on Lisska's neck.

"She remembers everything," she said to Dag. "Every crow on every dyke. Every word you've ever said about her. It's the most annoying thing about her."

Dag looked at Lisska a moment longer. Then he put his hand on Kharrak's neck, and Kharrak stepped off the ledge into the dark.

The Drover's Way went down off the moor in a long pale line through the heather, and Arn's riders went down it with torches.

From above they were a string of sparks going the wrong way, downhill, a long string, forty and more, and the hooves came up to her as a small hard drumming under the wind. Lisska went ahead of them. She couldn't help it. She went ahead and down to the bottom of the next slope to see what was there, and nothing was, and came back, and went ahead again, and Imra let her, because that was looking, and looking was the job.

It was cold. No moon. The stars were very sharp, as they are when the frost is coming, and the heather smelled of it already, and there were sheep on the moor that lifted their heads as she went over and put them down again, because sheep are stupid. Kharrak flew above the torches, high and heavy and steady, a dark shape cutting the stars off. Every so often she heard Dag's voice from up there, short. She didn't ask what it said.

Imra sat light on her. Imra always sat light. She sat forward over the pad with her knees in, close along Lisska's neck in the wind, so that the two of them made one long shape in the air, and that was how Lisska liked it. Through the bond, she could feel the hard flat thing in Imra still, where the laugh had been. It was there all the way down the Way. It was there past the ford on Hollow Beck, where Imra wouldn't look down and Lisska did, and saw the black gorse, and the stones, and nothing on them.

They came off the moor. The Way went down between fields toward the river. Far off ahead, at the bottom of everything, there were lights in a valley.

*Is that it?* said Lisska. *Is that Harrow's End? Is that the mill? That's a lot of lanterns. Those are the carts. Three carts. They're loading. I can hear the sacks.* She could; a soft thump, and a soft thump, a long way down. *What happens to flour if Kharrak burns it?*

*It burns.*

*Does it go white? It's white already. Does it go whiter?* She thought about it, with the wind going past. *If Kharrak burns forty sacks of flour, will he come out white? All over? Like the ghost?*

Imra laughed.

It came out of her in a burst, aloud, into the wind, before she could stop it, and it came down the bond at the same time, all of it, warm. It came up under Lisska like warm air coming up off a south crag on a spring morning, that lifts you, so that you go up without beating, without anything, just lifted.

Four days. Lisska hadn't felt it in four days.

*Again,* she said. *Do it again. Imra. Again?*

"Don't push it," said Imra, and her voice was still laughing, a bit, at the edges. Her hand came down hard and flat on Lisska's neck, and stayed.

Lisska kept it. The laugh, and the hand, and the cold and the stars and the smell of frost on the heather and the sacks going thump, far off, in the valley. She put it with the other things. With the apples, and the roof, and *green menace*, and the small one in the yard at Upper Rush with a cup of water in each hand and her face turned up.

What would Kharrak look like white?

---

From above, Harrow's End was a bowl with a river at the bottom.

She went round it once, high, with the cold coming off the water, and looked at all of it. That was the job.

The mill at the bottom of the valley, by the beck, with the wheel stopped and the lanterns all over its yard and three carts drawn up with men on them, loading. A long low house by the mill with light in its door and men coming in and out of it. A green, with a well. Lanes going up from the green to farms on the slopes, and the farms dark. The river beyond, wide and black and loud, going past, and beyond the river a low far bank with a few lights very far down it, which was Hearthmere, which the Clan didn't go near. And in the middle of the river, upstream, a long dark island with trees on it.

She didn't look at the island. You didn't. It was the oldest rule.

She looked at the farms instead.

Up the slope behind the village, on the shoulder of the hill under the Way, there was a farm with a light in one window. A house and a long byre and a yard and fields going up behind, pale with stubble, with the stooks standing in rows. Somebody was moving in the yard. A man, going in and out of the byre. Leading something out. Sheep. A pig, which wouldn't go, and he bent and did something to it and it went.

And down the back of the farm, by a beck that ran along the top of a pasture, two shapes were going away down a path in the dark. One tall and slow. One small. Holding hands.

Lisska's head came round.

*Is that her?* she said. *Imra. Down there. By the water. Is that the small one? With the old one? Is it? It is. Isn't it? Is it?*

*Leave it.*

*But is it?*

*I can't see that far, love. Nobody can see that far but you.* Imra's hand tightened. *Leave it. Count.*

So she left it. She went round, and counted. Three carts. Eleven men on the carts and round them. Six horses. A man with a lantern on the mill steps, who was big and wore an apron and kept waving his arms. She told Imra. Imra told Dag, up on Kharrak, with her own voice, shouting into the wind, because Imra could only speak inside to Lisska and Dag could only hear inside from Kharrak, and the two of them couldn't hear each other's dragons at all. Lisska had always thought that was the stupidest thing about people. Why have a voice inside, if only one other thing can hear it?

Kharrak could hear her. Kharrak could always hear her if he could see her.

*Eleven,* she told him. *On the carts. And the apron.*

*Eleven,* said Kharrak. *Easy.*

---

Then Arn's riders came down the last of the lane at the gallop, and somebody put fire in the mill.

She saw who. A man off a horse at the back of the mill with a torch and a jar, and the jar going in through a window, and the torch after. The whole roof went up at once, in one sheet, so fast and so bright that the light came up the valley like a blow and she flinched off it sideways in the air with Imra swearing on her back. Below her every shape in the valley jumped out black: the carts, the men, the horses rearing in the shafts. Arn's riders came into the mill yard with their swords out and the torches streaming.

And Kharrak tipped over, and dropped.

She went round him. She went round and round him, tight, fast, as he went down, because that was what she did when Kharrak went down; she went round him so that whatever was on the ground looked at her and not at him. He came over the mill yard low and slow and opened his mouth.

The fire went across the carts.

The flour went up white.

It went up in a great rolling ball, white and then yellow and then red, with a noise like the cliff coming down, and the heat came up off it so hard that it threw her. She went up and over in the air, tumbling, with the wings no use, and Imra flat along her neck holding on with her knees and her hands and her voice, and came out of it high above the valley, and found which way was up, and beat. Her eyes were full of white. Her nose was full of burnt bread.

*Kharrak!*

*Here.*

He was down there in the smoke, turning low over the mill yard with Dag on his back, and he wasn't white at all. He was red, and black where the soot had caught him, and the flour-fire was on his scales and running off them like water.

*You're not white,* she said. *Why aren't you white? You were supposed to be white.*

*Shut up,* said Kharrak.

Imra was laughing again on her back, or something like laughing, high and breathless and wild, with no warm in it.

Then the men came out from behind the mill.

Lisska saw them first, because she was the one looking. Not eleven. Out of the dark behind the mill, and from the lane by the long low house, and up the river path from the water, men on foot, in plain grey, with axes, a great many of them, running, roaring. They came into the mill yard and the lane and the green from three sides at once and hit Arn's riders in the light of the burning flour, and the noise came up to her: iron on iron, horses screaming, men.

*Imra,* she said. *Imra, there's more. There's more than eleven. There's lots. Why are there lots? Did they know? Did they know we were coming?*

Imra didn't answer. Imra was counting, out loud, fast, under her breath, and Lisska felt her lose count, and start again, and lose it.

---

Lisska felt Aurveth before anyone.

It came in the air before it came in the ear. A slow beat, from the east, from over the hills. Deep, so deep it was under hearing, like the ground being struck far off. She'd have known it in her sleep. She'd lain against it in the cave since before she could fly, the long slow breath of the oldest thing on the ledges going in and out in the dark.

She turned her head toward it.

Aurveth came over the shoulder of the hill, low, huge, with the firelight from the mill coming up along her bronze and her great wings going up and down slow, each beat throwing the smoke from the valley sideways. There was a small grey figure on her back, sitting very straight.

*Down,* said Imra. *Down. Low. Now.*

Lisska went down. Down out of the high air, away from the mill, across the slope toward the dark fields under the farm, low, so low the stooks went past under her chin. Imra had her by the horns. Imra had told her. Whatever happens. We go wide.

But she could still see. She could always see. And she could still hear, inside, because they were in sight.

*Kharrak,* said Aurveth.

It was the voice from the back of the cave. Lisska had never heard it sound like that, so tired. Long, and slow, and deep, the bottom of the river.

*Kharrak. There is nothing in this valley worth the two of us. Take the young one home.*

*No,* said Kharrak.

He came up off the mill yard in a long climbing turn, beating hard, with Dag on his back, and went to meet her.

*Imra,* said Lisska. *Imra. Which one do we help?*

Imra had both hands on the horns. Lisska could feel her through them, through her knees, through the bond. Something was going through Imra so hard and so fast that Lisska couldn't tell what it was. It wasn't the slate any more. It was a great deal of everything.

*Which one?* said Lisska. *Kharrak's ours. Aurveth's ours. Which one?*

Imra didn't say.

---

They met in the air over the green.

Lisska had seen dragons fight. Young ones on the ledges, rolling over each other in the updraught off the falls for the fun of it, all teeth and no bite. Old ones seeing off a crag-eagle from the lambing fields, which she'd liked, because she hated crag-eagles. She had never seen this. This was too big to see. It was two pieces of the sky coming together, the bronze one and the red one, with a sound she felt in the roots of her wings, and locking, and going round and round, falling, climbing, tearing, with fire going out of them into the dark.

Kharrak was faster. She knew that. She'd always known it. Aurveth was bigger, and older, and had done this before, more times than anybody knew.

Kharrak went for her neck. Aurveth turned and took him on her shoulder and her jaws closed on the root of his wing.

He screamed. Out loud, and inside, both. Lisska felt it in her own shoulder.

*Kharrak,* she said. *Kharrak. Kharrak.* Nobody answered her.

On Kharrak's back, Dag Rorke stood up with his axe and swung at Aurveth's eye.

Lisska saw it go in. She saw Aurveth's head go back, and something dark come out of the side of it, and the old voice inside her made a sound she hadn't known Aurveth had. Aurveth let go of the wing. Her head came round, fast. Too fast. Lisska had lain beside her in the dark her whole life and never once seen her move fast.

The jaws opened, and closed. Not on Kharrak.

On Dag.

He went up off Kharrak's back and hung in the air between the two great shapes, small, for one beat of Lisska's wings. Then he was going down. Turning over, down, small, onto the burning roofs at the bottom of the valley.

And Kharrak screamed.

She'd heard him scream at the bite. This was nothing like that. It came out of him into the air and into her at once, through the kin-sense, through the eyes, through everything, high and torn and going on and on and on, as if the whole of him had been split down the middle like a log on a block, and both halves were screaming. Lisska knew what it was. Everybody knew what it was. Every dragon on the ledges had heard about it, in the dark, from the old ones. You hear it, they said, and then you never want to hear anything again.

The rider dies, and the beast goes after.

Kharrak fell.

He didn't fly. He didn't try to. He fell away from Aurveth with his wings half open and flailing and his head thrown back, screaming, and as he fell past her he lashed out, blind, at anything, and his hind claws went across Aurveth's back from her shoulders to the root of her tail.

Through the bronze.

Through the small grey figure sitting very straight.

Under Lisska, Imra went hard all over, like a ewe on the moor when she sees you coming down.

---

Kharrak came down on the farm.

Lisska saw a man at the door of the byre with something in his hand, a halter, and the red shape coming down out of the sky at him, and the man didn't move. Why didn't he move? Why doesn't anybody move? Then Kharrak hit the barley field above the farm and the whole hill jumped.

She felt it in the air. A blow, coming up from the ground. Earth and straw and burning stooks went up in a wave and came down, and Kharrak went on sliding down the field through the stubble and the stooks and the hedge, ploughing, and his tail came round at the end of the slide sideways through the hedge into the end of the byre, and the byre wall went in.

The man had gone. Where had he gone?

*Kharrak,* she said. *Kharrak.*

He'd stopped screaming. He was lying in the furrow he'd made, on his side, with his torn wing out across the stubble and burning straw all round him, and she could see him from where she was, low over the bottom of the field, very clearly. She could see a face at two leagues in daylight. This was close, and there was fire. She could see the red of him going out at the edges. Going grey, like the end of a stick pulled out of the brazier.

Above her, very slowly, Aurveth came down.

She came gliding, with her wings out stiff and still, not beating, across the hill from the valley, and landed on the top of the same field, badly, on her chest, and slid, and stopped. Sigrun was on her back, slumped over the pommel with her white head down.

Aurveth turned her neck and looked back at Sigrun. Then she lifted her head and called.

It was the sound from Teo's fire, the long low broken one they'd made going round in the rain. Only worse. Lisska had made it too, that night, as loud as she could. She couldn't make it now. She couldn't make anything. She went round over the bottom of the field, round and round, low, with Imra on her back gone silent, and listened to the oldest thing on the ledges call over her rider until there was no breath left in her for it.

*Aurveth,* she said. *Aurveth?*

Aurveth didn't hear her. Or didn't answer.

*Imra. What do we do? What do we do? Imra?*

---

Then she saw the man.

He was lying in the stubble a few lengths from Kharrak's head, on his back, outside the broken hedge. He'd been thrown there, out of the byre, with the wall. He was dark all down one side of his face with something wet. He'd turned his head and was looking at Kharrak.

And his mouth was moving.

She could see it. She was low enough. He was talking. Not shouting, like everybody in the valley was shouting, like the men in the lanes and the horses and the fire. Talking. Lying in the field with his face all blood, talking to a dragon who was going grey.

And Kharrak moved.

He dragged himself. On his belly, through the stubble and the burning straw, with his torn wing trailing, toward the man, slowly, until his great head was lying on the ground beside him. The man put out his hand.

*What's he doing?* said Lisska. *Imra. What's he doing? Who is he?*

"That's Fenn," said Imra.

It was the first thing she'd said aloud since the counting. Her voice was very strange.

*The ghost? The barley one? Why is he… What's he doing? What's he doing to Kharrak?*

She heard Kharrak, inside. Not the scream. Something else. Something huge and raw and astonished, that she didn't have a word for, that he didn't either. And then she saw it, quite clearly, because she was the one who saw things. The grey on Kharrak's scales had stopped. It was lying on him at the edges where it had got to and it wasn't going any further.

*It's stopped,* she said. *Imra. It's stopped. The grey's stopped. Why has it stopped? How? Has he… Imra, what's he done? How has he done it?*

*I don't know,* said Imra.

Aurveth had stopped calling. Aurveth was coming down the field. Lisska watched her come, slowly, the great bronze shape dragging itself on its chest with its back torn open in long black lines, and Sigrun sliding off it as it came, falling, lying still in the stubble behind. And the bronze going grey at the edges, in the firelight, the same.

Aurveth's head came down over the man. He put out his other hand.

Lisska went round, and round. She was making a sound. She could hear it coming out of her, high and thin and up and down, and she didn't know when she'd started it, and she couldn't stop. Imra had her hands on the horns, and through them and through the bond Lisska could feel what Imra was looking at, which was the man, and the two dragons, and the grey stopping on Aurveth too.

*Go down,* said Imra. *Go on. Go down to them.*

*Can we? Are we allowed? You said wide. You said whatever happens.*

*Go down, love.*

So she went in. Low and fast over the hedge at the bottom of the field, toward the two great shapes lying in the stubble and the man between them. To see. To help, if you could help. To ask. Was it a thing you could ask?

Something in the hedge went *click*.

She heard it. She heard it under everything, under the fire and the fighting and her own crying, because she heard everything. A small sound. Wood, and something tight. *Click. Click.* Like a beetle in a dry stalk. Down in the dark of the hedge at the bottom of the field, under her, behind her.

Imra's head turned toward it.

*What's that?* said Lisska.

*Thock.*

---

On her back, Imra put her hand up to her throat.

That was all, at first. Lisska felt the hand go up. She felt Imra's whole body go small and surprised. And then the cold came down the bond.

It came all at once. Not pain. She'd felt Imra's pain before, a hand caught in a buckle, a fall on the ledge ice, a tooth. This wasn't pain. It was cold, and it was everywhere, and it came down into Lisska so fast that her wings forgot what they were.

She came down out of the air.

Too fast, wrong, with nothing working. The stubble came up and hit her in the chest and she was rolling, over and over, through the straw and the earth, with the sky and the fire and the ground all going round, and something on her back going with her, and then she stopped. She was lying on her side at the bottom of the field with the hedge behind her. There was earth in her mouth. Her bruises were small and many and sharp, and none of them mattered.

Imra was still on her.

She'd gone sideways out of the saddle, and the leg-strap had held her, so she was lying half across Lisska's back and half off it, in the stubble, with her face up to the stars and one hand still at her throat. There was something in her throat. A thin thing, sticking out. Lisska could see it. She couldn't stop seeing it.

*Imra,* she said.

*Cold,* said Imra. Inside, very small. *It's cold, Lisska.*

*I'm here. I'm warm. I'm here. Imra? I'm here.*

It didn't hurt. That was the strange thing. Lisska could feel that it didn't hurt. Imra could feel it too, and was surprised, and was almost glad, and then something came up in her, warm and quick, out of all that cold, like a fish coming up in black water.

*Teo,* said Imra.

Just that. And then, very quietly, from further away than Lisska had ever heard her:

*Wait for me.*

*Wait?* said Lisska. *Who? Me? I'm here. I'm not going anywhere. I'm right here, you're on me, I'm here. Imra, where are you going? Where? Can I come? Imra? Wait for me. Wait.*

And then nothing.

---

Dead feels like nothing.

She'd felt a deer die in her jaws and that wasn't it, that was something going out of something else. She'd felt Teo go and that wasn't it either; that was Imra's grief, coming through. This was nothing. This was a place in her where Imra had always been, every moment since she was small enough to sit in a pen, full of Imra, warm and quick and laughing and sharp, and now it was nothing. Not empty. Empty is a cave with nobody in it, and you can still go into an empty cave.

You couldn't go into this.

And it pulled.

The nothing pulled her. It was like the black water of Coldtarn, that the dragons won't drink from, that never freezes because it's too deep. She'd flown low over it once, as a yearling, for a dare, and felt the cold come up off it into her belly and pull, down, as if the water wanted her, as if the whole tarn was a mouth. She'd gone up off it so fast she'd hit the cliff. She was over it now. She was in it. She was going down into it, and there was no cliff, and the cold came in at the edges of her and went inward, slowly, toward the middle, and she could feel her own green going out.

So this was where they went.

Not up. Down. Down into the cold, after.

Was that where Teo had gone? Was Imra going there now, after Teo? Was that why she said wait?

Was Lisska meant to go after Imra?

She was crying. She could hear it, from outside, high and thin and going on and on. She couldn't stop it, and she didn't try. All round her the valley was shouting. Men in the lanes. Horses. The fire roaring in the mill and crackling in the straw. Somebody screaming, far off, on and on. All of it, shouting, and none of it was to her. Nobody was saying anything. Everybody was making a noise, and nobody was saying anything at all.

Then somebody did.

A voice, from up the slope. Low. Not much breath in it. Mouth-sounds, people-sounds, and she didn't know them, she'd never known people's sounds, she'd only ever had them through Imra and there was no Imra.

But they were to her.

She knew that. She didn't know how. Nobody else in the valley was making a sound *to* anything. This one was. It came down the slope through the smoke, over and over, steady, the same few sounds. Low, like Teo's voice under the roof when he'd finished shouting. Like Imra's on the ledge at night, not to anybody, to the dark.

And with it, up the slope, there was something warm.

She could feel it. She didn't know how she could feel it, with nothing in her where Imra had been. But up the slope where the voice was, where Kharrak and Aurveth lay with their heads by the man, there was a warmth in the dark. Like a cave mouth with a fire in it, seen from a storm. Like the slates in the sun.

She got up.

It was hard. Everything was a long way off. But she got her legs under her and her chest off the ground, and as she came up off the stubble the girth strap went, with a small dry sound, where the buckle had always bitten it. Imra slid. The saddle went with her. She went off Lisska's back, sideways, slowly, into the stubble by the hedge, and lay there on her back with her face up and her hand at her throat and her black hair spread out on the burned straw.

Lisska looked at her.

The voice was still going, up the slope. The cold was still going, at her edges, inward.

*Wait for me,* she said to Imra. *Will you? Wait?*

Then she went toward the voice.

---

She dragged herself. Her wings were no use, and her legs weren't much, and the slope went up and up through the stubble and the ash. She was still crying. She couldn't see where she was going for the smoke, so she went toward the sound, the low sounds, over and over, coming down to her. The warmth got closer. It got bigger as she got closer, not smaller.

Then she smelled him.

Under the smoke and the burnt bread and the blood, and Kharrak, hot iron, and Aurveth, old bronze, there was a smell she knew. Barley. Barley, and a cow.

*The ghost,* she thought. *It's the ghost.*

She came up against him. Against his legs. She pushed her face in under his arm, against his side, hard, frantic, wanting the warm, and he cried out. Something in his side was broken. She'd pushed on it. She didn't know. She pulled her head back a little and her muzzle found his hand, his wrist, and stopped there, trembling, all of her trembling, from her nose to the end of her tail.

He turned his hand over.

He held her.

---

It wasn't like the pen.

In the pen it had taken a whole summer. The girl had climbed in when they'd told her not to, and sat in the straw with her knees up, every day, every day, and Lisska too small to fly had come a bit nearer every day and gone away again, and come nearer. Imra used to say it was a long knocking. And then one day there'd been a door, a small one, on both sides, and Lisska had gone through it, and Imra had been there.

This was a door standing open in a storm.

She fell through it. She didn't knock. She didn't choose, or she did; she couldn't tell, after. She was going down into the black water and there was a warm open place, and she went in. And it was full. Kharrak was in it, enormous, hot, raw, red. Aurveth was in it, slow and deep and bronze and old as rock, filling it to the roof. They were already there, pressed in, and they were dying and not dying, and they were astonished. They were both of them saying the same thing, that wasn't words: *what is this, what is this, what have you done.*

And there was still room.

How was there room?

There couldn't be. The two of them were the biggest things she knew. She'd slept beside them her whole life and been the small one, always, on top of Kharrak, under Aurveth's chin. There couldn't be room for her too. But there was. She came in, and there was a place, small and narrow, tucked in under the other two, that was hers. It was the size of her.

How? Why? Who makes a place like that, in a man? Why was it here?

Nobody answered. The hand closed.

It closed in the middle of her, where Imra had been. Like a root going down. She felt it go down and hold. She felt the cold stop at her edges, all at once, where it had got to, and not come any further.

And she felt him.

She felt his rib. She felt the stake of it, going in when he breathed, and she felt her own side flinch. She felt the blood running into his eyes. She felt him crying, and that he hadn't known he was. She felt Kharrak's fury going round in him, and Aurveth's sorrow, and Dag, falling, and Sigrun, and under all of it the thing she'd brought in with her, that she hadn't known she was carrying: cold, and quick, and a name.

Imra's cold. Imra's *Teo*. Going into him.

He'd felt it. She felt him feel it. She felt him lying there in the stubble being Imra, for a moment, on her back, with something in his throat, and cold, and almost glad.

She cried out.

They all cried out, the three of them, at once, one long sound together, Kharrak's and Aurveth's and hers. It went out of them into the field and the valley and the dark. And it stopped, all at once, as if somebody had shut a door on it.

---

It was very quiet, after.

Not outside. Outside the valley was still roaring. Inside. Inside him it was quiet, and dark, and very close, three of them jammed into the dark with him, so close that she couldn't tell where her own fear stopped and Kharrak's started. And his. His was in there too. It was the biggest.

He thought he was going to die.

She didn't hear him think it. She felt it. It came up through the dark quite calmly, like mist coming up off the beck at dusk, that this was too much for one man, that there was no room in him, that nobody could live with this. And she was in him, in the room that he thought wasn't there, and she felt him think there wasn't room for her, and she couldn't tell him.

Was he? Was he going to? And then were they? All three, after all, just later?

Then something went through him. Small, and warm, and fierce. She didn't know what. She couldn't hear it. It had a shape, and the shape was small, and he held on to it with everything he had.

And then the roar came.

---

It came from the river.

It came up out of the ground first, under her belly, a shaking, so deep it wasn't sound. Then it rose. It came up into the air and went on rising, out of the dark to the west, from the island, from the place you didn't look at, and it was the biggest sound she had ever heard. Bigger than Kharrak's scream. Bigger than the flour going up. It went over the mill and the green and the lanes and the burning field, and over her, and up the hill behind, and away into the Scarfell, and came back off the far hills, and went away again.

Everything stopped.

The fighting in the lanes stopped. The shouting. The horses. Down in the valley, all along the bank, every living thing stood still.

Her head came up off the stubble.

She didn't lift it. It lifted. All three of their heads came up at once, Kharrak's and Aurveth's and hers, together, on their long necks, toward the river. It went through all of them and through him and through the three new roots in the middle of him, like a stone dropped into still water, the rings going out. It wasn't fear. She knew fear. It was something else. It was like being small in the pens and hearing a voice you know in the yard outside, before you can see who.

*What,* she said. Or someone said. It was very hard, in there, to tell whose voice was whose. *What was that?*

Nobody knew. She could feel that nobody knew.

*Old,* somebody said. *Something old.*

Was it Aurveth? Kharrak? Was it him? She didn't know.

Then the man went down.

She felt him go. Not dying. Down. The dark came up over him slowly, and drew him in toward the middle, and he went, and she lay with her face against his side and felt his heart going, slow, under her nose, and didn't let go of it.

---

Afterwards, the night went on a long time.

The fighting didn't start again. She heard the men in the lanes going, both lots of them, away from the river as fast as they could, up the Way and down it, and their horses, and nobody shouting now, and then nobody at all. The mill went on burning. The stooks went on burning on the top of the field, and burned down, one by one, and smoked. The stars went round over the valley, very sharp, and the frost came, as it had been coming all night, and lay on the stubble that wasn't burned.

She lay round his feet.

She'd moved there, after. She didn't remember deciding. Her face had been under his arm and his side was the broken side and every time he breathed it hurt her too, so she'd taken her face out, carefully, and gone round, and curled herself round his feet and lain her head on his shins, where she could feel his heart through his legs and not hurt him. It was warm there. Was it? She thought it was. Kharrak lay with his head by the man's knee. Aurveth lay above them all, on the slope, a hill. Nobody spoke.

He breathed. In, and the stake. Out.

Was he dead? No. He was breathing. Would he die before morning? She didn't know. Did he know they were here? Could he feel them, wherever he'd gone down to? Would he come back up? Would he be cross?

Would he want them?

She looked down the field.

She could see Imra from where she lay. Everyone in the valley could have seen her, if they'd come; it wasn't far. A shape at the bottom of the field, by the broken hedge, on her back on the black straw, with the saddle beside her. She hadn't moved. Lisska had known she wouldn't. She looked anyway. She looked at her all night.

She went over it. All of it. She couldn't help it. She went over everything, every time, it was what she did.

The apple, sweet and cold. *You've been a lump on a roof.* The cracked strap. *Tomorrow.* Dag on the ledge: *she going to sit on a roof and ask questions?* The heather and the frost and the sacks going thump. *Will he come out white?* And the laugh, coming up under her like warm air off a crag, and the hand flat on her neck.

*Which one do we help?*

The hedge. *Click.*

What was it? That sound. Like a beetle in a dry stalk. She'd heard it, and asked, and Imra had turned her head. Had Imra known what it was? Had Imra seen something, down in the dark of the hedge? Lisska went over it and over it and she couldn't see anything there, only hedge, and dark, and the click. What was it? What makes a sound like that?

*Teo. Wait for me.*

Had Teo waited? Was that where he'd gone, down the black water? Had he been waiting there all these four days, while she lay on the roof waiting for him? Was Imra with him now? Was it cold there? Was it always cold?

Why hadn't Imra said *wait for me* to her?

*Lie still, child,* said Aurveth. Very low, from the slope, out of the dark. The first thing she'd said since the roar. *Lie still. You'll wake him.*

*I'm not moving.*

*You are asking. It is very loud.*

*I'm not asking you.*

*No,* said Aurveth. *I know.*

Kharrak said nothing all night. But once, toward morning, she felt it come up in him, red and hot, swelling, and heard him inside, in fragments, not to her, not to anyone. *Who. Who did it. Who.* And then it went down again, because he couldn't move either.

And the man's voice. She had that too. She hadn't lost it. She had the sounds of it, the ones he'd said into the smoke, over and over, the only ones in the valley that were to her. She'd kept them without knowing what they were.

She knew now.

They'd come open. Somewhere in the dark, after the root went down, they'd opened up inside her, as Imra's words always had, as if they'd been his all along and she'd only needed to be his to hear them.

*Here. Here. Come here. Come on. I'm here. Come on, little one.*

She lay with her head on his shins and said them over to herself, in the dark, until the sky went grey.

---

At first light people came to the hedge.

She heard them before she saw them, coming up the lane from the village, a lot of them, slow, with their feet dragging. They stopped at the hedge. Some of them came to the gaps where Kharrak had gone through, and stopped there. She lifted her head and looked at them. They were smudged with smoke and they had forks and axes in their hands, and they were frightened. She could smell it from where she lay.

None of them came into the field.

An old woman came to the hedge, with a shawl. A small one with her. The old woman stood at the gap and looked up the field at the man lying in the stubble with the three of them round him, and put her hand to her mouth.

Lisska lifted her head higher. Was it? In the grey, among all the legs and the smoke, she couldn't be sure, and the old woman had the small one by the hand, tight, and then the people at the hedge closed round them, and nobody came.

Why didn't they come? Was it her? Was it Kharrak? Did they think…

The man moved.

---

*Are you dead?*

He was lying on his back with his eyes open, looking up at the sky. There was one star left in it, low over the hill.

*You're not dead. Are you? You don't feel dead. I've felt dead. Dead feels like nothing. You feel like a lot of things. You feel like a rib. Did you know you've got a broken rib?* She couldn't stop. It came out of her in a rush, like water out of a broken dyke. *Kharrak. Kharrak, is he dead?*

*Not dead,* said Kharrak.

*How do you know?*

*Breathing. Shut up.*

She didn't. She couldn't. She asked him if he was awake, and if he could hear her, and whether it was strange for him, because it was strange for her, and he shut his eyes again, which was rude, and she told him so. And Aurveth told her to let him be. *He has been where we were, and further,* Aurveth said, *and he has come back up through all of it.* And Lisska said she was only asking, and Aurveth said she knew, Lisska was always only asking.

Then he spoke. Out loud. With his mouth.

"Who," he said. "Who's that."

And she understood it. She understood every sound of it, without anybody in between. It came straight into her, as Imra's had, and nobody had to tell her.

---

After that the morning went fast and slow at once.

He sat up, with the rib going in and out, and looked at them. She said hello. He said hello back, out loud, and was polite about it, which Kharrak never was. He said they were in his head. Aurveth said yes, and told him she was more than three hundred years old and had never heard of it, not in the Clan, not in any song, no one holding more than one. And Lisska asked if that was bad, and Aurveth said she didn't know, and Aurveth always knew.

He pulled his torn shirt open and there were marks on his chest, three of them, over his heart, overlapping at the edges. Bronze, broad and smooth. Rust, jagged. And under them a little narrow green one, bright, tucked in.

*That's me,* she said. *Isn't it? Is it? Why is mine small?*

*It's the size of you, child.*

*I'm not small.*

*Small,* said Kharrak.

*I'm the fastest.*

*Small.*

And he laughed, sitting there with his fingers on the marks, and it went through all three of them, through the marks, and it hurt his rib so much he had to bend over. When he came up again his face was wet.

She felt the wet. *He's crying,* she said, and it came out very small. *Why is he crying? Did I do that? I didn't mean to.*

"No," he said. Out loud, to the field. "No. You didn't. It's all right, little one. It's all right."

*Little one.* He'd said that in the smoke. She had it.

*It isn't,* she said. *Is it?*

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. She could feel that it wasn't, and that he knew she could.

---

The small one came through the hedge.

Lisska saw her come. A small shape in a coat over a nightgown, wriggling through a gap at the bottom of the hedge where Kharrak had broken it, and standing up on the field side, and looking up the slope. The old one grabbed for her, and missed, and called, and the small one didn't stop.

She came up the field. Carefully, in her boots, through the ash and the black stubble and the smoking stooks, up the long raw furrow. Past the place by the hedge. Lisska watched her go past it and not look.

Lisska's head came up off his shins. All of her came up, bright, all at once.

*It's her,* she said. *It's her. Isn't it? The small one. From the burned farm, with the cups of water, who looked up at me and wasn't frightened. The one who said hello. You told me. At the hold. Wren says hello.* It was all coming out too fast. *I remember. That's Wren. Isn't it? That's your Wren.*

"Yes," he said. "That's Wren."

*At the hold, on the ledge, when you said it, Imra told you I said hello back. I did. I did say it. Nobody had ever sent me a hello before.*

Nobody had. Not in all the years on the ledges. Not anybody. She thought about it, while the small one came up the field. Teo had shouted at her and thrown apples. Imra had laughed at her and loved her. Nobody had ever just sent her a hello, across two days of road, by a man covered in barley dust, because they'd wanted to.

*Nobody has,* she said. *Except her.*

The small one stopped in front of Kharrak's head. It was longer than she was. She looked at it, and it looked at her, and Lisska felt the red thing in Kharrak, that had been going round all night, go still.

*Small,* said Kharrak.

The small one said things to the man. Lisska didn't know the sounds. She knew his answers, though, because those came straight in. "I know, chick." "It's mostly not mine." "Yes." "Yes." Then the small one walked round Kharrak's head, carefully, and sat down in the stubble beside him on the side that wasn't broken, and put her arms round him and her face in his torn shirt.

She was shaking. But she wasn't afraid. Lisska could smell the difference. Milk, and river mud, and smoke, and no fear in it at all.

*Oh,* said Lisska. *Oh. I like her.*

---

The old one came up the field after, and stood at the edge of the furrow, and said things.

Lisska didn't understand those either. They were mouth-sounds, people-sounds, noise. Why did his come through and nobody else's?

*What is she saying?* she asked him. *She smells frightened. What's she saying?*

*She thought you were eating me,* he told her. Inside. Not out loud. He'd found out how.

*Eating you!* Lisska put her head up. *We wouldn't eat you. Would we? Kharrak, would we?*

*No.*

*Why not?*

*Bonded.*

*Oh. Yes.* She looked at the man's arms, and his neck, and his wrists in the grey light. *Well. Also you're very thin.*

The old one came across the furrow in the end, past Kharrak's head without looking at it, and knelt and felt along his side with her hands. When the old one and a white-faced man who'd come up from the hedge tried to lift him and carry him to the house, all three of them put their heads up at once.

*No,* Lisska said. They all said it. *Don't. Don't take him. Stay. Stay.*

She hadn't known she'd say it until she had.

So they left him there. The old one bound up his rib where he lay, with strips of her own clothes, tight, and he swore at her through his teeth while she did it. Lisska asked him what each word meant. He told her some of them. He wouldn't tell her the others. She kept them anyway.

---

In the middle of the morning the old one went down to the house, and came back with a coat.

The byre's far end was a heap of stone where Kharrak's tail had gone through it, but the door end had stood, and the coat had been hanging on the back of the door. The old one put it round his shoulders. The wind came up the field and lifted the collar of it.

Something in the pocket was heavy.

Lisska felt it before she knew she'd felt it. Through him. A coldness against his hip. A weight. A thing he didn't want to look at, that he'd hung on a door so as not to look at it.

*What's that?* she said at once. *In there. That heavy thing. It's cold. I don't like it. What is it?*

He went very still.

*He's gone very still,* she said. *Why have you gone very still? What is it? Is it a secret?*

She waited. She found, lying there, that she couldn't hear what it was. Only that it was cold, and heavy, and that he'd gone still round it, as a hare goes still in the heather when the dogs are out. Whatever was in him about it, he was keeping. She could feel the shape of the keeping, and not what was kept.

So it was like that. He could keep things. Could she?

He let his breath out round the rib.

"It's a chain," he said. "It's nothing. I'll tell you later."

*Promise?*

"Promise."

*All right.*

She put her head back down on his shins. The coat was warm and his legs were warm and the small one was leaning against his side, and down at the bottom of the field, by the hedge, nothing had moved all night and nothing was moving now. She looked at it. Then she looked at him.

She had the apple, and the roof, and the strap. She had the laugh. She had *Teo* and *wait for me*. She had *click*, and *come here, little one*, and every word he'd sworn at the old one. She had all of it, every bit, from the boy on the pony to the coat on his shoulders. She wasn't going to put any of it down.

*I'll remember,* she said. *I remember everything. Imra says…*

She stopped.

She had never said it before. Imra always said it for her.
